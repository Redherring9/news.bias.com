‘Don’t shoot. Listen’: The AI-aided quest to understand Yellowstone’s wolves

A chance encounter on a Montana farm five years ago changed Jeff Reed's relationship with wolves forever. Standing with a rifle pointed at a gray wolf in his headlights, Reed experienced what he calls an "Apostle Paul moment" — suddenly questioning why he intended to shoot. He fired high over the animal's back, and that decision sparked an unlikely journey from wolf indifference to scientific obsession. Reed, a linguist and Silicon Valley software engineer, has since become a pioneer in wolf bioacoustics. His Cry Wolf Project, launched in 2023 within Yellowstone's renowned Wolf Project, deploys fifty automated recording units across the park's backcountry. These devices capture half a million hours of ambient sound annually. Using large-language models and artificial intelligence, Reed has isolated over 8,000 hours of continuous wolf vocalizations — the world's largest database of its kind. His analysis has identified more than twenty distinct call types, from barks and whimpers to the haunting chorus howl where entire packs vocalize in unison, possibly conveying specific meanings or emotions. This story matters because it represents a new frontier in understanding animal communication. Reed's technology is already being used to study wolf packs in Italy and holds promise for wildlife research and conservation globally. By combining his diverse expertise in linguistics, computer science, and his self-taught naturalist passion, Reed is working to decode what he calls "wolfish" — moving beyond centuries of mythology to actually listen to what wolves might be saying. It's a quiet reminder that sometimes the most groundbreaking science begins with a simple decision to lower the rifle and pay attention.

"Carnival of the Nearly Extinct Animals," premiering at Honolulu's Hawaii Theatre in September, reimagines the natural world through sound—from the thunderous murmuration of extinct passenger pigeons to the gentle chuff of tigers rendered on harp strings. Composer Stephanie Ann Boyd crafted the piece as a contemporary companion to Camille Saint-Saëns's 1880s "Carnival of the Animals," matching its structure with 14 movements celebrating 13 species at risk or already lost. The Hawaii Symphony Orchestra performs the score alongside Ballet Hawaii dancers and hand-built puppets by Obie Award winner Greg Corbino, while local students' poetry provides narration. Each animal receives its own musical portrait: floating violins evoke bees and butterflies, bassoons capture a swimming hawksbill turtle, and cellos tap their bodies to mimic ivory-billed woodpecker percussion. Some movements, like those for gray wolves and American buffalo, transform near-extinction into celebration of species that clawed their way back. The production reserves special attention for Hawaii's kāhuli snails, whose ornate shells once covered island trees so densely they created an eerie forest song. With an estimated 90% of Hawaii's 750 land snail species now gone, cellos carry the kāhuli's melody over harp harmonics. This work offers something quietly powerful: a chance to hear the world we're losing translated into beauty, making the invisible crisis of extinction visceral and unforgettable through art.

Kahurangi National Park predator control programme hailed as success

In New Zealand's Kahurangi National Park, a conservation effort has yielded remarkably encouraging results. Volunteers from Friends of Flora, working across more than 1,300 trap boxes, found just a single rat during recent checks — a dramatic shift from the 300 rats caught in the same period last year. The difference appears to stem from a carefully executed aerial drop of 1080 poison bait conducted in late June, which volunteer Robin Toy described as "wildly successful." The operation works through a cascade effect: helicopters distribute the bait in precise patterns that reach into the territories of individual rats. When the rats consume the poison, they die, and when stoats and weasels eat the poisoned rats, the predators are eliminated as well. This year's checks found zero stoats or weasels, creatures that would typically appear in small numbers even during successful operations. The timing and existing rat population both contributed to the drop's effectiveness, and while possums weren't the primary target, any that consumed bait were also controlled. The effort matters because Kahurangi National Park shelters extraordinary biodiversity, including native birds like kea, kākā, and roroa (the great spotted kiwi), as well as giant wētā and rare forest butterflies. Marine heatwave likely caused sharp decline in Hawaiian humpback whales, study finds

A marine heatwave that swept through the northeastern Pacific Ocean between 2013 and 2016 had devastating consequences for Hawaiian humpback whales, according to recent research that reveals just how vulnerable these ocean giants are to warming waters. The population declined by 37% during and after the heatwave, with adult survival rates plummeting from roughly 99% to just 68%—an unusually sharp drop for long-lived mammals. The culprit was a collapse in the food chain: the heatwave decimated populations of krill and forage fish that humpbacks depend on during their summer feeding months in Alaskan and Canadian waters. Researchers pieced together this story by analyzing more than 27,000 photographs uploaded to Happywhale, a citizen science platform where anyone can contribute images of whale flukes, which serve as unique identifiers like fingerprints. The data painted a sobering picture, with an estimated 5,000 fewer whales by the end of the heatwave period. During those years, scientists documented unusual mortality events in northern waters, including starving and dying humpbacks. The population has not yet recovered, and another marine heatwave is currently underway in the North Pacific, compounded by El Niño conditions warming much of the Pacific Ocean. This story matters because it offers a window into how climate disruptions ripple through marine ecosystems in ways that can persist for years. The Hawaiian humpbacks, already struggling to rebuild their numbers, now face additional human-caused threats including ship strikes and entanglements while navigating an increasingly unstable ocean. What happens in the Pacific may foreshadow challenges for humpback populations worldwide, reminding us that even species found in every ocean remain tethered to the delicate balance of their food sources and the stability of water temperatures.

What began as a lighthearted idea hatched over drinks with friends has evolved into something much more meaningful for the community. The giant peg has attracted its share of devoted admirers, from photographers arriving in the pre-dawn hours to capture it against the night sky to a couple who decided on their wedding day to exchange vows in front of it. Now their young daughter points it out as the place her parents married, and extended family members do the same. The sculpture even appears on Google Maps, where reviewers have compared it to the Sistine Chapel in cultural significance—tongue firmly in cheek, though the affection seems genuine. Local residents consider it an emblem of their district, a quirky drawcard that puts Canowindra on the map. Now the Staceys are downsizing and selling the property, peg included. They've fielded commentary about the awkward roadside location and occasionally overgrown lawn, but Peter Stacey simply laughs it off. His goal was always modest: if people drive past, smile, and have a bit of a laugh, the sculpture has done its job. This story is a gentle reminder of how the most unexpected creative gestures—born from whimsy and built in a shed—can stitch themselves into the fabric of a community's identity.

The town where every week is Te Wiki o Te Reo Māori

In New Zealand's Far North, the town of Kaikohe offers something unusual: a place where te reo Māori, the Māori language, isn't confined to a single week of celebration but woven into daily life. A new five-part bilingual podcast from Radio New Zealand follows three journalists as they bicycle the Te Pou Herenga Tai trail, an 87-kilometer route connecting coast to coast, and discover a community where roughly a quarter of residents speak te reo Māori fluently. Along their journey, the team encounters bilingual signage, traditional carvings, and spontaneous waiata (songs). They meet Mayor Moko Tepania, a second-language learner who moves seamlessly between Māori and English, describing his role through the metaphor of the kahikatea tree—a native pine that can only thrive when its roots intertwine with others. The podcasters also witness traditional practices still vibrantly alive: they soak in Ngāwhā Springs while learning its 400-year-old origin story, sit with families still healing from land confiscations, and watch weavers extract fiber from flax under expert guidance. Weaving instructor Makereta Jahnke explains how craftspeople listen not just to human language but to the "language" of the flax itself, reading the environment through the plants. This story matters because it quietly documents what linguistic and cultural revitalization looks like on the ground—not as policy or aspiration, but as lived reality. In Kaikohe, te reo Māori isn't a museum piece or a political statement; it's the ordinary soundtrack of community life, so normalized that locals only notice its richness when visitors "geek out" over what feels unremarkable to them. It's a portrait of resilience, continuity, and what happens when language becomes inseparable from place.

The exceptional cases of people immune to HIV (and what role they play in a possible cure)

Los excepcionales casos de las personas inmunes al VIH (y qué papel tienen en una posible cura)

For over three decades, Loreen Willenberg defied what scientists understood about HIV. The California landscape designer tested positive in 1992, yet the virus remained dormant in her body without medication, allowing her to live a normal life. Willenberg belonged to an extraordinary group called "elite controllers"—roughly 0.5% of HIV-positive individuals whose immune systems somehow keep the virus suppressed without treatment. Her case became even more remarkable when, after a stage IV cancer diagnosis in 2022, aggressive immunosuppressive therapy failed to reactivate her HIV. When researchers examined her cells, they found no detectable trace of the virus. In 2025, Professor Xu Yu announced at the International AIDS Society conference that Willenberg had likely cleared HIV from her body entirely—a stunning achievement against one of the century's most devastating infectious diseases. Though Willenberg passed away in April 2026 from cancer, her legacy offers profound hope. Another elite controller, known as "Patient Esperanza" from Argentina, is also believed to be cured, suggesting that under rare circumstances, the human immune system can eradicate HIV on its own. This story matters because it transforms what once seemed impossible into a roadmap for the future. Scientists are now studying the biology of elite controllers, searching for clues that could lead to next-generation treatments for the 40.8 million people living with HIV worldwide. Willenberg's exceptional immune response may hold the key to developing a cure—proof that the body's own defenses, when understood deeply enough, might teach us how to win battles we've long thought unwinnable.

The bird that changed Ecuador: how a chance discovery of a rare species helped to save many more

In November 1997, renowned ornithologist Robert Ridgely heard a mysterious call while exploring Ecuador's eastern Andes. Following the sound through dense bamboo, he encountered a plump, thrush-sized bird that local farmers knew as the "jocotoco"—a name mimicking its distinctive barking call. The discovery stunned the scientific community: an entirely new species, later named the jocotoco antpitta, representing one of the late twentieth century's most significant ornithological finds. Few new bird species of such size and distinctiveness are discovered so close to human communities. Ridgely quickly recognized that protecting this rare bird meant safeguarding its threatened cloud forest habitat. Within a year, he and colleagues established the Jocotoco Foundation, raising international funds to purchase and restore critical land. What began as an effort to save a single species has blossomed into something far more expansive. Today, the foundation manages nineteen reserves across Ecuador, encompassing roughly 50,000 hectares of protected land plus an additional 82,000 hectares of community-managed territory. These reserves now shelter more than 1,350 bird species—eighty percent of Ecuador's avian diversity and one-eighth of all bird species worldwide. This story quietly illustrates how a chance encounter can ripple outward into transformative conservation action. The foundation's success stems from its collaborative approach, employing local rangers who often grew up on the land they now protect and supporting communities through wildlife tourism. One bird's discovery didn't just add a name to scientific records—it catalyzed a movement that now protects an astonishing wealth of biodiversity while empowering the people who live alongside it.

Ervas orgânicas produzidas na capital do ovo e da cultura pomerana no ES viram chás premiados na França

In the mountainous region of Espírito Santo, Brazil, a collaboration between organic farmers and tea artisans has turned locally grown herbs, flowers, and fruits into award-winning beverages recognized on the world stage. Two tea blends crafted from ingredients cultivated in Santa Maria de Jetibá — a town known for its egg production and Pomeranian cultural heritage — recently earned gold medals at the Teas of the World 2025 competition in Paris. At the heart of this success is Selene Tesch, who has spent 35 years growing food without pesticides on her rural property. Her farm produces about 20 tons of organic crops annually, including roughly 120 varieties of vegetables, fruits, and legumes, plus hundreds of species of herbs and flowers like hibiscus, lavender, and graviola. The secret lies in careful soil management, crop rotation, and respecting each plant's natural growing cycle. After harvest, ingredients are carefully selected and dehydrated at controlled temperatures — up to 60°C for leaves, 70-80°C for fruits — to preserve their aroma, flavor, and beneficial properties. Entrepreneur Bárbara Borges then transforms these raw materials into exclusive tea formulas at her Vitória-based company, serving customers across several Brazilian states and creating custom projects for clinics, hotels, and cafeterias. This story offers a quiet testament to what happens when traditional farming wisdom meets creative craftsmanship. It shows how dedication to organic practices and attention to detail can elevate regional ingredients into products that compete internationally, while keeping the process rooted in local culture and sustainable methods.

How Bryce Dessner juggles orchestral concertos with indie rock stardom

Bryce Dessner has built a rare dual career, thriving as both the guitarist for indie rock giants The National and a critically acclaimed classical composer. The journey from Brooklyn experimental music circles to collaborating with Philip Glass, Steve Reich, and Taylor Swift might seem unlikely, but Dessner sees deep parallels between his two worlds—both rely on sustained human collaboration, just with different creative processes. The centerpiece of the Australian performances is Trembling Earth, a cello concerto inspired by Edvard Munch's landscape paintings from the eve of World War I. Dessner, who grew up surrounded by Ohio forests, was moved by how Munch's canvases captured both natural beauty and emotional turmoil during another era of global tension. Written for Russian cellist Anastasia Kobekina and premiered during his residency with the Czech Philharmonic, the piece reflects on humanity's troubled relationship with the natural world while maintaining space for hope and renewal. Landscape runs through much of Dessner's compositional work, including Impermanence, which responded to Australia's Black Summer bushfires through music and dance. This story offers a window into how artists navigate multiple creative identities without abandoning either. When Charlie isn't at school, he's welding thousand dollar scrap sculptures

This year marks a bittersweet transition: while Charlie opens his shed studio for the first time, veteran glass artist Gerry Reilly will close his doors after operating an open studio since 1991 and participating in the regional event since its beginning 13 years ago. This story captures a quiet changing of the guard in Australia's arts community—a teenager just beginning to share his work with the world as a master craftsman steps back after five decades.
