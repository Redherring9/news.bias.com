Floating wind is building ‘A lot of momentum’ toward commercialization

Floating wind turbines—anchored in deep ocean waters rather than fixed to shallow seabeds—represent an emerging frontier in renewable energy. Though still in its early stages, this technology is gaining traction in countries like China, Japan, South Korea, and across Europe, particularly in places where shallow coastal waters or available land make conventional wind farms impractical. For these nations, floating wind could become essential to meeting clean energy commitments. Currently, the global floating wind industry consists mainly of pilot projects, producing around 300 megawatts of power—a fraction of the nearly 100 gigawatts generated by traditional fixed offshore turbines. Norway's Hywind Tampen, the largest floating wind farm to date, powers oil and gas platforms with 11 turbines. While experts acknowledge floating wind won't dominate the offshore sector, it offers a vital option for regions constrained by geography. The technology itself is sound, but scaling it commercially remains complex. High costs, underdeveloped supply chains, and the need for specialized port infrastructure pose significant hurdles. Economic headwinds like inflation and rising interest rates have further slowed momentum across the entire offshore wind industry. What makes this story quietly remarkable is the promise it holds for unlocking renewable energy in places previously thought unsuitable. As innovation progresses and policy support strengthens, floating wind could transform how we think about harnessing the ocean's power—offering a glimpse of a future where deep waters become vast, sustainable energy reserves.

Is it genuine love – or obsessive passion? Kierkegaard, the father of existentialism, had some thoughts

When a new relationship feels all-consuming, it can be hard to tell whether it's the beginning of something beautiful or a warning sign of something unstable. A counselor and philosopher writing for The Conversation turns to 19th-century Danish thinker Søren Kierkegaard to explore the difference between genuine love and obsessive attachment. Kierkegaard, often called the father of existentialism, distinguished between two types of love: "aesthetic" love, which feels like falling under a powerful spell, and "ethical" love, grounded in choice, trust, and respect. Aesthetic love, Kierkegaard argued, is driven by intense desire and emotion—a force that seems irresistible but lacks the stability needed for a healthy relationship. It centers on personal feelings rather than principles, and can resemble addiction: the lover may feel compelled to stay despite knowing the relationship is harmful. The author describes a counselee who was willing to upend his entire life for a few months of passionate intensity, only to spiral into despair when it ended. In such cases, the beloved becomes an object meant to fill an inner void, rather than a person with their own needs. This kind of love, Kierkegaard wrote, is rooted in a form of self-rejection or escape from oneself. By contrast, ethical love expands freedom rather than restricting it. It's built on virtue, deliberate choice, and a strong sense of self—qualities that allow both people to flourish. The piece offers a thoughtful lens for anyone trying to understand their own relationships, reminding us that love at its best isn't about losing ourselves in someone else, but about choosing connection from a place of inner wholeness.

My grandmother was a seal

In 1948 Ireland, an 80-year-old storyteller shared a tale that echoes through Celtic folklore: his ancestor had married a woman who was once a seal. The story follows a familiar pattern—a farmer discovers a seal shedding its skin to become human, steals the skin to prevent her return to the sea, and claims her as his bride. She bears his children, who carry webbing between their toes, while she gazes longingly at the ocean, never smiling. These shapeshifters, now commonly called selkies, appear throughout folklore from Ireland to Scotland to Sweden, described variously as sea maidens or mermaids who transform by shedding their skins. The tale was collected by the Irish Folklore Commission, which became a 3-million-page archive at University College Dublin. Following centuries of colonialism, famine, and the near-extinction of Irish language and culture, collectors raced to preserve these stories before they disappeared with their tellers. What makes the selkie tales particularly striking is their intimate, local quality—storytellers name specific beaches and families, treating these encounters not as myth but as genealogy. Multiple families claimed descent from human-seal unions, their stories blending the fantastic with the matter-of-fact detail of hard fishing lives. This story offers something quietly profound: a window into how communities preserved identity and memory through folklore, even when colonialism and catastrophe threatened to erase them. The selkie tales—neither quite human nor animal, caught between two worlds—may have resonated especially deeply with people whose own culture existed in a precarious in-between state, holding onto something ancient while navigating forces of assimilation and loss.

Mysterious Mercury is about to get a visitor - after spacecraft's eight-year epic voyage

After an eight-year odyssey covering nearly six billion miles, the spacecraft BepiColombo is preparing to enter orbit around Mercury, the Solar System's smallest and least-explored planet. The mission, which launched in 2018, has had to fight the Sun's gravitational pull the entire way, taking a winding route that looped around Earth once, Venus twice, and Mercury six times just to slow down enough to arrive. A propulsion issue added an extra year to the already epic journey. Now comes the most perilous moment: an 11-minute maneuver to slip into orbit, with no room for error. Mercury is a world of extremes and mysteries. Temperatures swing from 430°C to -180°C, and despite being the closest planet to the Sun, ice hides in its polar craters. Thousands of strange, shallow depressions called "hollows" dot the surface, their origin unknown. The planet also harbors an outsized metal core, leading scientists to wonder if Mercury was once much larger and lost its outer layers in ancient collisions, or simply formed in a metal-rich pocket of the early Solar System. Time itself behaves oddly there: a year lasts just 88 Earth days, while a single day stretches to 59. Only two spacecraft have visited Mercury before, both from NASA, leaving much still to discover. BepiColombo's mission is to unravel these puzzles and deepen our understanding of how planets form and evolve in extreme environments. It's a reminder that even in our own cosmic neighborhood, profound mysteries remain—and that patient, carefully orchestrated journeys can still unlock them.

Wreck of first Australian-built warship found off Far North Queensland coast

A team of archaeologists has discovered the wreck of HMCS Spitfire, the first purpose-built Australian warship, lying off the coast of Far North Queensland exactly 128 years after it sank. Built in Sydney's Darling Harbour in 1855 from Australian timber, the vessel was originally commissioned by the New South Wales colonial government amid fears that Russia might invade Australia during the Crimean War. Despite being designed as a warship, the single gun intended for the 19-metre vessel was never installed, and it spent most of its service life on humbler duties—patrolling coastlines, delivering supplies to lighthouses, and eventually working as a pilot vessel before being sold to private owners as a fishing boat. After more than four decades of service, the aging Spitfire met its end on August 20, 1898, when a fierce gale opened the seams between its planks during a voyage near Lockhart River. The crew, composed largely of Aboriginal, Torres Strait Islander, and South Pacific Islander people, abandoned ship but all survived, reaching a nearby island before being rescued by a Japanese steamship. Finding the wreck proved challenging—conflicting historical records made pinpointing its location difficult, and the rapid marine growth characteristic of the Great Barrier Reef area had obscured it for over a century. Archaeologists James Hunter and Celeste Jordan, working with the crew of HMAS Diamantina, finally located it this August by searching for the telltale straight lines and round shapes that don't occur naturally. This discovery offers a rare window into Australia's early maritime history and the diverse communities who worked its waters. With much of the crew's belongings still aboard, next year's planned expedition promises to reveal intimate details about life at sea in nineteenth-century Australia.

The long reach of El Niño impacts on Southeast Asia’s primates

A new review published in the journal Oryx reveals troubling knowledge gaps about how El Niño-driven droughts and fires affect Southeast Asia's primates, both immediately and for decades afterward. While researchers know that 94% of the region's primate species face extinction threats, they found only 15 published papers examining El Niño's impact—covering just seven of the region's 105 primate species, all in Indonesia. The effects documented so far are predominantly negative. Habitat loss forces primates to change their diets and behaviors, crowds populations into smaller forest areas, and can lead to localized disappearances. Long-term studies of siamang gibbons following the catastrophic 1997-98 El Niño fires showed that these apes avoided heavily burned areas for nearly two decades, even after forests regrew with altered compositions. More concerning still, researchers found dramatically higher infant mortality rates persisting for years after fires ended, potentially due to reduced habitat quality, stress, or toxin exposure—factors that make it harder for already small, isolated populations to bounce back. This story matters because it illuminates a hidden dimension of climate events. The dramatic images of fires may fade, but their echoes ripple through primate communities for generations in ways scientists are only beginning to understand. With El Niño events expected to intensify, and so little research covering such a small fraction of affected species, conservationists face the challenge of protecting primates from threats they can't yet fully measure. It's a quiet reminder that survival isn't just about escaping the flames—it's about what happens in all the years that follow.

Star 350 times larger than the Sun caught eating its neighbour

For the first time, astronomers have watched a stellar merger unfold from start to finish. Over seven years, an international team led by Columbia University tracked a star system called AT2025abao in the Andromeda galaxy, observing as a red giant—nicknamed Big Bao—gradually consumed its smaller companion, Little Bao. Big Bao, roughly 350 times larger than our Sun, swelled with age and engulfed the Sun-like star orbiting nearby. The dramatic finale came in October 2025, when the system suddenly brightened, signaling Little Bao's final moments. This process, called common envelope evolution, happens when an aging giant star expands and traps a smaller neighbor inside its outer plasma shell. The smaller star spirals inward, either merging with the core or ejecting the shell and forming a tight binary system. Such interactions are thought to produce some of the universe's most extreme objects, like merging neutron stars and white dwarfs. Yet witnessing the event in real time had been nearly impossible—it lasts only a few years against the backdrop of stellar lifespans measured in millions of years. The team got lucky, noticing unusual brightness in 2023 and tracing it back through archival data to 2018. This discovery offers more than cosmic spectacle. It provides direct evidence for a process long understood only in theory, allowing scientists to refine models of how binary star systems evolve. As more powerful telescopes come online, researchers expect to catch similar events more often, turning rare glimpses into a richer understanding of the universe's hidden dynamics. It's a reminder that patience, timing, and a little luck can reveal the universe's most dramatic transformations.
