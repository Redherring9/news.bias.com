Photos: Taiwan’s tea revival blends ancient terraces with new climate adaptations

In the misty hills just outside Taipei, a quiet agricultural revival is underway. Taiwan's Shenkeng region, once a powerhouse of tea production that accounted for a significant portion of the island's export earnings in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, saw its terraced slopes return to jungle after industrialization drew workers away in the 1970s. Now, researchers and local growers are working to restore both the tea industry and the cultural heritage woven into these historic stone terraces—but they're doing so under increasingly unpredictable conditions. Climate change is reshaping the very environment that once made Shenkeng ideal for tea cultivation. The morning mists that used to keep the slopes damp now dissipate earlier, stressing the shallow-rooted tea plants. Rainfall arrives in intense bursts rather than gentle drizzles, and typhoons have grown notably more severe, with category 4 and 5 storms becoming two to three times more common this century. A single typhoon can devastate an entire plantation, creating landslides on the loosely terraced hillsides. The challenge has prompted a creative response: blending traditional farming wisdom with modern technology like drip irrigation, soil sensors, and more resilient tea varieties. This story offers a window into how communities preserve cultural practices in a changing world. It's not simply about nostalgia or economic revival—it's about adapting cherished traditions to new realities while honoring the landscape and knowledge passed down through generations. The quiet determination of those working these slopes reminds us that resilience often looks like patient innovation, one terrace at a time.

Researcher who listens to forests wins 2026 Pritzker Environmental Genius Award

A scientist who listens to the quiet pulse of the world's forests has been recognized for her innovative approach to conservation. Zuzana Burivalova, a tropical forest ecologist at the University of Wisconsin–Madison, recently won the 2026 Pritzker Emerging Environmental Genius Award for her work using sound to monitor forest health across the globe. Her method is elegantly simple yet profoundly effective: placing remote audio recorders in forests from Papua New Guinea to Madagascar, capturing the calls, chirps, and rustles of mammals, birds, amphibians, and insects around the clock, year-round. These soundscapes do more than document biodiversity—they create acoustic baselines that reveal how ecosystems change over time, offering scientists and conservationists a new way to measure the vitality of wild places. As principal investigator of the Sound Forest Lab, Burivalova plans to use the $100,000 prize to expand her Soundscape Baselines Project, partnering with more local scientists and communities in intact forest regions. She also aims to build a portfolio demonstrating practical conservation applications of this acoustic data. The award also honored two finalists: Doris Njagi, who works with thousands of smallholder farmers in East Africa to restore degraded land through native tree planting, and Suzanne Pierre, a microbial ecologist studying how human activity affects fundamental ecological processes. This story matters because it illuminates a quieter form of scientific heroism—one that asks us to stop and listen rather than simply look. In an era when forests face mounting pressures, Burivalova's work reminds us that understanding what we're losing begins with truly hearing what remains.

Putting a ring on it: the plan to green London’s beloved edges

London is getting an ambitious green makeover with a plan to create a 200-kilometer ring of trees and connected habitats encircling its outer boroughs. Dubbed the "M25 for nature," the London Tree Ring project aims to safeguard greenbelt land, boost biodiversity in overlooked woodlands, and reintroduce species that have nearly vanished from the capital's landscape. Over the next 25 years, the initiative invites community groups, schools, and landowners to plant and care for trees, with benefits ranging from urban cooling to carbon sequestration and community orchards. The project, spearheaded by CPRE London, has been quietly building momentum since 2023 and officially launches in October following years of research and pilot programs. One showcase effort on London's southwest edge involved planting 1,500 disease-resistant elm trees, a species nearly eliminated by Dutch elm disease in the 1960s and 70s. The elms are critical for the white-letter hairstreak butterfly, whose populations have dropped by 80 percent. Meanwhile, in Sutton, volunteers and schoolchildren created a densely planted Japanese-style Miyawaki microforest, designed to thrive even in drought conditions. What makes this story quietly remarkable is its emphasis on community engagement and long-term thinking. Rather than a top-down environmental project, the London Tree Ring invites residents to follow trees from seed to maturity, fostering a sense of ownership and connection. With ten million people standing to benefit from a cooler, more biodiverse capital, the project offers a template that could be replicated in cities across the UK—a reminder that large-scale environmental restoration can begin at the local level.

Country diary: Fungi season is here, so I head – yes – to the beach | Nigel Brown

When fungi season arrives in North Wales, naturalist Nigel Brown doesn't head to the forest—he goes to the beach. At Bangor University's annual fungus foray, 225 participants catalogued over 200 species in just 90 minutes, including the vivid orange ping-pong bat fungus, a climate-change-driven newcomer first spotted in the UK only in 2012. But it's the treeless, windswept sand dunes of Anglesey that reveal fungi's quieter, essential work. On Aberffraw Dunes, fairy rings of parasols and champignon dot the landscape, while toxic fibrecaps and webcaps hide in damper hollows. The real star, though, is a humble fungus few notice: the dune brittlestem. This salt-resistant species lives exclusively with marram grass, the only plant capable of stabilizing sand into towering dunes. Without fungi to help extract minerals from barren sand and decompose failing roots, marram would starve. The brittlestem and tiny mycorrhizal partners form an underground support network, recycling nutrients and sustaining the very plant that holds the coastline together. This story is a gentle reminder that some of nature's most vital relationships unfold beneath our feet, in places we least expect. Brown's seaside foray offers a glimpse into a hidden world where collaboration, not competition, shapes the landscape.
