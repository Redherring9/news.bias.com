How a 'fecal explosion' transformed Earth 540 million years ago

Como uma 'explosão de fezes' transformou a Terra há 540 milhões de anos

A new scientific review suggests that a dramatic shift in how early animals ate and digested food may have helped transform Earth's oceans around 540 million years ago. Researchers have dubbed this phenomenon the "Cambrian fecal revolution," proposing that the increase in quantity and variety of animal waste during this period helped distribute nutrients throughout the sea and paved the way for more complex ecosystems to emerge. This timing coincides with the Cambrian explosion, when many major animal groups first appeared and marine life underwent rapid diversification. As animals developed more sophisticated digestive systems and consumed different types of food, they began producing larger and more varied waste. These fecal pellets acted like small packages of nutrients, sinking through the water column and delivering organic matter from surface waters to deeper regions of the ocean. The review draws on evidence from more than 35 fossil deposits worldwide, including coprolites—fossilized feces—ranging from microscopic particles to specimens several centimeters long. Some preserved shells and animal fragments, revealing increasingly complex feeding relationships. Researchers also found signs of predation and prey remains within the digestive systems of fossils. The scientists emphasize that feces aren't the sole explanation for the Cambrian explosion; factors like rising oxygen levels and environmental changes also played crucial roles. But this study adds a compelling dimension to our understanding of that ancient transformation. The efficient transport of carbon and nutrients by animal waste may have accelerated the diversification of ocean life, establishing patterns that persist in modern marine ecosystems. It's a reminder that even the most humble biological processes can have profound consequences for life on Earth.

What will happen when a SpaceX rocket collides with the Moon?

A piece of SpaceX rocket hardware is about to become an unexpected participant in lunar science. The upper stage of a Falcon 9 rocket, launched in January 2025 as part of a U.S.-Japanese lunar mission, has been drifting through space and is set to collide with the Moon on August 5th. The impact, near the Einstein Crater on the Moon's visible side, will occur at speeds of around 5,400 mph—creating a crater estimated at over 17 meters in diameter. The Moon, already scarred by countless asteroid impacts over millennia, is no stranger to collisions. This one, however, offers scientists a rare controlled opportunity. The rocket stage—roughly the size of a five-story building and weighing at least 4,000 kilograms—will strike with enough force to send an estimated million kilograms of debris rocketing skyward in a plume possibly reaching 50 kilometers high. Without an atmosphere to slow it down, the impact promises to be dramatic, though safely distant from Earth. Telescopes across the Americas, along with NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter and South Korea's Danuri probe, are positioned to observe the event, while a citizen-science project invites amateur astronomers to participate. This accidental collision transforms space debris into a teaching moment. By studying the flash, the dust plume's height and spread, and the freshly exposed subsurface material, scientists hope to refine their understanding of lunar impacts and what lies beneath the Moon's ancient surface. It's a reminder that even our unintended marks on the cosmos can yield valuable insights—turning an oversight into an opportunity for discovery.

In coastal Japan, one project is planting forests to stand against tsunamis

Along Japan's northeastern coast, an ambitious reforestation project is taking a nature-based approach to disaster resilience. The Chinju no Mori Project has planted more than 680,000 trees over fourteen years, creating forest barriers designed to work alongside traditional seawalls in protecting communities from tsunamis. The initiative draws inspiration from Japan's "guardian forests"—dense groves that historically surrounded Shinto shrines and demonstrated their protective power during the devastating 2011 earthquake and tsunami that claimed over 19,700 lives. The project employs the "Miyawaki method," developed by renowned Japanese botanist Akira Miyawaki, which aims to recreate natural forest conditions by planting more than twenty varieties of trees and shrubs in tight arrangements. This biodiversity creates competition that drives trees to grow taller while smaller plants thrive below, resulting in dense, fast-growing forests. In Minamisoma, a coastal city in Fukushima prefecture, over a thousand volunteers recently gathered for an annual tree-planting event, adding 12,000 trees to the protective barrier along sandy beach dunes. Despite the method's global popularity, it faces scientific scrutiny, with critics noting a lack of rigorous evidence for some claims. This story matters because it represents a thoughtful reimagining of coastal protection—one that looks to tradition and ecology rather than relying solely on concrete infrastructure. While most Japanese towns continue building seawalls after disasters, this project offers a living alternative rooted in the region's 130-year history of earthquakes and tsunamis, honoring both environmental wisdom and community resilience.

Curlew revival continues at nature reserve

On a quiet nature reserve in Kent, England, a careful experiment in conservation is beginning to bear fruit. Curlews — Europe's largest wading birds, now on the red list of conservation concern — are returning to nest at the very place where they hatched as part of a multi-year effort to restore the species to lowland Britain. Thirty-three curlews were released at Elmley Nature Reserve in 2026, and now, birds from earlier releases are pairing up and choosing the marshland as home. The project addresses a stark decline: curlew numbers have dropped by about half since 1995, largely due to changes in farming and increased predation. These ground-nesters once thrived in hay meadows across the countryside, but those habitats have dwindled. The South of England Curlew Recovery Project takes a resourceful approach — eggs are carefully removed from vulnerable nests in healthier populations, like those in the Yorkshire Dales, and transported south to hatch at Elmley. Because curlews instinctively return to their birthplace to breed, conservationists hope these Kent-hatched birds will establish a new stronghold. So far, more than one hundred curlews have been released over four years, and the returning birds are still young — "kind of teenagers," as estate manager Gareth Fulton puts it. They're expected to mature enough to raise chicks of their own by next year. It's a story worth following because it illustrates patience, collaboration across landscapes, and the quiet hope that careful stewardship can turn a troubling trend around — one nest at a time.

How the naked mole rat queen prevents other females from getting pregnant

Como a rainha das ratas-toupeiras impede que outras fêmeas engravidem

Scientists have discovered how naked mole rats maintain their unusual social hierarchy through chemistry rather than brute force. In colonies of these wrinkly African mammals, only one queen reproduces while all other females remain infertile—and researchers have finally figured out how she enforces this arrangement. For decades, biologists knew that naked mole rats are eusocial, living in highly organized colonies similar to bees or ants, with worker members caring for the queen's offspring. The prevailing theory suggested the queen physically bullied other females into submission. But when researcher Mohammed Khallaf, who previously studied insect smell, first observed the animals, he noticed they sniffed constantly. This observation led to a breakthrough: the queen produces high concentrations of a chemical compound called isopropyl myristate, which is nearly absent in non-breeding individuals. When the team exposed female mole rats to this scent, it triggered dramatic brain activity in regions controlling smell and hormone production, specifically raising prolactin levels (which suppress fertility) while keeping progesterone low. In experiments, females isolated from the queen's scent quickly became sexually active and pregnant, while those exposed to the chemical remained infertile. This discovery reveals that naked mole rat queens wield what researchers call a "super contraceptive"—a fragrance-based birth control system that temporarily suppresses fertility in their subjects. It's a quietly remarkable example of how nature engineers complex social structures through elegant chemical solutions, offering insights into mammalian biology that challenge our assumptions about dominance, reproduction, and the surprising power of scent.

Breastmilk research could protect against deadly respiratory infections in babies

Scientists at the QIMR Berghofer Medical Research Institute in Australia have uncovered how a protein naturally present in breastmilk helps shield infants from dangerous respiratory infections. The five-year international study, published in the journal Cell, focused on osteopontin, a protein found in particularly high concentrations in early breastmilk. While researchers have long known that breastfeeding protects babies and that osteopontin levels are elevated after birth, the mechanism behind this protection remained unclear until now. Led by immunologist Simon Phipps, the team compared baby mice given osteopontin from cow's milk with those who weren't. The mice receiving the protein developed stronger immune defenses against respiratory infections. The researchers discovered that osteopontin works by altering the gut microbiome, changing how bacteria produce certain chemicals that then signal cells in organs like the lungs to boost immune system development. In an intriguing twist, the study also found that a drug currently used for type 2 diabetes could trigger similar immune responses in mice. This research offers potential pathways for improving commercial infant formulas, and the laboratory is already in discussions with formula manufacturers. The study was partly funded by Nestle and Australia's National Health and Medical Research Council. Still, experts emphasize that the greatest benefits for mothers and babies will likely come from better access to breastfeeding support. The discovery illuminates one of nature's elegant systems for protecting vulnerable newborns, revealing how a single protein can orchestrate complex biological processes that prepare an infant's immune system for the world beyond the womb.

The musicians turning bat calls into club music

Two Scottish artists have created an unlikely fusion: electronic dance music built entirely from the echolocation calls of bats. Using bat detectors to capture sounds normally beyond human hearing, the duo discovered a sonic landscape rich with clicks, buzzes, and syncopated patterns—the lesser horseshoe bat's calls evoke dub reggae, while the Daubenton's bat channels jazz percussion. The project grew out of community bat walks held during Covid lockdowns in Arbroath, Scotland. After collecting recordings from 13 of the UK's 18 bat species, Tuulikki and Perman layered processed bat calls with human voice to create what they describe as a hybrid sound world. The work first took shape as outdoor "silent bat raves," where audiences danced through headphones at dusk while real bats flew overhead. Now released as a full album on Glasgow's KIN-TU label, the music aims to do more than entertain—it gently challenges the unease many people feel toward bats, creatures vital to ecosystems yet shadowed by superstition. This story is worth your time because it quietly reframes how we relate to misunderstood species. The story behind New Zealand's horse ambulance service

A casual conversation at a backyard barbecue in New Zealand has evolved into a world-first service that's changing how injured horses receive care. Ten years ago, veterinarian Dr. Peter Gillespie and a transport engineer began discussing the possibility of creating a purpose-built ambulance for horses—one that could hydraulically lower to ground level, allowing an injured animal to walk aboard rather than be lifted or dragged. That offhand chat has since grown into a fleet of ten specialized vehicles, making New Zealand the only country in the world with a dedicated horse ambulance at every race meet. The innovation addresses a gap Gillespie noticed during his decades as a race day vet. While treatment for equine injuries had advanced significantly since the 1980s, the actual transport of injured horses remained problematic. Working with colleague Bill Bishop, Gillespie developed clear criteria: horses needed to board at ground level and feel stable during transit. After initial funding challenges, the project gained backing from Thoroughbred Racing NZ and Harness Racing NZ, leading to the formation of a charitable trust. The first ambulance debuted at Addington Raceway in 2018, and demand was immediate—five more were ordered within two months. Today, these ambulances serve 700 events annually, covering not just race meetings but also agricultural shows and equestrian competitions. The trust raises over $200,000 yearly through community fundraisers and tournaments, having collected more than $2.25 million to date. This story is a quiet testament to how practical compassion and engineering ingenuity can address a need that went unmet for generations, improving welfare for animals that hold a special place in New Zealand's culture.
