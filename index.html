‘From this perspective, the bullfrog feels powerful and mythical’: Steven Scott Grogin’s best phone picture

Steven Scott Grogin begins each day with a 6am ritual: a hike to his favorite lake in Michigan, a former quarry pit that's become a haven of biodiversity and quiet contemplation. On one such morning, he encountered the largest American bullfrog he'd ever seen—a hefty six to eight inches long, floating in the water. What followed was a patient dance between photographer and subject, as Grogin worked to capture an image that would transport viewers into the frog's world rather than simply observing it from above. The challenge lay in the bullfrog's nature. Though predators themselves, these amphibians are notoriously skittish, making close approaches nearly impossible. Grogin waited for the frog to drift naturally toward shore, then lowered himself into the mud, moving with careful deliberation. He positioned his phone at the water's surface and dipped the lens just below the waterline, holding steady through what required "patience, balance and a lot of problem-solving." He remained there until late afternoon before heading home to make subtle edits that preserved the image's natural texture. The resulting photograph offers something rare: an eye-level encounter with a creature we typically see only from above. From this vantage point, the bullfrog transforms from a small pond dweller into something that feels, as Grogin puts it, "powerful, surreal and mythical." It's a reminder that perspective can alter our understanding of the everyday, revealing hidden grandeur in the familiar and inviting us to see the natural world not as spectators looking down, but as participants floating face to face with its quiet wonders.

The king of the Andes: How a village learned to coexist with condors

High in the Colombian Andes, a remarkable transformation is taking place between sheepherders and one of the continent's most majestic birds. Doris Torres, who once saw Andean condors as threats to her livestock, has become an unexpected champion for their survival. In her remote village of Páramo El Almorzadero, generations blamed condors for sheep deaths, leading to widespread poisoning and shooting that pushed the species toward extinction. Today, fewer than 250 Andean condors remain in Colombia, earning them critically endangered status. Torres's shift began with education. Working alongside Fernando Castro from the Parque Jaime Duque Foundation, she learned that condors lack the strong talons needed to attack livestock—they're scavengers, not predators. Their role is actually beneficial: by consuming decomposing meat, condors prevent disease spread across the ecosystem. Armed with this knowledge, Torres and her neighbors transformed their approach. The community installed camera traps, improved livestock pens, and fenced pastures to protect against actual threats like foxes. Former persecutors became protectors, creating a safe haven where condors now soar once again over the páramo. The project's next chapter involves releasing captive-bred juveniles into the wild. Castro's team in Bogotá raises chicks using condor-shaped puppets to prevent human habituation, aiming to release at least three young birds by 2027. This story matters because it shows how understanding can dissolve conflict. What began as fear rooted in myth became a grassroots conservation model—proof that even long-standing enmities can transform when communities are included as partners rather than obstacles in protecting the natural world.

How a Sudanese refugee became a head teacher in a Chadian camp

When war erupted in Sudan in April 2023, Sahra had already built a career as an education administrator in el-Geneina, the capital of West Darfur. Like nearly one million other Sudanese refugees, she fled across the border into Chad, where thousands of displaced families found little more than open ground and makeshift shelter. For many children among them, formal education had abruptly vanished. Today, Sahra leads a primary school in a refugee camp in eastern Chad, serving over 1,000 pupils with a staff of 30 teachers—all recruited from the refugee community itself. The school, established by Plan International and partner organizations in 2024, runs two shifts daily to accommodate demand, teaching mathematics, science, history, geography, Arabic, and French. Yet the work is challenging. Sudanese teachers must adapt to Chad's unfamiliar curriculum while navigating language barriers between Arabic-speaking Sudanese students and Chadian children who speak local dialects. Despite these obstacles, the school has become more than a place of learning. It offers protection from early marriage, violence, and the dangers that linger in camp life. Initial tensions between Sudanese and Chadian children have softened, and the students now play together outside class. For parents like Charfadine, who arrived during the rains of 2023, the school represents safety and a fragile thread of hope. This story is a quiet testament to resilience in displacement—how educators in exile are rebuilding not just classrooms, but community and dignity, even as they hold onto dreams of one day returning home.

This story quietly captures something meaningful about persistence and possibility. Yet here, through the dedication of educators and students, a small population thrives, keeping the species visible and demonstrating how restoration might one day be possible. Country Life: The Trust helping New Zealand's raptors take flight again

In a backyard in Timaru, New Zealand, a small group of volunteers began caring for injured birds of prey in 2016. What started as makeshift recovery efforts in garages and living rooms has grown into the NZ Raptor Trust, a dedicated facility that has now helped 551 raptors over its ten-year journey. The trust takes in kāhu (Australasian harrier hawks), kārearea (New Zealand falcons), and owls, nursing them from injury through to release back into the wild. Co-founder Ron Lindsay, whose lifelong passion for birds began with breeding finches as a child, now oversees operations from a purpose-built center complete with shipping containers, netted enclosures, and an education facility. The birds arrive for varied reasons: some are hit by vehicles while feeding on roadkill, others nest in dangerous farm machinery, and some suffer lead poisoning from scavenging culled pests. The statistics are sobering—only one in three birds can be released, with another third requiring immediate euthanasia due to severe injuries. Still, 175 birds have successfully returned to the wild. The trust's newest initiative is a breeding program for falcons too injured to be released but healthy enough to produce offspring that can fly free, addressing the uncertain population of kārearea, estimated between 5,000 and 8,000. This story offers a quiet reminder of how dedication and compassion can create meaningful change for vulnerable wildlife. It's about ordinary people building something extraordinary, one bird at a time, and ensuring New Zealand's raptors have advocates when they need them most.

Artefacts from across the globe returned to southern WA

Cultural artefacts from Western Australia's southern coast have made their way home after centuries in museums across the United Kingdom and Australia, now forming the centerpiece of an exhibition that reunites Indigenous knowledge with historical collections. The Kalyagul exhibition at the Museum of the Great Southern tells a quietly remarkable story of collaboration between Menang elders, historians, and scientists working to reconnect with ancestral wisdom. The journey began with watercolor drawings of fish created in the 1840s by commissariat officer Robert Neill, who worked closely with Menang people in Albany. What makes Neill's collection unusual is his careful acknowledgment of Indigenous knowledge—each fish specimen bears a label prominently displaying its Menang name and crediting the people who caught it. When researchers discovered fish scales still attached to drawings held at London's Natural History Museum, they uncovered twenty-seven boxes of preserved fish at Scotland's National Museum, each one a testament to Menang expertise. The project also located plant specimens collected by Menang women Lucy Webb and Lucy Eades in the late 19th century, representing the first definitive evidence of Aboriginal contributions to herbarium collections from that era. This story matters because it reverses a familiar pattern. Rather than knowledge being extracted and claimed by colonial visitors, these artefacts acknowledge their Indigenous sources by name, creating an archive that Menang descendants can now explore and expand. The project has also sparked cultural renewal, with community members recreating traditional items and adding new specimens to museum collections, ensuring that Menang knowledge continues forward—kalyagul, as the Menang word beautifully expresses: always, ever, continuing.

Draft dark skies guidance drawn up for the Highlands

Highland Council in Scotland has drafted new guidance designed to protect the region's famously dark skies from light pollution. The proposal would help communities establish dark sky sites—areas with minimal artificial light—and require builders near these locations to justify their outdoor lighting through formal assessments. The goal is to preserve one of Europe's best stargazing locations while delivering practical benefits beyond astronomy. The guidance encourages limiting exterior lighting to what's truly necessary for safety and security, with measures like strategic tree planting and fencing to reduce light glare. Officials point to multiple advantages: lower carbon emissions, reduced energy costs, less disruption to wildlife and human health, and sustained tourism appeal for visitors drawn to the Highlands' celestial views. The draft will undergo public consultation before potentially becoming part of the Highland Local Development Plan, which maps out future housing and development zones across the region. This initiative builds on recent recognition of the Highlands' exceptional darkness—in 2024, the Isle of Rum became Scotland's first International Dark Sky Sanctuary, an international designation celebrating places where night remains truly night. The story is worth attention because it represents a practical intersection of environmental stewardship, community choice, and astronomical wonder. In an increasingly illuminated world, the effort to preserve darkness is both a return to something ancient and a forward-looking act of conservation, recognizing that what we choose not to light matters as much as what we do.

Satellite captures Moon's shadow crossing Earth during total solar eclipse; see IMAGE

Satélite registra sombra da Lua cruzando a Terra durante eclipse solar total; veja IMAGEM

A weather satellite operated by the U.S. National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration captured a striking view of the Moon's shadow sweeping across Earth during the total solar eclipse on August 12. The image, recorded by the GOES-19 satellite from its geostationary orbit, shows the shadow as a dark patch moving over Greenland, Iceland, and Spain—regions where the Sun was completely obscured for a few moments. The GOES-19 satellite, launched in 2024 and operational as GOES-East since April 2025, maintains a constant watch over much of the Western Hemisphere. While its primary mission involves tracking storms, hurricanes, wildfires, and other environmental events, it also captured this rare celestial alignment. The darkest part of the shadow, known as the umbra, marked the path of totality where millions of observers experienced the sky darkening in the middle of the day. The satellite's perspective offers a reminder of the vast scales at play during an eclipse—what appears as a brief moment of darkness on the ground unfolds as a dramatic shadow racing across the planet's surface. This story offers a rare perspective on a familiar phenomenon, showing how modern satellite technology allows us to witness celestial events from vantage points that were unimaginable just decades ago. It's a quiet reminder of our place in a solar system constantly in motion, where alignments like this create moments of shared wonder across continents.

Infrastructure and land use behind devastating India monsoon floods

When devastating floods swept through northeastern India's Assam state between June and August 2026, affecting over half a million people and claiming 99 lives, researchers found a surprising culprit. A new study by the World Weather Attribution network reveals that climate change wasn't the primary driver—instead, human infrastructure choices and land-use patterns were largely to blame. Scientists analyzing weather records discovered that the rainfall itself wasn't extraordinary, occurring at levels expected every one to two years. What transformed typical monsoon rains into catastrophe was decades of environmental degradation and poor planning. Rapid urbanization, deforestation, and wetland destruction have disrupted natural drainage systems throughout the Brahmaputra River basin. The river carries massive sediment loads each monsoon, gradually raising its bed and reducing its capacity to contain water. Meanwhile, unplanned development has eliminated the natural buffers that once absorbed excess flow. The study's review of 35 research papers confirmed that these human-driven changes now matter more than rainfall variability in predicting flood frequency. Colonial history also plays a lingering role: tea plantation expansion forced Indigenous communities onto flood-prone riverbanks and sandbars, creating vulnerability patterns that persist today. This research offers a sobering reminder that our relationship with rivers matters as much as the rain that fills them. While Assam has invested heavily in concrete embankments, the study suggests this rigid infrastructure may actually worsen flooding by trapping sediment and increasing breach risks. The path forward, researchers suggest, lies not in controlling rivers but in working alongside them—through floodplain restoration, wetland conservation, and community-based adaptation that respects rather than resists natural water flows.

Whitefly: understand how pest seen in Roraima's interior can affect crops

Mosca-branca: entenda como praga vista no interior de Roraima pode afetar as plantações

Residents of Alto Alegre, a town in northern Roraima, Brazil, recently witnessed an unusual sight: swarms of tiny whiteflies filling the streets. While visually striking, the phenomenon points to a larger agricultural concern. The whitefly, known scientifically as Bemisia tabaci, is a minuscule insect—barely a millimeter long—with a yellowish body and white wings. Despite its common name, it's not actually related to houseflies but belongs to a different order of sap-sucking insects that pose significant risks to crops. Researchers from Brazil's agricultural research agency Embrapa explain that the current outbreak aligns with favorable climate conditions: high temperatures, low humidity, and reduced rainfall during the soybean harvest season. As soybean plants are removed from fields, adult whiteflies seek new hosts. The insect feeds on plant sap, weakening crops and stunting growth. More worryingly, it secretes a sugary substance that encourages sooty mold growth on leaves, interfering with photosynthesis. The whitefly also transmits plant viruses—a particularly serious threat since infected plants must be destroyed, as no cure exists. Affected crops include tomatoes, melons, peppers, and other vegetables. Fortunately, the insects pose no danger to humans or animals. This story matters because it illustrates how climate patterns and agricultural cycles intersect in unexpected ways, sometimes quite visibly. What appears as a curious natural spectacle on city streets is actually a window into the delicate balance farmers must maintain to protect their crops. It's a reminder that agriculture operates within complex ecological systems where tiny creatures can have outsized impacts.

'Squashed poo' a sign of water voles' success

In a quiet corner of Surrey, conservationists are celebrating an unusual marker of success: squashed droppings along the muddy banks of the Hogsmill River. One year after releasing 42 water voles into this chalk stream, researchers have found compelling evidence that the population is not just surviving, but thriving. Urban rewilding officer Pia Trevelyan-Ashby spends her days searching for the telltale signs—Tic Tac-shaped droppings, burrows in the riverbank, and the peculiar phenomenon of "squashed poo," where water voles trample their own waste to mark territory using scent glands on their feet. The return of water voles to the Hogsmill marks the end of a more than 20-year local extinction. These small rodents, immortalized as Ratty in The Wind in the Willows, have declined dramatically across the UK since the 1970s, with populations dropping by as much as 90 percent. The culprits include habitat destruction and predation by non-native mink, each of which can consume about one animal per day. The water vole reintroduction works alongside an intensive mink eradication program, creating conditions where the native species can reclaim their ecological niche. Camera traps and specially designed "latrine rafts" help researchers track the expanding population, which has now spread both upstream and downstream from the original release site. This story offers a gentle reminder that conservation success often comes in unexpected forms. Water voles play a vital role in healthy river ecosystems, managing vegetation and serving as prey for larger species. Their presence signals improving river health and more naturalized habitats. Sometimes the most meaningful environmental victories are measured not in grand gestures, but in small, trampled droppings along a riverbank—quiet evidence that nature, given a chance, can find its way back.

Research uncovers what determines flavour of Australian chocolate

Australian researchers have uncovered a surprising twist in what makes chocolate taste the way it does: the location where cocoa beans are fermented matters even more than the cocoa plant variety itself. In trials conducted across three Far North Queensland sites—Mossman, Fishery Falls, and Mission Beach—scientists found that fermentation location could account for nearly half of a chocolate bar's final flavor profile, with each region producing distinctly different tastes. The secret lies in the microorganisms naturally present in each environment. During fermentation, these tiny organisms produce metabolites that create flavor precursors, eventually transforming into notes ranging from fruity to chocolatey to buttery. Because Australian cocoa fermentation relies on naturally occurring microbes rather than controlled yeast strains, the local environment plays an outsized role. The Mossman beans developed fruity notes, Mission Beach cocoa emerged more traditionally chocolatey, and Fishery Falls fell somewhere between. University of Queensland food chemist Marlize Bekker explained that these environmental microorganisms and their metabolic processes are the key drivers behind the regional variation. This discovery could reshape how Australian chocolate is marketed and enjoyed. Industry leaders envision a future where chocolate carries regional designations similar to wine appellations, allowing consumers to choose based on terroir-driven flavor profiles. For Australia's emerging premium cocoa industry, this research offers both scientific validation and commercial opportunity—a way to justify premium pricing while celebrating the distinct character that comes from a specific place and its invisible microbial residents. It's a quiet reminder that flavor is as much about location and process as it is about the raw ingredient itself.
