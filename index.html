'If you see me, weep': The story behind Europe's 'hunger stones'

'Ao me ver, chore': A história por trás das 'pedras da fome' da Europa

When Europe's great rivers recede during droughts, they reveal more than sunken ships and wartime relics—they expose stones bearing centuries-old warnings carved into their surfaces. Among the most striking is a message inscribed in German on a boulder in the Elbe River near Děčín, Czech Republic: "If you see me, weep." These are hunger stones, or Hungersteine, markers that spend most years submerged but emerge when water levels drop to dangerous lows. Unlike flood markers that record high water, hunger stones commemorate the opposite: the droughts that brought hardship to communities dependent on rivers for transport, trade, and survival. For centuries, residents along rivers like the Elbe, Danube, and Rhine have carved dates and inscriptions onto these stones during severe droughts—some bearing years as far back as 1616. Historical research has shown these weren't merely symbolic memorials. A 2020 study found that many inscriptions on Elbe hunger stones correspond closely with documented periods of exceptionally low water levels, suggesting they served as practical records of river depth. When waters dropped, barges carrying grain and timber couldn't navigate shallow channels, disrupting vital trade routes. Crop failures followed, along with rising prices and famine. The famous "weep" inscription from 1904 represents just one chapter in a much longer chronicle of drought. These stones have captured renewed attention during recent European droughts in 2020 and 2022, with media describing them as ominous harbingers. Yet they're perhaps better understood as a bridge between past and present—a reminder that communities have long grappled with water scarcity, and found ways to document their struggles for future generations. In an era of climate uncertainty, these submerged chronicles offer both historical perspective and quiet testament to human resilience.

How singing in a choir hits the right note for people living with brain conditions

Nearly two decades ago, the University of Auckland's Centre for Brain Research launched the CeleBRation Choir with an unexpected mission: helping people with stroke, Parkinson's disease, dementia, and other brain conditions regain their voices and rebuild their lives through song. Research now shows that participants in these neurological choirs report quality of life levels comparable to those of community choir members without communication disabilities—a quietly remarkable finding. The therapeutic power lies in the overlap between singing and speech. Both activities engage the lungs, voice mechanisms, and similar brain pathways that conditions like Parkinson's or stroke can damage. For people with Parkinson's, singing strengthens breathing and articulation, helping them speak more clearly. Stroke survivors with aphasia—a language disorder that makes finding words difficult—often discover they can sing familiar lyrics more fluently than they can speak. Meanwhile, people with dementia find that beloved songs unlock emotional and autobiographical memories, sparking conversation even when everyday speech has become elusive. The act of group singing also triggers beneficial brain chemistry: oxytocin and endorphins rise while cortisol drops, fostering connection and easing stress. Given that one in three New Zealanders will face a neurological condition in their lifetime, the researcher behind CeleBRation has created resources to help others launch similar choirs. No musical experience is necessary—just a willingness to try. This story matters because it reveals how something as simple and joyful as singing together can offer people living with brain conditions not just therapy, but friendship, dignity, and a path back to themselves.
