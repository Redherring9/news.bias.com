'Sabores, Sons e Memórias': Reinado do Rosário transforma Itapecerica com fé, cultura e tradição há mais de 200 anos

In the Brazilian town of Itapecerica, the rhythmic beat of drums signals the annual transformation of the streets each August. For more than 200 years, the Reinado do Rosário—a celebration honoring Our Lady of the Rosary—has woven together faith, memory, and cultural identity in a tradition born from the African diaspora and shaped by resilience during slavery. Dating back to at least 1818, when the settlement was still known as Vila de Tamanduá, this living tradition has endured interruptions and changes while maintaining its essential character as both devotion and resistance. At the heart of the celebration are the guardas—ceremonial groups with distinct instruments, songs, and roles—that parade through the historic center in vibrant costumes. Seventy-seven-year-old Anielo Antônio D'Alessandro, the celebration's Capitão-Mor, carries memories passed down through generations of participants. He recalls how enslaved Africans, despite surveillance from landowners, held fast to their faith in the Virgin Mary while integrating Catholic saints into their own spiritual traditions. The Brotherhood of Our Lady of the Rosary of Black Men organized festivities in villages and settlements, creating sacred space within oppressive conditions. Different guardas evolved from earlier groups like the Terno de Moçambique and Terno de Mouros, each representing distinct narratives—from the Christian-Moorish conflicts to the story of Africans retrieving the Virgin from the sea. This story offers a window into how communities preserve identity across centuries, transforming colonial impositions into something distinctly their own. It's a testament to cultural continuity, where memory becomes inheritance and celebration becomes survival—a tradition that continues to define Itapecerica's identity today.

In rural Chad, solar kiosks bring healthcare closer

In rural Chad, where medical facilities are concentrated in distant cities, a solar-powered telemedicine network is reshaping how communities access healthcare. For residents like Ache Kodo in Bitkine, consulting a doctor once required saving money for long journeys that could cost upward of $200 when transport, accommodation, and consultation fees were combined. Now, through Telemedan kiosks stationed closer to home, she can see a doctor remotely for as little as $2 to $15. Founded in 2021 by Abakar Mahamat, who grew up witnessing the dual challenge of doctor shortages and geographic isolation, Telemedan operates through solar-powered stations equipped with diagnostic tools like blood-pressure monitors, glucometers, and even ultrasound equipment in some locations. A local operator registers patients, takes basic measurements, and facilitates the remote consultation with a doctor who appears on screen. The system is designed for areas with unreliable internet, storing patient data locally and syncing when connectivity returns, while a text-based service allows appointment booking without internet access. The network has expanded to 37 kiosks across 11 provinces, reaching an estimated 143,000 people with support from Chad's health ministry and various grants. While the kiosks cannot replace emergency care or specialist hospital treatment, they offer a practical bridge in a country where distance can be as formidable a barrier as the healthcare shortage itself. This story is worth attention because it illustrates how thoughtfully designed technology can meet people where they are—literally—turning a solar panel and a screen into a lifeline that allows families to seek care without sacrificing their next meal.

He listens carefully for the sound of impact and adjusts based on verbal feedback, relying entirely on touch, sound, and trust. His coach, Peter Bennett, notes the unique challenge: there's no visual relationship to the target, making Griffiths' progress all the more impressive. This story quietly illustrates how adaptive sports can transform lives after devastating health challenges. He wants to prove that blind people can do what others say they cannot, and he hopes to inspire his granddaughter and others living with disabilities. It's a reminder that determination and creativity can turn obstacles into opportunities—and that some of the most remarkable achievements happen when we aim at targets we cannot see.

China’s new moon mission could unlock secret of lunar ice: Why that matters

China is preparing to launch its most ambitious lunar mission yet, with Chang'e-7 set to explore the permanently shadowed craters at the moon's south pole in search of ice water. The mission, which could lift off any day between August 24 and August 31, will deploy an unprecedented combination of spacecraft: an orbiter to map and relay communications, a lander equipped with scientific instruments, a rover to analyze rocks and soil, and a solar-powered hopper designed to fly short distances and explore the depths of the 21-kilometer-wide Shackleton Crater. No previous lunar mission has ventured this close to the pole. Scientists have suspected the moon held water since the 1960s, but Apollo samples initially appeared dry. The breakthrough came in 2009 when NASA deliberately crashed a probe into a shadowed crater, confirming significant ice deposits. By 2020, researchers discovered that water on the moon is more widespread than imagined, with molecules trapped in mineral grains and ice patches hidden in areas that have remained dark for billions of years. These permanent shadows, untouched by sunlight for eons, may preserve a frozen record of the moon's volcanic past and ancient comet impacts that could reveal how Earth's own oceans formed. Beyond its scientific value, accessible lunar water could transform space exploration. It might provide drinking water for astronauts, coolant for equipment, hydrogen for fuel, and oxygen for breathing—potentially supporting future Mars missions or lunar mining operations. This story matters because it represents not just China's technological ambition, but humanity's evolving relationship with our nearest celestial neighbor, transforming the moon from a distant object of wonder into a potential resource for deeper space exploration.

How this 1960s ex-council house is managing to stay cool without air con

As the UK faces what may become its hottest summer on record, architect Marion Baeli's 1960s ex-council house in south London offers a glimpse into climate adaptation. On a sweltering 35°C day, her home maintained an interior temperature 10°C cooler than outside—without any air conditioning. The secret lies in the Passivhaus standard, a German building concept designed to keep temperatures constant by effectively sealing homes against the elements. When Baeli's family moved in a decade ago, the house was typical of many UK homes: draughty windows, minimal insulation, cold in winter and stifling in summer. Over ten years, she carried out a staged retrofit that included insulating shared walls, floors, and the roof, eliminating "thermal bridges" where heat transfers rapidly, installing triple-glazed windows, and creating an airtight seal with a ventilation system to maintain fresh air. The transformation reduced her energy demand by 88%, saving nearly £750 annually. Now, a single radiator heats the entire four-bedroom home across three floors during winter. The approach isn't without barriers—Baeli's retrofit cost around £80,000, with windows alone accounting for £30,000. She acknowledges this puts the standard out of reach for many, though spreading the work over time made it more manageable. With more than half of UK homes already at risk of overheating, and projections suggesting 92% will be by 2050 without adaptation, her house represents both a practical solution and a sobering reminder of the scale of the challenge ahead. It's a quiet testament to what's possible when thoughtful design meets the demands of a changing climate.
