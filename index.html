Inside the Wellington Town Hall after 13 years shut

After being shuttered for 13 years due to earthquake safety concerns, Wellington's historic Town Hall is nearing completion of what has become one of New Zealand's most complex building restoration projects. The venue, which first closed in 2013 after being deemed earthquake-prone on its original unreinforced concrete piles and reclaimed coastal land, has undergone a dramatic transformation—both structurally and in budget. The project ballooned from an initial $43 million estimate to nearly $330 million, though the final phase appears likely to come in $20 million under the latest budget. The engineering challenges were formidable: crews had to lift the entire building, install base isolation to protect against earthquakes, and navigate a stream running underneath—all while preserving the 121-year-old Adam Auditorium's world-renowned acoustics and the original 1906 organ. Modern touches complement the heritage restoration, including light installations honoring indigenous design and coastal themes. Beyond renovation, the Town Hall will house the new National Music Centre, a collaboration between Victoria University's music school and the New Zealand Symphony Orchestra. Students will share practice and performance spaces with professional musicians, filling the building with sound 135 days a year. What makes this story quietly remarkable is the vision of culture as civic infrastructure. Young trombonist Jakob Elijas captures it simply: inspiring environments shape the art people create. For a city that invested hundreds of millions in restoration, the hope is that filling a grand old building with hundreds of young musicians daily might give them reason to build careers at home rather than looking overseas—a modest but meaningful bet on culture keeping communities alive.

The swan sanctuary wetland fighting climate change

As the Wildfowl and Wetlands Trust celebrates eight decades since its founding, scientists are highlighting an unexpected climate ally: wetlands can absorb carbon four times faster than rainforests. The charity, which now operates ten reserves across the UK, is calling for the restoration of one million hectares of wetlands worldwide—a crucial goal given that 85% of the world's wetlands have already been lost. The story begins in 1946, when naturalist Peter Scott arrived at marshy land near Slimbridge, Gloucestershire, captivated by thousands of migrating geese and swans. He built a home with a panoramic window overlooking the wetlands, dug lakes and channels, and created observation towers. What started as one man's passion grew into a pioneering conservation movement. Scott went on to campaign against commercial whaling, present early BBC natural history programs, and help establish the World Wildlife Fund. His work earned him a knighthood in 1973—the first ever awarded for conservation. One of his boldest projects involved rescuing the Hawaiian Goose, or Nēnē, from near-extinction. When only 24 birds remained in 1949, Scott hand-reared them at Slimbridge, eventually helping the population recover to over 3,000. This story quietly reminds us that individual vision can spark systemic change. Beyond providing sanctuary for endangered species, these wetlands offer flood protection, water purification, and mood-boosting spaces for visitors—all while silently fighting climate change beneath the surface. It's a testament to how protecting nature's most overlooked places might be among our most powerful tools for the future.

Every evening at dusk, thousands of ring-necked parakeets descend on Hither Green cemetery in south-east London, creating a spectacle that feels oddly out of place in the English suburbs. The small breaks that help the brain learn new things

Os pequenos descansos que ajudam cérebro a aprender coisas novas

Common wisdom says practice makes perfect, but recent neuroscience research suggests the brain needs something else to truly lock in new skills: brief pauses. Scientists at the U.S. National Institutes of Health have discovered that during short breaks between practice sessions, the brain performs lightning-fast mental "replays" of what it just learned—up to 50 times faster than the actual movements. This phenomenon helps transform fleeting memories into lasting ones, offering fresh insight into how we master everything from piano scales to athletic techniques. In a 2021 study published in Cell Reports, researchers tracked brain activity in volunteers learning to type number sequences with their non-dominant hand. Using magnetoencephalography, they observed that after brief ten-second rest intervals, participants not only improved in speed and accuracy, but their brains were actively replaying the motor sequences in milliseconds. Researcher Leonardo Claudino explains that this consolidation happens far more quickly than previously believed, challenging the assumption that memory consolidation occurs mainly during sleep. While sleep may encode the broader context of an experience, these micro-breaks appear to cement the precise, repetitive details—the exact synergy between fingers on keys, for instance. This research holds promise beyond the practice room. Athletes, musicians, and stroke patients relearning lost skills might all benefit from structured rest intervals. The findings quietly reframe how we think about learning: mastery isn't just about relentless repetition, but about giving the brain space to weave new knowledge into its neural fabric. It's a reminder that progress sometimes happens not in the doing, but in the pausing—a small shift in approach with potentially profound implications for education, rehabilitation, and human potential.

The recent uptick coincides with a stretch of warmer, drier springs and summers, conditions researchers believe may be helping dormice thrive. These famously sleepy creatures hibernate from October through May and even enter torpor—brief periods of deep sleep—during chilly or wet summer days when food is scarce. Warmer weather appears to reduce their need for torpor, giving them more active time to feed, build fat reserves, and potentially raise additional litters. It's a striking example of how shifting climate patterns can reshape the fortunes of a species in unexpected ways. Yet conservationists urge caution. The population remains far below historical levels, and many questions linger about how dormice will cope with warmer, wetter winters and other long-term climate shifts. Protecting the woodlands and hedgerows these animals depend on remains essential. Why the human body may be cooling down

Por que o corpo humano pode estar esfriando

In the 1860s, German physician Carl Wunderlich collected over one million temperature measurements from 25,000 patients, establishing 37°C (98.6°F) as the standard for normal human body temperature. His extensive dataset became the universal diagnostic benchmark that has guided medical practice for more than a century. Yet recent research suggests something intriguing: our bodies may actually be cooler than they were in Wunderlich's time. Studies analyzing temperature data from nearly 190,000 Americans between 1862 and 2017 reveal that average body temperature has declined, with men's temperatures dropping by about 0.59°C since the early 19th century. The new normal appears closer to 36.39°C (97.5°F). This cooling trend isn't limited to developed nations—the Tsimané, a semi-nomadic indigenous community in the Amazon rainforest, experienced a similar drop from 37°C to 36.5°C between the early 2000s and 2018, coinciding with rapid socioeconomic changes in their region. Researchers believe this shift reflects improving public health. In Wunderlich's era, when life expectancy hovered around 38 years, people routinely battled tuberculosis, rheumatic fever, and other serious infections. Fighting these threats required significant caloric energy—which generates heat. Today's generally healthier populations may simply need less metabolic firepower to maintain wellness. This story offers a quiet reminder that our baseline understanding of the human body continues to evolve, and that broad improvements in living conditions can literally change our biology in measurable ways.
