How Scotland went from Europe's 'murder capital' to one of the safest places in the world

Como a Escócia foi de 'capital de assassinatos' da Europa a um dos lugares mais seguros do mundo

Scotland, particularly Glasgow, once held the grim distinction of being Europe's murder capital. Between 2003 and 2005, the United Nations labeled Scotland the most violent country in the developed world, with assault rates nearly three times higher than those in the United States. Knife attacks were a daily occurrence, and newspapers routinely covered brutal murders and bloody gang confrontations. The situation seemed hopeless, but an unconventional approach was about to change everything. In October 2008, a Glasgow courtroom witnessed an extraordinary scene: 85 members of rival gangs gathered to hear testimonies from victims and their families, doctors, and community members about the devastating impact of violence. Organized by the Scottish Violence Reduction Unit (SVRU), established in 2005, these "self-referral sessions" offered gang members a lifeline—a phone number to call if they wanted to leave violence behind. Of the 473 young people who attended ten such sessions, nearly 400 reached out for help. This initiative was part of a radical shift in how Scotland addressed violence: treating it not just as a criminal justice issue, but as a public health crisis. The results have been remarkable. Between 2006 and 2015, violent crime dropped by nearly a third across Scotland, with homicide rates falling 56% in Glasgow and 38% nationwide. Today, Scotland's murder rate sits in the middle of European rankings, lower than Sweden, France, and England and Wales. What makes this transformation quietly extraordinary is not just the statistics, but the willingness to try something different—to see beyond punishment and toward prevention, addressing the roots of violence with empathy rather than force alone.

In the Brazilian state of Rondônia, a university-led project is placing language preservation directly into the hands of Indigenous communities. DicWeb Indígena, developed by the Federal University of Rondônia, is a digital platform that allows Indigenous peoples across the Amazon to collaboratively create dictionaries of their own languages. Rather than relying on outside researchers to document vocabulary, community members themselves record words, audio pronunciations, translations, and everyday usage examples, maintaining control over how their linguistic heritage is represented. A symbol of summer, this Japanese dessert brings sweet relief

In the long and storied tradition of Japanese sweets that stretches back more than two millennia, anmitsu is a relative newcomer—but one that has captured hearts and taste buds for nearly a century. This beloved summer dessert was born in 1930 at Wakamatsu, a wagashi shop in Tokyo's Ginza district, when second-generation owner Hanjiro Mori listened to his customers' requests for something sweeter than his existing offerings. Mori's innovation was elegantly simple: he took his existing mitsumame—a refreshing mix of cubes of kanten (agar jelly), soft glutinous rice dumplings, boiled red peas, and brown sugar syrup—and added a generous serving of anko, the smooth adzuki bean paste central to so many Japanese confections. The result was a dessert that balanced textures and flavors in a way that resonated with sweet-seekers of the early Showa Era and continues to offer cool relief on warm days. This story reminds us that culinary tradition is always evolving, and that sometimes the most enduring creations come from simply listening to what people want. Anmitsu may be young compared to ancient treats like dango and mochi, but in less than a century it has earned its place as a symbol of Japanese summer—a testament to how a thoughtful addition can transform the familiar into something new and lasting.

Scientists at the University of Southern Queensland examined 50 years of temperature, rainfall, and heat records from eight towns across the region, uncovering patterns that are quietly reshaping how livestock producers plan their year. The findings reveal warmer summers stretching into what were once cooler months, fewer cool nights, and notably hotter conditions in the shoulder seasons of autumn and spring. For wool producer Peter Thomas and his wife Kimble, who manage 12,000 merino sheep near Longreach, these changes prompted a rethink of their breeding calendar. After noticing poor lamb survival during May lambing—when pregnant ewes endured the hottest months—they shifted their schedule so lambs arrive in August instead, allowing pregnancies to unfold during cooler weather. The result: a 20 percent improvement in lamb survival rates, enough to warrant continuing the experiment. What makes this story quietly remarkable is its practicality. Climate trend analysis is being translated into actionable farming decisions through tools developed by regional research hubs and natural resource groups like Desert Channels Queensland. The data shows some towns now experience eight or nine additional days per month above 35 degrees Celsius during October, and warmer nights mean heat stress lingers longer, affecting fertility and weight gain in livestock. For producers navigating an uncertain future, this half-century archive offers something invaluable: the ability to adapt with confidence, guided not by guesswork but by the patient record of five decades under the Australian sun.

The structure spans 13.7 kilometers in diameter and rises more than 400 meters high, with concentric rings still visible in satellite imagery today—like ripples spreading when a stone is dropped into water. The name "cangalha" comes from local tradition—when viewed from the side, the formation resembles a horse saddle. This story offers a fascinating glimpse into deep geological time and the powerful forces that shaped our planet's surface. Ele 'rabiscava' versos entre as entregas: o entregador de comida que ganhou um dos principais prêmios literários da China

Wang Jibing, a 56-year-old food delivery worker in China, has won the prestigious Lu Xun Literary Prize for his poetry collection *Low Flight*—a work inspired by interviews with around 200 fellow delivery workers. Many of his verses were scribbled on scraps of paper between deliveries, capturing the rhythms and pressures of gig economy life. The prize, named after the father of modern Chinese literature, comes with significant recognition and a monetary award, marking an extraordinary milestone for someone who left school as a teenager due to his family's financial struggles. Wang's path to literary acclaim was anything but linear. Before delivering food, he worked in construction, collected recyclables, and sold goods from a street stall. Yet through it all, his love of reading endured. Over the past decade, he began sharing poems and essays on social media. In 2022, his poem *A Man in a Hurry* went viral after recounting hours spent trying to deliver an order to the wrong address. Despite his success, Wang has no plans to quit his job—he intends to keep working until he turns 60, valuing the routine and physical activity it provides. "I want to live a simple and grounded life," he told state media. "The prize will never change who I am." Wang's story reflects a broader trend in China, where workers from various sectors are gaining literary recognition. His win, alongside bestsellers by other gig workers, offers a rare window into the lives of millions navigating China's vast informal labor market. It's a quiet reminder that poetry can emerge from the most unexpected places—and that flying low, as Wang writes, is still flying.

Orcas 'explode' ton-sized ocean sunfish in hunting technique

Orcas 'explodem' peixe-lua de toneladas em técnica de caça

Researchers have documented a striking and previously unknown hunting technique used by orcas in the Gulf of California: launching themselves at full speed against ocean sunfish carcasses with enough force to shatter the massive animals into thousands of pieces. The behavior, described in a study published in the journal Frontiers in Ethology, involves a coordinated sequence where one orca holds the fish by its tail fin while another accelerates and rams into it at high velocity, releasing just before impact to maximize the explosive effect. What makes this discovery particularly intriguing is the size of the prey involved. The sharptail mola, one of the heaviest bony fish on the planet, can reach three meters in length and weigh up to two tons—yet the orcas reduce them to fragments scattered through the water column. The technique was observed on two separate occasions more than a year apart, both times involving already-dead fish, which puzzled scientists. The leading hypothesis is that fragmenting the carcass serves a practical purpose: creating smaller, manageable portions for young calves whose jaws aren't yet strong enough to tear apart large pieces of food. However, researchers acknowledge another possibility—it might simply be play. This behavior offers a window into the sophisticated cognitive abilities of orcas, requiring spatial awareness, acoustic communication, and shared understanding of physics to execute successfully. The story reminds us that even apex predators we've studied for decades continue to surprise us with new behaviors, and that the ocean still holds countless mysteries about how intelligent social animals interact with their world and each other.

For Brazil’s Xokleng people, saving a sacred tree means saving their culture

In the Atlantic Forest of southern Brazil, the Laklãnõ/Xokleng Indigenous people are working to save a tree that sits at the heart of their identity. The Brazilian pine, called "Zág" in the Xokleng language, appears in their creation myths and has shaped their culture for generations. But after centuries of deforestation and logging reduced the species' original range by more than 97%, it now faces critical endangerment—a fate the Xokleng see mirrored in the threats to their own community, language, and traditions. What began in 2016 as one man's modest plan to plant ten seedlings has grown into a full-scale restoration project. Carl Gakran, executive director of the Zág Institute, has mobilized his community to produce more than 140,000 seedlings, planting nearly 100,000 across 1,000 hectares of their traditional territory. The effort involves schools, families, and collective planting events throughout the year. Children are encouraged to care for individual seedlings as if they were pets, creating a personal connection to the trees. The reforestation work is restoring not just the pines themselves but also degraded land and water sources across the region. This story quietly illustrates how cultural preservation and ecological restoration can be inseparable. For the Xokleng, saving the Brazilian pine isn't simply about conservation—it's about maintaining a living link to their history, their language, and their place in the world. It's a reminder that the most effective environmental stewardship often comes from those whose identities are woven into the land itself.

Islanders asked to 'track' not 'trample' Asian hornets

On the small British island of Guernsey, residents are being enlisted in an unusual conservation effort: carefully tracking Asian hornets rather than simply destroying them on sight. The approach reflects a thoughtful strategy to protect both public safety and the island's delicate ecosystem from an invasive species that has steadily spread across Europe since 2004, when the hornets reportedly arrived in southern France hidden in pottery shipments from Asia. Asian hornets pose a significant threat to Guernsey's biodiversity, particularly its honeybee population, which makes up nearly two-fifths of the hornet's diet. Since the species first appeared on the island in 2017, local authorities have focused on keeping numbers low by locating and destroying primary nests before they can expand. The "Track Don't Trample" campaign asks islanders to photograph any hornets they spot, note the direction of travel, and report their observations rather than attempting removal themselves. This citizen-science approach has already proven effective: so far this year, volunteers have helped authorities capture 205 Asian hornet queens and destroy nine primary nests across Guernsey and the neighboring island of Herm. The effort is especially timely as nests can grow explosively during late summer, housing up to 2,000 hornets in trees, hedges, and buildings. Officials emphasize the importance of caution, urging people to check for hornet activity before trimming vegetation. What makes this story quietly remarkable is how it demonstrates the power of community engagement in environmental stewardship—turning everyday islanders into ecological detectives whose careful observations help protect both human neighbors and the pollinators that sustain local agriculture.

Bikini at 80: How a swimsuit named after a nuclear bomb became a symbol of Brazilian identity

Biquíni, 80 anos: como traje batizado por causa de bomba nuclear se tornou símbolo de brasilidade

This year marks the 80th anniversary of the bikini, a garment whose origin story weaves together post-war France, nuclear testing in the Pacific, and an unlikely path to becoming synonymous with Brazilian beach culture. In July 1946, French mechanical engineer Louis Réard pivoted from the automotive industry to his mother's lingerie business and launched what he called "the world's smallest swimsuit"—a two-piece design that exposed the navel for the first time. While two-piece swimwear existed before, Réard's version daringly minimized fabric in ways earlier attempts had failed to popularize. He named it after Bikini Atoll in the Marshall Islands, where the United States was conducting nuclear tests, signaling that his creation would have explosive cultural impact. The debut was indeed controversial. No professional model would wear it, so Réard hired 18-year-old exotic dancer Micheline Bernardini to model the suit at a Parisian public pool. Religious authorities, including Pope Pius XII, condemned it as sinful, and several European countries banned or restricted its use on public beaches. The bikini represented more than new swimwear—it challenged social codes about morality and how women's bodies were regulated. Yet despite initial scandal, the garment gradually spread through American pop culture and found its most natural home in Brazil, where beach culture, especially in Rio de Janeiro, embraced it fully. This story resonates because it captures how a small piece of fabric became a flashpoint for changing attitudes about women's autonomy, body image, and cultural expression. The bikini's journey from shocking provocation to everyday beachwear—and its particular association with Brazilian identity—reveals how fashion can both reflect and drive social transformation across decades and continents.

Outback hubs prepare for 'amazingly majestic' 2028 solar eclipse

A rare astronomical event is quietly transforming the remote Australian outback. On July 22, 2028, a total solar eclipse will sweep across the Northern Territory, and the tiny communities of Tennant Creek and Devils Marbles are bracing for an unprecedented influx of visitors. Tourism officials estimate around 10,000 eclipse chasers will descend on the Barkly region, drawn by the chance to stand in the moon's shadow as it crosses one of the world's most dramatic landscapes. The preparation is considerable. The Devils Marbles Hotel, which normally accommodates about 60 guests, has been fielding booking requests since late 2024—just hours after new management took over. Local officials are scouting campsites along the Stuart Highway, considering everything from portable toilets to security needs. There's been interest from Michelin-star chefs eager to cook in the outback and Aboriginal tourism operators planning cultural experiences. Mayor Sid Vashist is urging the government to declare it a major event to unlock additional resources, calling it the biggest calendar event Tennant Creek will ever see. What makes this moment worth the journey? Astronomers describe totality—when the moon completely blocks the sun—as an experience that transcends ordinary observation. The world holds its breath, darkness falls at midday, and colours shift in unsettling ways. It's both majestic and primal, eclipse chaser Kate Russo explains. Even being 99 percent eclipsed isn't enough; you must stand directly in the shadow's 200-kilometer-wide path. For a few precious minutes in 2028, the remote Australian outback will become one of the most sought-after places on Earth.

New bird species identified on remote Indonesian island

A songbird long believed to be a single species living across two Indonesian island groups has turned out to be two distinct species, according to recent research. For over 120 years, scientists thought the cinnamon-tailed fantail lived on both the Babar and Tanimbar islands in eastern Indonesia. Now, researchers have confirmed that the Babar population is its own species, newly named the cheerful fantail. The discovery came through careful detective work combining museum specimens and field recordings. Researchers examined 19 historical specimens and conducted 132 playback experiments over several years, recording the birds' songs and playing them back to wild populations. While physical differences between the two species are subtle—the cheerful fantail has slightly darker feathers and less cinnamon coloring—their songs tell a different story. Birds on Babar sing single whistles that rise in pitch, while Tanimbar birds produce more rhythmic combinations of short and long whistles. Crucially, birds consistently responded to recordings of their own population but ignored songs from the other island, suggesting they wouldn't recognize each other as potential mates. This finding matters beyond scientific taxonomy. With the single species now split in two, both the cheerful fantail and cinnamon-tailed fantail have much smaller ranges than previously thought, potentially qualifying them for threatened status. However, both birds appear remarkably adaptable, thriving in modified habitats and clearings rather than pristine forests. The story is a quiet reminder that even in our well-documented world, new species await discovery—sometimes hiding in plain sight, distinguished not by dramatic differences but by the songs they sing to one another across a stretch of sea.

Orcas seen ramming prey so hard it explodes may be playing game

Off the coast of Mexico, marine biologists have documented a striking orca behavior: the whales are ramming into giant sunfish with such force that the fish explode into thousands of fragments. The behavior was first captured on a GoPro camera in July 2024, when researchers witnessed a female orca holding a sunfish carcass by the tail while an adult male charged at high speed, creating an audible crunch and a cloud of white tissue. A similar event was recorded by a tourism operator in September 2025, raising questions about why these apex predators engage in such dramatic displays. Sunfish are already a regular part of the orca diet—these massive fish can grow over three meters long and weigh up to 2,000 kilograms. Scientists have proposed two explanations for the ramming behavior. It may serve a practical purpose, breaking down the sunfish's tough, rubbery body into smaller pieces that juvenile orcas with smaller jaws can more easily consume. But given what we know about orca intelligence and culture, it could also simply be play. Orcas are famous for their social fads and playful antics, from balancing dead salmon on their heads like hats to surfing in boat wakes and engaging in elaborate games with kelp. What makes this story quietly remarkable is what it reveals about the depth of orca cognition. The coordinated, multi-step behavior suggests sophisticated planning and communication. It's also a reminder that modern wildlife tourism—complete with underwater drones and cameras—is opening new windows into animal lives, capturing mysteries that might otherwise remain invisible to science. The challenge now is balancing our curiosity with the respectful distance these intelligent creatures deserve.

School students successfully launch high-altitude balloon into the atmosphere

A group of ten primary school students in Lower Hutt, New Zealand, successfully launched a high-altitude weather balloon that soared 26,700 meters into the atmosphere—three times the height of Mount Everest—before landing on a farm in Pahiatua, less than 10 kilometers from their predicted touchdown spot. The Maungaraki School pupils, part of their tongue-in-cheek "Maungaraki Aeronautic Space Administration" (MASA), spent ten weeks preparing for the launch, with each student taking on specialized roles from avionics engineering to logistics and recovery. The balloon carried two 360-degree cameras and instruments that recorded video, temperature, pressure, and altitude data, which the children used to map its journey in 3D. The project was far more than a science experiment. Students had to solve real-world challenges, like insulating electronics against temperatures as low as -60°C and coordinating with landowners for potential recovery sites. They even prepared gift packs of treats and coffee for farmers who might help retrieve their payload. When the balloon landed in dense scrub, design teacher Ashton Stuart had to ford rivers and climb a tree to recover it, while students back at school tracked its location. The team celebrated their success with ice cream, and for many students, the experience sparked dreams of future STEM careers. This story is worth reading because it shows how curiosity and hands-on learning can transform abstract concepts into tangible adventures. Only a handful of schools in New Zealand have attempted such a launch, and the students' genuine excitement—one couldn't sleep the night before—reminds us that wonder and rigorous science make excellent companions.

Even camels can't cope: Africa's ships of the desert hit by rising temperatures

In one of Earth's most unforgiving landscapes, the animals most synonymous with desert survival are now struggling. Across the Afar region of Ethiopia and throughout the Horn of Africa, camels—long celebrated for their remarkable tolerance to heat and aridity—are showing troubling signs of distress as temperatures climb beyond even their extraordinary capacity to adapt. Herders report blistered feet, watery eyes, burned skin, and calves dying from heat exposure, while the very winds that once brought relief now carry scorching air. The impacts extend far beyond individual suffering. Recent studies document mortality rates reaching 73% in some Somali regions, with nearly half of camel-owning households experiencing drought-related losses. Veterinarians across Kenya, Ethiopia, and Somalia report a surge in hyperthermia cases, infectious diseases, and a 15% increase in deaths over two years in some counties. The crisis affects the 80% of the world's dromedary camels living in North Africa and the Horn of Africa—animals that pastoralists increasingly rely upon precisely because they were thought resilient to climate extremes. Yet rising temperatures are stripping away vegetation, evaporating water sources, and eliminating shaded refuges these animals need. This story matters because it reveals climate change reaching a threshold few anticipated: the point where even nature's most heat-adapted creatures can no longer cope. When camels—evolutionary marvels of desert survival—begin to fail, it signals both the severity of environmental shifts and the precarious future facing the millions of people whose livelihoods depend on these remarkable animals.

Captain Cook Shopping Centre sold to Arnhem Land Aboriginal corporation

The Gumatj Corporation purchased what was long known as the Captain Cook Shopping Centre—despite the British explorer never having visited the area—and rechristened it the Gumatj Retail Precinct after one of the region's traditional clan groups. The complex houses essential services including the town's post office, hardware store, and café. The acquisition takes on added significance as Nhulunbuy faces economic uncertainty with Rio Tinto's bauxite mining project set to wind down in 2029. Michael Yunupiŋu, grandson of celebrated land rights leader Galarrwuy Yunupiŋu, led the negotiations and sees the purchase as a pathway to economic independence and local employment. The Gumatj Corporation, which has previously developed community assets including a timber mill, aims to ensure that wealth generated in the region remains within it, supporting the local Yolŋu workforce and creating opportunities for the future. This story offers a quiet but meaningful glimpse into community self-determination and economic transition. It's about more than changing a sign—it's about a community actively shaping its own future, honoring the vision of leaders who thought generations ahead, and anchoring local prosperity as the region navigates change. The handover represents both an ending and a beginning, marking the departure of a long-term local businessman while opening a new chapter of Indigenous-led economic stewardship.
