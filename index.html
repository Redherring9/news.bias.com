‘Once-in-a-lifetime chance’: Japan launches historic mission to collect rocks from Mars’s moon Phobos

Japan has launched an ambitious mission to collect rocks from Phobos, one of Mars's two small moons, in what scientists are calling a historic first. The Martian Moons Exploration spacecraft lifted off aboard an H3 rocket from Tanegashima island in the pre-dawn hours, watched by thousands via livestream. If successful, the mission will return at least 10 grams of soil and rock from Phobos to Earth by 2031, marking the first time any nation has attempted to land on either of Mars's potato-shaped moons. The five-year, $578 million mission is a collaborative effort involving five space agencies, including NASA and European partners. After reaching Mars orbit in August 2026, the spacecraft will deploy a French-German rover named Idefix, which will bounce across Phobos's surface for 100 days to scout landing sites. The main lander will then spend just two hours drilling and collecting samples before launching back into space. Scientists hope the samples will help settle a fundamental question: were Phobos and Deimos formed by a giant impact with Mars, or are they captured asteroids? The answer could reveal important clues about how habitable environments arose on Earth and other terrestrial planets. This mission arrives amid a broader race to return samples from the Martian system. While NASA's Mars sample return has been halted and China's Tianwen-3 mission won't launch until 2028, Japan's focus on Phobos offers a unique window into the early solar system. For the engineers and scientists involved, it represents a career-defining moment—a chance to touch a world no human probe has ever visited and bring home answers to questions billions of years in the making.

Colorado wildlife overpass cut vehicle-animal collisions by 91%, report finds

A wildlife overpass in Colorado has proven remarkably effective in its first ten months, reducing collisions between vehicles and animals by 91% along a busy stretch of Interstate-25. State officials have documented elk, bears, pronghorn, and even northern leopard frogs using the bridge, which connects 39,000 acres of habitat between Denver and Colorado Springs. Before the overpass was built, this corridor averaged one wildlife-vehicle crash per day. The structure spans six lanes of traffic and measures 200 feet wide and 209 feet long, covered in grass with gradually sloped entrances. It was specifically designed for large game animals like elk and pronghorn, which avoid the confined spaces of underpasses and tunnels. For pronghorn migrating between Trinidad and Greenland, the overpass represents the first safe crossing opportunity in decades. Smaller animals, including mule deer, coyotes, rabbits, and the threatened Preble's meadow jumping mouse, have also been spotted using the corridor. Colorado wildlife biologist Brandon Marette described the heartwarming sight of an elk mother safely guiding her calf under the interstate. This story matters because it demonstrates how thoughtful infrastructure can reconcile human development with wildlife needs. Research shows such crossings nearly eliminate animal-vehicle accidents and often pay for themselves by preventing costly crashes. With nearly 20 similar projects underway across the United States, Colorado's success—completed under budget at $15 million instead of the anticipated $30 million—offers a compelling model for protecting both people and animals in an increasingly fragmented landscape.

What Phobos Is Like, the Moon of Mars That Is the Destination of a New and Revolutionary Space Mission

Cómo es Fobos, la luna de Marte que es el destino de una nueva y revolucionaria misión espacial

A Japanese spacecraft is preparing to launch on an ambitious mission to collect samples from Phobos, the larger of Mars's two tiny moons, and return them to Earth. If successful, the Mars Moons eXploration (MMX) mission will mark the first time material from Mars or its moons has been brought back for laboratory analysis on our planet. The spacecraft is scheduled to depart from Japan and reach Phobos by August 2027, with samples expected to return to Earth's surface in 2031. Phobos is a peculiar object—just 20 kilometers in diameter, irregularly shaped like a space potato, and orbiting extremely close to Mars. Landing on such a small body presents unique challenges, as it has minimal gravity of its own while sitting next to a massive planet with much stronger gravitational pull. The mission will deploy a small rover to scout the terrain before the main spacecraft touches down to collect approximately 10 grams of lunar dust using robotic arms and sample collectors. Though modest in quantity, scientists say even individual grains can yield significant discoveries. The samples promise to solve a long-standing mystery: did Phobos and its companion moon Deimos form from debris after a massive collision with early Mars, or are they captured asteroids from the outer solar system? Chemical and mineral analysis should provide a clear answer. There's an added bonus too—Phobos orbits so close to Mars that grains from the Martian surface may be embedded in the moon itself, potentially offering direct insights into the Red Planet's composition and history without ever landing there.

A Belgium-sized area of Amazon wetland has been converted, and losses are climbing

Over the past four decades, an area of Amazon wetlands roughly the size of Belgium has been cleared, with nearly a third of that loss occurring in just the last nine years. These seasonally flooded forests cover about 30% of the Brazilian Amazon and have long supported riverine communities who fish and farm in rhythm with the annual flood cycle. The acceleration of wetland conversion raises questions about which land management approaches actually protect these fragile ecosystems. Researchers examining 13 different land designation types discovered striking differences in conservation outcomes. Undesignated public lands showed the highest conversion rates—nearly 14%—driven largely by land-grabbing practices where people deliberately clear and burn forest to demonstrate "productive use" and stake ownership claims. In contrast, Indigenous territories, whether formally recognized or awaiting demarcation, maintained conversion rates under 0.3%. Military-controlled areas performed even better, with conversion below 0.01%. Government agrarian reform settlements designed for individual farming families showed troubling trends: wetland clearing accelerated from 0.9% before settlement to 24% after fifteen years, as occupants rushed to meet productivity requirements with minimal environmental oversight. Sustainable settlements with collective land tenure fared somewhat better, though conversion still occurred. This research quietly underscores a crucial insight: effective conservation depends less on simply having designated owners than on having meaningful rules and support systems in place. The contrast between land-grabbing incentives and Indigenous stewardship reveals how policy design shapes landscape outcomes. Margaret Hamilton obituary

Margaret Hamilton, who passed away at age 90, was a pioneering software engineer whose work proved essential to the Apollo moon landings. In an era when NASA's space program was overwhelmingly male and white, Hamilton led the software engineering division at MIT's Instrumentation Laboratory, overseeing development of the Apollo Guidance Computer—a revolutionary system that allowed astronauts to navigate and control spacecraft with unprecedented precision. Hamilton's innovation replaced the joystick controls of earlier Gemini missions with computer-linked keypads, creating more accurate flight paths and freeing astronauts for other critical tasks. The system represented a technological leap: it was the first computer built on silicon-integrated circuits, the same technology that would eventually power everyday devices from smartphones to laptops. Her meticulous preparation proved vital during Apollo 11's historic descent in July 1969, when alarms threatened to abort the landing.
