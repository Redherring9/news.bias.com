Meet Phil, the bar-tailed godwit tracked on a 6,800-mile round trip to Siberia

A bar-tailed godwit named Phil has given scientists an unprecedented window into one of nature's most impressive migratory journeys. Fitted with a lightweight 6-gram tracker in January on England's Norfolk coast, Phil traveled more than 6,800 miles roundtrip to Siberia's Taymyr peninsula and back, revealing the full scope of this small wading bird's endurance capabilities. Though not much larger than a pigeon, bar-tailed godwits are known for making the longest recorded non-stop flights of any bird species. Phil's journey took him from the Wash estuary to the Wadden Sea coastlines of the Netherlands, Denmark, and Germany before his late-May arrival in the Arctic Circle for breeding season. His return route carried him over the Arctic Ocean and past northern Norway, with brief stops on three small Norwegian islands before returning to the Wadden Sea and finally to RSPB Snettisham. The tracking data highlights the importance of the east Atlantic flyway, a migratory superhighway stretching from the Arctic to South Africa that millions of birds travel annually. This story matters because it illuminates the invisible threads connecting distant ecosystems across continents. Phil's successful journey underscores why conservation must be collaborative and international—these birds depend on a chain of wetlands, each one essential to their survival. The mudflats of the Wash aren't just a local treasure; they're a critical link in a hemispheric network. In naming the bird after longtime research leader Phil Ireland, scientists have created a touching reminder that understanding and protecting migratory species requires patience, dedication, and a recognition that borders mean nothing to wings.

Star 350 times larger than the Sun caught eating its neighbour

For the first time, astronomers have watched a stellar merger unfold from start to finish. Over seven years, an international team led by Columbia University tracked a star system called AT2025abao in the Andromeda galaxy, observing as a red giant—nicknamed Big Bao—gradually consumed its smaller companion, Little Bao. Big Bao, roughly 350 times larger than our Sun, swelled with age and engulfed the Sun-like star orbiting nearby. The dramatic finale came in October 2025, when the system suddenly brightened, signaling Little Bao's final moments. This process, called common envelope evolution, happens when an aging giant star expands and traps a smaller neighbor inside its outer plasma shell. The smaller star spirals inward, either merging with the core or ejecting the shell and forming a tight binary system. Such interactions are thought to produce some of the universe's most extreme objects, like merging neutron stars and white dwarfs. Yet witnessing the event in real time had been nearly impossible—it lasts only a few years against the backdrop of stellar lifespans measured in millions of years. The team got lucky, noticing unusual brightness in 2023 and tracing it back through archival data to 2018. This discovery offers more than cosmic spectacle. It provides direct evidence for a process long understood only in theory, allowing scientists to refine models of how binary star systems evolve. As more powerful telescopes come online, researchers expect to catch similar events more often, turning rare glimpses into a richer understanding of the universe's hidden dynamics. It's a reminder that patience, timing, and a little luck can reveal the universe's most dramatic transformations.

Could insects help turn Marlborough's primary industry waste into animal feed?

In Marlborough, New Zealand, researchers are exploring an innovative solution to a dual challenge: what to do with mountains of organic waste from the wine and seafood industries, and where to source protein for animal feed. Their answer involves black soldier flies, insects capable of transforming grape marc and aquaculture byproducts into high-quality protein in less than two weeks. The three-year, $2.6 million pilot project will operate out of two shipping containers, functioning as both a fly nursery and processing facility. Black soldier fly larvae—chosen because they're the most widely farmed insect globally and grow remarkably fast—will be mixed with slurries of organic waste. Within days, they'll reach harvestable size and can be dried and milled into protein for fish and pet food. The operation aims to process 250 kilograms of waste daily, producing around nine tonnes of insect biomass annually. Even the leftovers have value: the remaining frass, a mixture of uneaten food and insect waste, is nutrient-rich and could improve soil quality. The biggest challenge will be maintaining consistent feed quality across seasons, requiring careful blending of grape waste with seafood byproducts to keep nutrient levels balanced. This story matters because it represents a genuinely circular approach to resource use—turning what would otherwise go to landfill into valuable products while reducing New Zealand's reliance on imported animal feed. With organic matter comprising 40 percent of Marlborough's landfill waste and feed protein shortages looming globally, the project addresses real environmental and economic pressures with an elegantly simple biological solution. It's a quiet example of innovation that works with nature rather than against it.

NASA’s Voyager 1 has reached 1 light-day from Earth, the first human-made object in space to reach that distance

On November 18, 2026, NASA's Voyager 1 spacecraft will become the first human-made object to reach one light-day from Earth—a distance of roughly 16 billion miles, more than four times the span between the Sun and Pluto. At that point, any signal from the probe will take a full 24 hours to reach home. This milestone arrives just months before Voyager 1 and its twin, Voyager 2, celebrate their 50th anniversaries in space in 2027, making them the oldest operating spacecraft in history. Launched in 1977, the Voyagers were designed to explore the outer planets. Voyager 1 flew past Jupiter and Saturn, capturing unprecedented images and making discoveries like faint rings around Jupiter and new details of Saturn's moons. Its close encounter with Titan in 1980 revealed a moon shrouded in a dense atmosphere, findings that continue to inform missions like NASA's upcoming Dragonfly. After its planetary tour, Voyager 1 was flung out of the plane of the solar system and eventually crossed into interstellar space—the region between stars. Despite being built with 1970s technology, the spacecraft continues to send back data, though only two of its original ten instruments remain functional and its plutonium power source weakens each year. This story is a quiet testament to human ingenuity and endurance. A relic from another era of exploration, Voyager 1 has far outlasted its expected lifespan, reshaping our understanding of the solar system and beyond. As it drifts ever farther into the cosmic unknown, it reminds us that some journeys, once begun, continue long after we expect them to end.
