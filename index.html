The artist subverting colonial history in her new photography show

Atong Atem has become the first African Australian artist to mount a solo exhibition at the National Gallery of Victoria, a milestone that reflects both her singular vision and the institution's evolving commitment to contemporary voices. Her show, *Passage*, is a layered exploration of identity, history, and power, featuring self-portraits in which Atem appears in various costumes and personas—not as autobiography, she insists, but as visual inquiry and performance. Born in Ethiopia to South Sudanese parents and raised in a Kenyan refugee camp before settling in regional New South Wales at age six, Atem trained in painting and photography at institutions including RMIT. Her work has garnered international attention and prestigious awards, including the inaugural NGV and MECCA M-Power scholarship. The exhibition draws on a wide range of themes: postpartum identity, the racist legacy of ethnographic photography, family albums, tarot, and the history of enslavement. In one series, *Men in this Town*, Atem poses in neon suits and a Stetson hat—a nod to South Sudanese political leader Salva Kiir—playfully challenging expectations around motherhood, femininity, and Blackness. Her portraits are bright, direct, and unapologetically confrontational, inverting the exploitative gaze of colonial-era imagery by asserting full agency over her own image. What makes *Passage* quietly remarkable is its refusal of simple narratives. Atem uses her own face as a canvas for questions rather than answers, blending personal experience with broader historical reckonings. It's a thoughtful reminder that photography can be both deeply personal and expansive, a medium for thinking out loud about who gets to be seen—and on whose terms.

Stranded turtle's 16-hour road trip to recovery

A young loggerhead turtle found floating in chilly waters off Esperance, Western Australia, has begun an unexpected journey to recovery thanks to one woman's dedication. Letesha Heslewood, a retail worker from Bunbury, drove nearly 1,400 kilometers round trip—16 hours on the road—to retrieve the stranded turtle after seeing an urgent late-night volunteer call-out. The juvenile loggerhead, affectionately nicknamed "Shaggy" for the thick layer of green algae coating his shell, had wandered far from his tropical habitat into dangerously cold southern waters that left him dehydrated and vulnerable. The rescue trip itself was marked by gentle care: every few hours, Heslewood would stop to splash clean water over Shaggy's eyes and shell, a ritual the little turtle visibly enjoyed. For Heslewood, who rarely works with wildlife in her day job, the experience was transformative. She's now registered as a volunteer wildlife transport officer, hoping to make more such journeys. Marine scientists note that while loggerheads typically nest around Ningaloo and Shark Bay in Western Australia's warmer north, it's not unusual for turtles to drift southward when sick or injured, sometimes seeking calmer, warmer inshore waters to recover. Shaggy is now recuperating in a heated tank at Bunbury's Dolphin Discovery Centre, where he'll remain until April. Then, staff plan to transport him north to Exmouth for release off the Ningaloo coast, back to waters more suited to his species. It's a story that quietly illustrates how acts of individual compassion—a long drive through the night, regular water splashes, patient care—can give vulnerable creatures a second chance at life in their proper home.

Vanuatu woman internationally recognised for solar scheme tackling violence

In the village of Lawital on the island of Tanna in Vanuatu, women have found an unexpected tool in their fight against violence: solar-powered lights. In a place where many traditional villages have no electricity, the darkness after sunset brought danger. Women described locking themselves indoors at night with machetes for protection, fearing attacks both from strangers and intimate partners. Vanuatu has one of the world's highest rates of intimate partner violence, with half of surveyed women reporting they had experienced physical or sexual violence from a partner during their lifetime. Fed up with the fear that surrounded them, Dalila Laus Lalulu and other local women took action. They installed solar-powered lights in streets and nearly 120 homes, and distributed dozens of solar lanterns for women to carry. The partnership with the Australian government and local organizations has brought light to a community of 1,380 people. While only anecdotal at this stage, residents report that violence has decreased and women now feel safe enough to travel to and from their gardens after dark, work evening market stalls, and take on roles maintaining the solar system—activities that have increased household incomes and elevated women's standing in the community. Children can now complete homework at night. The ripple effects extend beyond safety. Lalulu, who initiated the project, has been invited to become the first woman on the Tanna Chief's Council, a traditional governing body. She recently traveled outside Vanuatu for the first time to receive a Gender Just Climate Solutions Award at the COP-31 summit. This story reminds us how a simple, practical solution can address multiple challenges at once—illuminating not just streets, but pathways to safety, economic opportunity, and social change.

Rare 1941 plane returns to Nelson skies

A gleaming piece of aviation history has returned to Nelson, New Zealand, nearly 65 years after it last flew scheduled routes there. The Kuaka, a meticulously restored 1941 Lockheed 10A Electra with a striking chrome exterior, drew crowds at Nelson Airport during a special visit. This rare art deco airliner is one of only two still flying worldwide, the other residing in the Czech Republic. During World War II, these sophisticated monoplanes were the sole aircraft servicing Nelson, and they pioneered most of New Zealand's airline routes in the late 1930s. The aircraft's journey back to the skies is a testament to dedication and patience. Owner Rob Mackley discovered it advertised in a magazine in 1996, flew to Alaska on a whim to view it, and won it through a sealed-bid tender despite having no benchmark for its value. What he optimistically estimated as a three-year restoration stretched into nearly three decades as he and his team worked to remain faithful to every detail. Mackley and his wife Caroline, both pilots, now use the plane to give passengers a taste of what early aviation was like—smooth, quiet, and luxurious by 1940s standards. During the Nelson visit, air cadet Tayla McLean was among those who experienced flight aboard this living museum piece. This story offers a quiet reminder of how passion and perseverance can resurrect forgotten marvels. Beyond the impressive restoration itself, the Kuaka represents a tangible connection to New Zealand's aviation heritage—a time when these sleek metal birds were faster than military aircraft and represented the cutting edge of travel. It's a story about preserving not just a machine, but an experience of a bygone era.

Miguel Ángel Asturias, the Guatemalan writer who won the Nobel Prize for Literature in 1967, has remained curiously overlooked compared to his Latin American contemporaries like García Márquez, Vargas Llosa, and Neruda. A pioneer of magical realism and one of the first to portray dictators in Latin American fiction, Asturias brought indigenous Maya culture and mythology into the continental literary conversation through monumental works like "Men of Maize," "Legends of Guatemala," and "The President." His narrative universe blended dreams, myth, and everyday life in a distinctly Guatemalan voice that anticipated the literary boom to come. Yet the same year he received the Nobel, a bitter dispute with García Márquez over "One Hundred Years of Solitude"—along with political controversies and generational rifts—cast a shadow over his legacy. Though a leftist intellectual forced into exile by dictatorships, he found himself rejected by parts of the Latin American left during Cold War tensions. After his death in 1974, Asturias was buried in Paris rather than under dictatorship in Guatemala or Spain, where he remained for over fifty years. This week, his remains finally returned home to state honors and four days of commemoration. The story reminds us how literary reputations can be as influenced by personal conflicts and political crosscurrents as by the work itself. For readers willing to look past the forgotten controversies, Asturias offers a voice deeply rooted in indigenous tradition and a pioneering vision that helped shape Latin American literature—a legacy worth rediscovering.

Return of the wild oyster: conservationists bring shellfish back from local extinction off Scottish coast

Off the coast of Scotland, something remarkable is happening beneath the waves. For the first time in the UK, scientists have confirmed that wild European flat oysters are reproducing naturally at a site where they had vanished more than a century ago. In the Dornoch Firth north of Inverness, researchers discovered baby oysters — each about the size of a 50-pence coin — proving that a twelve-year restoration effort is finally bearing fruit. The story of Britain's native oysters is one of dramatic boom and bust. Victorian-era maps reveal that billions once carpeted the seabed, forming vast reefs that supported thriving marine ecosystems. At their peak, 30 million oysters a year were harvested from the Firth of Forth alone, shipped by railway to feed growing cities. But within decades, these populations were fished to extinction across much of the UK. Today, European flat oysters are critically threatened worldwide. The Dornoch Environmental Enhancement Project has spent over a decade carefully relocating roughly 140,000 adult oysters from one of Scotland's few surviving populations, cleaned by volunteers to protect biosecurity. The breakthrough came when researchers found juveniles thriving on their own — helped, unexpectedly, by warmer seas in recent years. This quiet success matters because oysters do far more than feed us. They filter water, stabilize sediments, store carbon, and create complex habitats that can double seabed biodiversity within a decade. The Dornoch project has inspired similar efforts across the UK, offering hope that patient, science-led restoration can reverse what seemed like permanent loss. It's a story about second chances — for ecosystems, for species, and for the possibility of repair.

This story offers a gentle look at how a singular vision can redefine artistic expression—and how one artist's joyful celebration of volume and form became a universal visual language that continues to lift spirits worldwide.

The Flinders Ranges became famous for these fossils when they were first discovered in 1946, and the site remains one of the world's most important windows into this distant era. The research contributes to an ongoing effort to secure UNESCO World Heritage status for the Flinders Ranges, recognizing the area's unique geological and biological significance. For anyone curious about our planet's deep past, this work offers a rare glimpse into a world so ancient it predates nearly every form of life we recognize today—a landscape that is, in the words of the researchers, perhaps the most alien environment we can still touch.

When artist Vernon Ah Kee reached out to photographer Hoda Afshar in 2023, he carried an urgent message: the mass incarceration of Indigenous youth in Far North Queensland was too overwhelming for his community to confront alone. The statistics are stark—Indigenous children aged 10 to 17 represent just 6 percent of the population but are 20 times more likely to be imprisoned. What emerged from that call is "Code Black/Riot," a photography and video project that hands creative control back to the young people whose stories are so often told by others. Afshar set up a large-format camera in the backyard of Youth Empowered Towards Independence (YETI) in Cairns and waited. For nine days, she returned to the same spot, allowing trust to build gradually. Eventually, formerly incarcerated teenagers began to engage, asking to help, to take pictures, to participate. Using props—fresh flowers, a handmade mask, Joker makeup, an outstretched arm from outside the frame—the young subjects shaped their own portraits while maintaining anonymity. In one image, a young man wraps himself in an Aboriginal flag, only one eye visible. In another, a youth holds a mask of Melpomene, the Greek symbol of tragedy, over his face. A companion video installation reveals harder truths: testimonies of physical assaults by guards, prolonged "night mode" lockdowns, and a 14-year-old who has been incarcerated five times. This project, now on display at Buxton Contemporary in Melbourne, is a quiet act of resistance. It reminds us that how we frame a story—what we include, what we leave out, who controls the camera—shapes everything. By letting these teenagers direct their own image, Afshar offers something rare: dignity, agency, and a chance to be seen on their own terms.

Japan’s <em>gyaru</em> crown their slang words of the year

While Japan's official New Words and Buzzwords of the Year award prepares to announce its 2026 winners in December, a parallel linguistic tradition offers a different window into contemporary Japanese culture. The magazine egg, a chronicler of gyaru culture since 1995, has released its own annual buzzword awards—and they capture something the mainstream list might miss: the evolving vocabulary of Japan's Gen Z. Gyaru culture emerged among young women, particularly in Tokyo's Shibuya neighborhood, bringing distinctive fashion, makeup, and attitudes. After egg suspended its print edition in 2014, it found new life online in 2018, building a fresh audience on social media platforms. The magazine's annual buzzword collection now serves as a living document of how this community communicates, with this year's November 1st announcement spanning topics from social dynamics within gyaru circles to the very modern struggle of doom-scrolling. This parallel awards tradition reminds us that language doesn't belong only to official arbiters or mainstream media. The words young people choose—the slang they invent, adopt, and discard—reveal shifts in culture that formal lists might overlook. For anyone curious about how language actually lives and breathes among communities, especially online, egg's gyaru buzzwords offer a quieter, more intimate portrait of contemporary Japan than any official award could provide.

Giant corpse flower 'Pete' set to bloom in regional Victoria

A towering plant named Pete is about to put on one of nature's most unusual shows at the Geelong Botanic Gardens in regional Victoria. Standing 1.5 meters tall, this corpse flower — formally known as Amorphophallus titanum — is preparing to bloom for the first time in years, an event that happens only once every seven to ten years and lasts just one to two days. The anticipation is building, and gardens staff are bracing for crowds after a similar flowering in 2024 drew over 20,000 visitors and hundreds of thousands of live-stream views. What makes Pete's bloom so remarkable is the paradox at its heart: breathtaking beauty paired with an unforgettable stench. The flower releases a smell likened to dead possums, rotting cheese, or fermenting cabbage — all to attract pollinators like flies and beetles that typically seek out decaying flesh. The plant's deep red coloring mimics blood, completing the ruse. Native to Indonesia, the corpse flower stores energy in an underground corm, and Pete's has ballooned from under 10 kilograms to nearly 18 kilograms in just over a year, signaling the plant had enough reserves to flower. After blooming, Pete will collapse and retreat underground for up to a decade. This is a story worth savoring for its reminder that nature operates on its own timeline, rewarding patience with spectacle. Whether visitors return for a second look or decide once was enough, Pete's impending bloom offers a rare chance to witness something genuinely strange and wonderful — a plant that has mastered the art of being beautifully repulsive.

Chiquinha Gonzaga: who was the maestrina author of 'Ó abre alas' who broke barriers in music and women's rights

Chiquinha Gonzaga: quem foi a maestrina autora de ‘Ó abre alas’ que rompeu barreiras na música e nos direitos para as mulheres

Chiquinha Gonzaga, born in Rio de Janeiro in 1847, was a groundbreaking figure in Brazilian music and women's rights whose legacy recently sparked controversy. She became the first woman to conduct an orchestra in Brazil, one of the country's first divorced women, and the composer of "Ó abre alas," the first marchinha created exclusively for Carnival. Her contributions helped establish quintessentially Brazilian musical genres like choro and maxixe. Born into a family that valued musical education, Chiquinha married at sixteen but faced a husband who forbade her musical pursuits. She made the radical decision to leave her marriage in 1877, facing condemnation from an ecclesiastical court for "abandonment of home and culpable adultery." Despite losing regular contact with her children and confronting severe social stigma, she built a professional career giving piano lessons, performing publicly, and publishing compositions—transforming what was considered acceptable domestic accomplishment into scandalous paid work. She also championed abolitionism and fought for artists' copyright protections. While historians find no evidence Chiquinha explicitly identified as a feminist or joined feminist organizations, scholars widely regard her life as pioneering the movement in Brazil. By claiming financial independence and occupying professional spaces reserved for men, she challenged the rigid constraints on women's autonomy in nineteenth-century Brazil. Her story matters because it illuminates how individual acts of defiance can quietly reshape what seems possible, and how one woman's insistence on living and working on her own terms helped compose a new social reality alongside her musical innovations. She died in 1935 at age 87, leaving a body of work that continues to resonate across generations.

Taiwan’s tea growers adapt to climate change by letting nature take control

High in Taiwan's Alishan mountains, where some of the world's most prized oolong tea is grown, farmers are facing a sobering reality: climate change is reshaping their craft. Over the past decade, tea production in the region has dropped by 13.5% as temperatures rise, spring droughts intensify, and protective mountain mists fade. But rather than turning to more intensive agricultural methods, a small group of growers is doing something counterintuitive—they're stepping back and letting nature lead. Sixth-generation farmer Lin Kuang-li abandoned pesticides three decades ago, replacing chemicals with green manure made from alfalfa and using pruned branches to protect the soil. His crops, he says, are more resilient because they've learned to withstand stress. Meanwhile, tea growers Chang Chih-tsung and Yeh Hsin-wen practice an even more hands-off approach called yefang, or wild cultivation. They don't prune, water, or fertilize—allowing their Jhin-hsüan oolong bushes to grow freely under forest canopy. The result? Deep-rooted plants that survived recent droughts while neighboring conventional farms lost entire crops. The trade-off is steep: these growers harvest far less tea and must find supplemental income, embracing what researchers call a model of "degrowth." This story is a quiet testament to adaptation born not from technology, but from trust in ecological rhythms. It's a reminder that sometimes the most forward-thinking response to crisis is to work with the land rather than against it—even when doing so means producing less, earning less, and redefining success on nature's terms.

Vietnam’s freshwater obsession leaves decades-long scars for Mekong Delta farmers

In southern Vietnam, a massive sluice gate built more than two decades ago to protect rice farmers from saltwater has instead created a cascade of unintended problems. The Ba Lai sluice, constructed in 2002 to convert the Ba Lai River into a year-round freshwater reservoir, was designed to shield nearly 200,000 hectares of farmland from saline intrusion. It represented a broader post-1975 national policy that viewed saltwater as an enemy and prioritized rice cultivation above all else. Today, the river has become a stagnant lake, trapping agricultural runoff laden with pesticides and herbicides. Shrimp farmers downstream time their harvests around the sluice's discharge schedule to avoid pollution that could wipe out their stock. Meanwhile, upstream rice farmers—facing low incomes—have begun drilling illegal saltwater wells to cultivate more profitable shrimp, prompting crackdowns from local authorities. The infrastructure has also worsened coastal erosion and failed to stop saline intrusion as intended. The Ba Lai River, once one of the Mekong's "Nine Dragons," now suffers from the very infrastructure meant to protect it. This story offers a quietly powerful illustration of how well-intentioned solutions can unravel when they ignore the complexity of natural systems. It reveals the human cost of rigid policy—farmers caught between outdated mandates and economic reality—and hints at the difficult work of shifting from infrastructure-driven thinking to approaches that work with, rather than against, the rhythms of rivers and tides.

Park with 'stone forest' formed over 180 million years hosts open-air exhibition transforming roots into art

Parque com ‘floresta de pedras’ formada em 180 milhões de anos recebe exposição a céu aberto que transforma raízes em arte

A geological wonder millions of years in the making is hosting a new conversation between nature and art. Parque Vila Velha, located in Brazil's Paraná state, is known for its "stone forest"—dramatic sandstone formations sculpted by wind and water over 180 million years. Now, the park is home to "Impressions of the Earth," an open-air exhibition featuring eight monumental sculptures by artist Hugo França, crafted from the roots of native Brazilian trees including oiticica, pequi, and jacarandá. França's approach is deeply collaborative with his materials. Rather than imposing a predetermined design, he works with the natural contours, textures, and histories already present in each piece of wood—roots salvaged from trees felled by storms or urban development. His process mirrors the patient work of erosion visible in the park's ancient rock towers: both artist and elements respond to what's already there, revealing forms rather than forcing them. The exhibition, curated by Marc Pottier and organized by the Oscar Niemeyer Museum as part of its "Museum Without Walls" initiative, runs through March 2027 and invites visitors to consider the parallel stories told by geological time and living wood. This pairing of art and landscape asks quieter questions about waste and value—how society decides what materials are worth keeping, and what gets discarded. By placing works made from "condemned" trees alongside rocks shaped across eons, the exhibition offers a chance to reconsider the lifespans of natural things and the marks time leaves behind. It's a thoughtful reminder that transformation, whether by human hands or natural forces, can reveal unexpected beauty.
