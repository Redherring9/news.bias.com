Perseid meteor shower reaches peak: When and where to catch a glimpse

The Perseids meteor shower, widely considered the best annual celestial display, reaches its peak this week, offering skywatchers a chance to see up to 100 shooting stars per hour. This year brings an unusual bonus: a deep partial solar eclipse visible in the UK will occur just hours before the meteor shower begins, creating what astronomer Dr. Becky Smethurst calls "the perfect cosmic coincidence of events." The Perseids are caused by debris from the Swift-Tuttle comet, a massive object with a 16-mile solid core that takes 133 years to orbit the Sun, meaning some of the burning fragments may have been left behind more than a century ago. While the Perseids are active from mid-July through late August, the prime viewing window falls in the early morning hours of August 12-13, between midnight and dawn. This year offers especially favorable conditions due to the new moon, which means no competing moonlight to wash out fainter meteors. For the best experience, observers should seek locations away from city lights, allow their eyes 20-30 minutes to adjust to darkness, and look toward the widest possible expanse of sky. England and Wales are expected to have clear skies, while Scotland and Northern Ireland may contend with more cloud cover. This story offers a gentle reminder to look up and reconnect with the cosmos. The Perseids provide a rare chance to witness ancient debris burning up in real time, connecting us to celestial events that unfold on timescales far beyond human lifespans—what Smethurst aptly describes as "the Universe's own cosmic clock."

Yarning circles raise concerns about repetition of Stolen Generations

In South Australia, nearly 40 percent of children in out-of-home care are Aboriginal, despite Aboriginal people making up a small fraction of the population. Carers and community members are raising alarm that this disproportionate representation echoes the trauma of the Stolen Generations, particularly as many children are losing connection to their culture, language, and land. South Australia has the second-highest rate of children in out-of-home care nationally, and Aboriginal children there are placed in care at a rate significantly higher than the national average. An Aboriginal-led organization called KWY has launched a four-year research project, Wilto Willo—meaning "spring star" in the Kaurna language—to gather community voices and develop solutions. Through yarning circles in Port Lincoln, Port Augusta, and Adelaide, about 200 carers have shared their experiences. This story matters because it highlights an urgent question: despite policies aimed at closing the gap and protecting Aboriginal children's rights, why are the numbers worsening? Dogs can distinguish between fearful and angry or sad human faces, brain scans suggest

Dogs may be more attuned to human emotions than previously understood. New research using brain imaging has revealed that canine brains can distinguish not only between happy and unhappy human faces, but also between different types of negative expressions—a ability researchers say represents the first demonstration that dogs can differentiate emotions of the same emotional valence. In the study, researchers at the University of Vienna trained pet dogs to lie still in MRI scanners while viewing photographs of unfamiliar human faces displaying various emotions. Initial experiments showed increased brain activity in areas associated with visual processing, social cognition, and reward when dogs viewed happy faces compared to neutral ones. A second experiment involving twelve dogs and four different expressions—happiness, sadness, fear, and anger—revealed something more nuanced: while certain brain regions could distinguish happy from any negative expression, distinguishing between different unhappy faces required analyzing activity across the entire brain. The dogs' brains could tell fearful faces apart from angry or sad ones, though anger and sadness proved harder to differentiate from each other. Researchers suggest this may reflect how different negative expressions signal different types of threats, and that dogs might need additional cues like body language to make finer distinctions. This research offers a window into how thousands of years of domestication have shaped dogs' remarkable sensitivity to human communication. Beyond satisfying curiosity about our companions' inner lives, the findings suggest practical applications—such as using positive facial expressions to enhance training—and remind us that the bond between humans and dogs runs deeper than we might see on the surface.

The whale, designated B-964, was first identified in August 2023 swimming with her calf near the beaches of Garopaba and Imbituba. Her distinctive gray skin marked with black patches makes her unmistakable among her species. Semi-albinism is exceptionally rare in southern right whales, occurring in only about 3% of individuals worldwide. Of the 38 semi-albino whales documented in Brazil's photo-identification catalog over nearly four decades, B-964 is the sole female. The coloration results from a recessive genetic variation—both parents must carry the gene for a semi-albino calf to be born. These whales are born white and gradually develop their characteristic gray coloring as they mature. When B-964 was spotted again in July 2024, she was swimming alone in waters known as a natural nursery for the species. Researchers suspect she may have returned to give birth, as southern right whales typically bear calves every three years. This story offers a quiet window into the careful, patient work of marine conservation. The researchers' hope to spot B-964 with a new calf reflects decades of dedication to understanding individual whales through distinctive physical markers like head callosities and body patterns. Over the following years, this enslaved man would become the de facto leader of the small group and accomplish one of history's most remarkable survival journeys—yet his true name remains unknown to us. He later undertook an independent 2,400-kilometer journey northward, becoming the first known non-Native person to explore present-day New Mexico and Arizona. Despite being forbidden to travel to the New World as a Muslim, Estevanico pioneered routes and generated geographical knowledge that later Spanish explorers would use. This story matters because it challenges familiar narratives about who first explored the American West. His extraordinary journey reminds us that history's most significant pathfinders don't always appear in the expected chapters of our textbooks—and that courage and resilience can emerge from the most unlikely circumstances.
