Floating nature islands installed in parks as Glasgow Games legacy

Glasgow is weaving environmental care into the fabric of its 2026 Commonwealth Games, installing floating nature islands on ponds across five city parks. These buoyant platforms, crafted by a Scottish company that has exported similar designs worldwide, offer a clever blend of habitat creation and water purification. Community groups and local schools are helping plant the islands with native aquatic species like marsh marigolds and water forget-me-nots, whose roots dangle beneath the surface to filter the water and shelter aquatic life. The islands themselves are built from recycled materials—repurposed plastic pipes provide flotation, topped with lightweight clay into which plants are set. Together, they create nearly 250 square meters of new wildlife habitat expected to thrive for about two decades. Birds gain safe nesting spots in the middle of the water, free from most disturbances, while the plants' roots work quietly to reduce harmful algae and gradually improve water clarity. The project reflects a broader commitment by Games organizers to sustainability, from renewable energy at competition venues to a largely electric transport fleet. This story is a reminder that major sporting events can leave behind more than memories and infrastructure—they can nurture the natural world. By funding a project the city wanted but couldn't afford, the Commonwealth Games are helping Glasgow's green spaces flourish long after the medals are awarded. It's a quiet, hopeful example of how celebration and stewardship can float together.

Hong Kong becomes key link in illegal trade of Australia’s native reptiles

Hong Kong has become a central hub in the illegal trafficking of Australian native reptiles, with customs authorities intercepting four major shipments between 2025 and 2026. These seizures recovered at least 98 animals—including 74 lizards and 24 snakes—valued at over $141,000. In one particularly creative attempt, smugglers concealed 13 live lizards inside winter boots falsely declared as snacks. Researchers at Adelaide University's Wildlife Crime Research Hub confirm that Hong Kong serves both as a destination and transit point for these smuggling networks. The trade thrives on a regulatory mismatch: while Australia strictly controls native reptile ownership and has banned commercial exports since 1982, Hong Kong and mainland China require no proof of legal origin for possession. This gap allows illegally obtained wildlife to be laundered into legitimate trade channels. Demand surged during COVID-19 lockdowns, when reptiles became popular as low-maintenance pets. Chinese buyers are particularly drawn to distinctive species like the shingleback lizard—nicknamed the "pine cone lizard"—which can fetch $3,000 or more. These desirable animals are difficult to breed in captivity, sustaining high black-market prices. Organized crime syndicates have increasingly turned to recruiting international students as smugglers, with at least one Chinese student receiving an 18-month jail sentence last year. This story reveals how global wildlife trafficking adapts to regulatory loopholes and consumer trends, transforming rare creatures into commodities that move through the same channels as counterfeit goods. Despite Hong Kong adding wildlife trafficking to its serious crimes ordinance in 2021, enforcement experts say penalties remain too light to dismantle the networks, leaving smugglers to view prison time as merely a business expense in a lucrative trade.

With Uenf and Uerj among the pioneers, Brazil's first Racial Quota Law turns 25

Com Uenf e Uerj entre as pioneiras, primeira Lei de Cotas Raciais do Brasil completa 25 anos

Brazil's first racial quota law is marking its 25th anniversary this November, a milestone that transformed access to higher education in a country still grappling with the legacy of its colonial past. Enacted in Rio de Janeiro state on November 9, 2001, the pioneering legislation reserved 40% of spots at two public universities for students who self-identify as Black or mixed-race. What began as a state initiative became national policy in 2012, extending affirmative action to all federal universities and institutes across Brazil. The quota system was designed to correct historical inequities in university admissions, according to researchers. In a nation where education is a constitutional right but university seats are limited, traditional entrance exams had effectively excluded many students from poor and marginalized backgrounds. The policy has since evolved to include Indigenous students, quilombola community members, and people with disabilities. For students like Raissa Pequeno, a quota beneficiary studying Public Administration at UENF in Campos dos Goytacazes, the system has been life-changing—not just in gaining admission, but through financial aid that allows her to actually stay enrolled and live near campus. Yet experts and students alike emphasize that the work is far from complete. A quarter-century on, the focus has shifted from simply opening university doors to ensuring students can walk through them and reach graduation. The story is a reminder that addressing systemic inequality requires not just policy, but sustained support—and that sometimes the most meaningful change happens one student, one scholarship, one completed degree at a time.

Three months after Nepal disaster, worshippers return to lost temple

Three months after a catastrophic flood swept through Nepal's northern valleys, worshippers have returned to the site of a 1,500-year-old Hindu temple that was completely destroyed. The temple of Jalpa Devi in Devighat, along with homes, schools, and bridges, was lost when a massive piece of rock and glacier broke off a mountain and cascaded more than a kilometre down into the Trishuli River system. Where the sacred structure once stood, four branches now mark a makeshift shrine where devotees gather to pray, light incense, and honour those who died in the disaster. The community has used satellite imagery to locate the temple's exact position, hoping to recover sacred relics buried beneath tonnes of rock and silt. Among the lost treasures is a stone believed to mark the cremation site of Nepal's founding king from 1775. Survivors like Bharatgai Chitrakar describe the moments before the flood arrived, recalling the disbelief that turned to terror as the wall of water approached. The disaster's toll continues to mount: 1,400 bodies found, 5,000 people missing, and nearly 100,000 affected, with recovery costs in the billions. While international aid has arrived, much comes as loans rather than grants, sparking debate about who should bear the financial burden when climate disasters strike vulnerable mountain communities. This story captures both profound loss and quiet resilience—a community returning to sacred ground not with grand structures but with simple rituals, keeping faith alive amid rubble. It's a reminder that some of what disasters take cannot be rebuilt, only remembered and honoured in new ways.

NZ's biggest waka festival aims to pass on traditional voyaging knowledge

In Northland's Doubtless Bay, New Zealand is hosting its largest gathering of ocean-going canoes in centuries. Te Hau Kōmaru, a biennial festival, brings together six waka from around New Zealand, two double-hulled voyaging canoes from Hawaii, and one from Vanuatu, along with voyagers from Tahiti, Samoa, and Rarotonga. More than 2,000 people, including roughly 1,000 children from Far North schools, are expected to attend the week-long event focused on passing traditional Polynesian navigation and canoe-building skills to a new generation. The festival's location at Aurere holds special significance as the home of the late Sir Hekenukumai Busby, who built the Kupe Waka Centre and the voyaging canoe Te Aurere in the early 1990s. The revival of ocean voyaging in Aotearoa traces back to 1985, when the Hawaiian waka Hōkūle'a arrived at Waitangi, prompting elder Sir James Henare to challenge Māori to build their own voyaging canoes. Sir Hekenukumai answered that call, sailing to Rarotonga in 1992—the first of many journeys across the Polynesian Triangle. Several sailors from those original voyages are participating in this year's festival, including Stan Conrad, the only Māori aboard when Hōkūle'a landed nearly 40 years ago. This gathering represents more than maritime tradition—it's about identity, connection, and understanding how Pacific peoples navigated vast ocean distances using stars, currents, and environmental signs. As one participant notes, these canoes have become "floating schools," offering a tangible way to reconnect with ancestral knowledge and remember that Polynesian settlement was not accidental but guided by deep environmental wisdom. The story is a quiet reminder of how cultural revival can flow across generations and ocean waters alike.

The little-known Mexican origin of the turkey eaten at Thanksgiving in the United States

El poco conocido origen mexicano del pavo que se come en Acción de Gracias en Estados Unidos

Every Thanksgiving, millions of Americans gather around tables centered on a roasted turkey, but few realize this iconic bird traces its roots back over 2,000 years to central Mexico. The Meleagris gallopavo mexicana, the wild ancestor of today's domesticated turkey, was first raised in Mesoamerica, the region stretching from central Mexico to northern Costa Rica. Archaeologists have found evidence that the bird was especially established around Teotihuacan between 300 and 900 CE, where it held both practical and sacred significance. The Mexica people called it huexolotl in Náhuatl—a name combining huey (great) and Xólotl, a deity who, according to pre-Hispanic legend, transformed into a turkey to escape sacrifice. The bird played an important role in agricultural rituals and festive meals, a tradition that continues in Mexico today, where turkey is served at Christmas dinners and weddings, often prepared with mole sauce. When Spanish colonizers arrived in the 16th century, they introduced the bird to Europe, from where it spread globally. Curiously, the English called it "turkey" because traders brought it via Turkish ports, while in Turkey itself, it was called "hindi," mistakenly linking it to India—a nod to Columbus's original destination. This story offers a quiet reminder of how deeply interconnected our traditions are, and how a bird domesticated by ancient Mesoamerican peoples became the centerpiece of a quintessentially American holiday. It's a tale of cultural exchange, culinary history, and the often-overlooked origins of what we place on our tables.

What images reveal from the asteroid deliberately struck by NASA to test Earth's defenses

O que revelam imagens do asteroide atingido deliberadamente pela Nasa para testar defesas da Terra

In 2022, NASA deliberately crashed a spacecraft into an asteroid called Dimorphos at 22,000 kilometers per hour, creating a debris trail 10,000 kilometers long visible from Earth. It wasn't a matter of planetary defense against an actual threat—Dimorphos posed no danger—but rather a rehearsal, a test of humanity's ability to nudge a space rock off course should one ever threaten our planet. Now, four years later, the European Space Agency's Hera mission has returned to the scene with fresh images, revealing a stadium-sized crater on the tiny asteroid's surface. The impact did more than leave a mark. Before the collision, Dimorphos took 11 hours and 55 minutes to orbit its larger companion, Didymos. Afterward, that orbit shortened by 33 minutes—proof that redirecting an asteroid is possible. But Hera's images show the collision also set Dimorphos tumbling chaotically through space, no longer showing the same face to Didymos like our Moon does to Earth. Scientists compare it to a barrel rolling down stairs. Meanwhile, detailed photos of Didymos reveal car- and bus-sized boulders scattered across a surface with gravity 40,000 times weaker than Earth's. This story matters because it's a quiet rehearsal for a scenario we hope never comes. By measuring Dimorphos's mass and studying the crater in detail, researchers are building a playbook—calculating exactly what size spacecraft, traveling at what speed, would be needed to deflect a real threat. It's planetary defense made tangible, and a reminder that sometimes the most meaningful scientific work happens not in crisis, but in careful preparation.

New breed of black sheep has hair instead of wool
