'A proud moment for Ngāti Rehua': New Zealand's longest lizard returns to Aotea Great Barrier Island

New Zealand's longest lizard, the chevron skink—known in te reo Māori as niho taniwha—has returned to its ancestral home on Aotea Great Barrier Island after three decades. Eleven of these threatened reptiles, which can grow up to 30 centimeters long, were recently released into Glenfern Sanctuary, joining ten others released in July. The skinks originated from three injured individuals brought to Auckland Zoo for rehabilitation between 1997 and 1999, where they successfully bred in captivity to create a population of twenty-one. The homecoming represents far more than a wildlife relocation. For Ngāti Rehua, the local iwi, it's about reconnecting a precious taonga with its whakapapa and whenua—restoring not just a species but a relationship between people, place, and biodiversity. The skinks have been fitted with small transmitters and acclimatized to custom bamboo refuges, allowing researchers to track their movements and learn about these secretive creatures as they adjust to forest life. The release is part of broader conservation efforts on the island, including predator control programs targeting feral cats and other introduced threats that have long imperiled native species. This story quietly embodies what thoughtful conservation can look like: a collaboration between indigenous knowledge holders, government agencies, zoos, and local communities working in concert. It's a reminder that protecting biodiversity isn't just about preserving animals in isolation, but about restoring connections—between species and their habitats, between people and their ancestral landscapes, and between past and future generations who will inherit these living treasures.

This story offers a quiet lesson in how individual choices can ripple across generations. Seeds grown in simulated atmosphere secured in Arctic Circle doomsday vault

The genebank's selection criteria balance what matters to New Zealand's agricultural heritage with what might prove valuable to the world, particularly as climate patterns continue to shift.
