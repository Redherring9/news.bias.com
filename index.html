First Nations lead the return of salmon to Canada’s Upper Columbia River

In the shadow of the Rocky Mountains, students and community members are gathering along the Columbia River to release tiny salmon fry into waters that haven't seen their kind return in over eight decades. Led by First Nations salmon chief Mark Thomas, these ceremonies blend traditional practices with modern conservation efforts, reconnecting Indigenous peoples with fish that once sustained their ancestors for millennia. The Columbia River was once among North America's most prolific salmon-bearing waterways, its 2,000-kilometer journey from British Columbia to the Pacific Ocean teeming with fish that formed the backbone of Indigenous food systems and culture. That changed in 1938 when the Grand Coulee Dam was built without fish passage mechanisms, severing salmon access to more than half the river and cutting off spawning grounds throughout the Upper Columbia Basin. The loss rippled through Indigenous communities, eroding both food sovereignty and cultural connections that had endured for thousands of years. Now, the Bringing the Salmon Home initiative—a partnership among the Secwépemc, Syilx-Okanagan, and Ktunaxa nations alongside Canadian and British Columbia governments—is working to reverse that loss. Through programs like Fish-in-Schools, where students raise salmon from eggs in their classrooms before releasing them, and ceremonial releases that honor traditional practices, communities are rebuilding relationships with salmon. Technical studies confirm that reintroduction is feasible, offering hope that these ancient connections can be restored. This story matters because it represents more than ecological restoration—it's about healing cultural wounds and honoring the patience and determination of communities who never forgot the salmon, even when the salmon could no longer come home.

This recipe is worth attention not for complexity but for what it represents—the resourcefulness of Pantanal cooking, where preserved meats and humble ingredients transform into something comforting and communal. The Great Lakes’ most emblematic fish could be consigned to history books

The lake whitefish, once so abundant in the Great Lakes that it made Sandusky, Ohio, the world's largest freshwater fish market in the 1880s, is now facing a precipitous decline that could see it disappear from parts of the region within five years. For centuries, Indigenous communities built their lives around this fish, and settler towns later grew prosperous from its popularity. But beginning in the 1990s, something started going wrong—fewer young fish were making it to adulthood, and researchers began searching for answers. The culprit turned out to be an ecological chain reaction triggered by invasive zebra and quagga mussels, which arrived in the Great Lakes via transatlantic shipping vessels. These mussels consume the phytoplankton that feed diporeia, small shrimp-like crustaceans that young whitefish depend on after hatching. Without this crucial food source, fingerlings starve before they can grow large enough to shift to other prey. The mussels now blanket lakebeds for hundreds of miles, and while they've helped clean the water of agricultural and industrial pollutants, their overwhelming numbers have devastated the food web. Commercial harvests have collapsed—from 200,000 pounds in Ohio alone in 2009 to just 185,000 pounds across all of Lake Erie last year. This story matters because it illustrates how a single invasive species can unravel an entire ecosystem and the human communities that depend on it. For fishers like William Ortiz of the Little Traverse Bay Bands of Odawa Indians, the whitefish decline represents not just lost income but the erosion of treaty rights and traditional ways of life. Scientists are now racing to find solutions, including exploring whether native freshwater drum fish might help control mussel populations, offering a glimmer of hope in an otherwise sobering ecological transformation.

The solitary nature of this birth—the mother appeared to be alone rather than escorted by other whales—also offers new data for comparison with other documented cases. This story matters because it reminds us how much remains unknown about even the most watched creatures on Earth. New exhibition tracing evolution of Māori carving over 700 years

Te Papa, New Zealand's national museum, is preparing to open a sweeping exhibition that traces seven centuries of Māori carving. He Toi Whakairo: The Art of Māori Carving brings together 147 taonga—treasured objects—ranging from pieces created in the 1300s to contemporary works commissioned specifically for the show, which opens on August 29. Co-curators Jana Pātete and Dougal Austin have deliberately chosen not to arrange the exhibition chronologically. Instead, they've placed contemporary and traditional carvings side by side to emphasize that whakairo is a living, evolving practice. The earliest pieces reveal Pasifika influences tracing back to Māori origins across Te Moana Nui a Kiwa (the Pacific Ocean), while later works show how the art form developed its own distinct identity. Among the highlights are pieces unseen in perhaps a century, contemporary clay work by Stevie Houkāmau, and karetao (marionettes) by Ngaroma Riley, including a self-portrait puppet called Kapahaka Queen that swings poi. Austin expressed particular fondness for a hei tiki named after Ruatepupuke, the figure from Māori storytelling who is said to have brought carving knowledge from the ocean depths. Pātete pointed to a tokotoko (walking stick) that appears to be a collaboration between carver and nature, shaped as it grew around a vine. The exhibition offers more than aesthetic appreciation—it's a window into cultural continuity and innovation across generations. By showcasing agricultural tools, weapons, adornment, and puppets alongside each other, and by highlighting the work of women carvers, the show reveals both the diversity of the craft and its enduring vitality in contemporary Māori culture.

Three-way deathmatch unearthed at 100-million-year-old 'crime scene'

A remarkable fossil discovery in outback Queensland has preserved a prehistoric drama that played out 100 million years ago in what was once the Eromanga Sea. The find captures a rare "three-tier predator interaction": an ichthyosaur (a dolphin-like marine reptile) that had eaten a pterosaur (a flying reptile) was itself attacked and killed by a massive kronosaurus, an ocean predator up to 14 metres long with more than 100 razor-sharp teeth. Travellers first stumbled upon the ichthyosaur remains in 2019, affectionately nicknamed "BOB" for "bag of bones." Three years later, closer examination revealed the extraordinary nature of the find. The ichthyosaur's midsection bore more than 30 bite marks—possibly the most ever recorded in a marine fossil—and its body had been violently shattered by the kronosaurus. Even more remarkable, preserved stomach contents showed pterosaur remains, marking the first documented case of an ichthyosaur having consumed one of these flying reptiles. The combination of predator, prey, and scavenger preserved together is exceptionally rare in the fossil record. While the precise sequence of events remains a mystery—did the kronosaurus leap from the water to attack, or was the ichthyosaur breaching to catch the pterosaur mid-flight?—the fossil offers an unprecedented window into ancient behaviour. As one palaeontologist noted, it's one of the clearest examples of three-tier predator interaction ever discovered. This "crime scene" from deep time reminds us that the drama of life and death has always been part of nature's story, and that sometimes, against all odds, those moments get preserved for us to puzzle over millions of years later.

Mining, drilling, and fire are fragmenting the world’s largest forests

The world's largest unbroken forests are disappearing at an accelerating pace, and the causes are shifting in unexpected ways. According to new research from the World Resources Institute, these "intact forest landscapes"—vast areas showing no signs of industrial activity—have shrunk by more than 15% since 2000, losing an area roughly the size of Mexico. Canada, Russia, and Brazil hold nearly two-thirds of what remains, and they've also seen the steepest declines. What's particularly striking is how fire has surged as a driver of loss, now accounting for nearly 40% of the reduction since 2020, up dramatically from 21% in earlier years. Meanwhile, logging has dropped to about one-fifth of recent losses. But perhaps most revealing is the rising impact of mining, drilling, and exploration, now the third-largest cause at nearly 19%. This figure doesn't primarily reflect direct land clearing—mines and oil wells themselves occupy relatively small footprints. Instead, it captures fragmentation: a single road, pipeline, or exploration corridor can slice a massive forest into smaller pieces, disqualifying them as "intact" even though much of the canopy remains. This story matters because it reveals how industrial infrastructure quietly unravels wilderness in ways satellite images of clear-cut land don't always capture. Once roads penetrate remote forests, they often invite logging, settlement, and additional development—cascading effects that extend far beyond the original project. The challenge isn't just protecting trees, but preserving the scale and continuity that make these forests ecologically irreplaceable. It's a reminder that intactness, once lost to fragmentation, is nearly impossible to restore.

English gymnast Gabriel Langton was released from hospital after a frightening fall during the Commonwealth Games men's team gymnastics final in Glasgow. The former Japan international has stated he won't retire until reaching 60, and his presence remains a significant draw for fans both in Japan and internationally. Between parents, children and grandchildren, congada keeps tradition alive for 246 years in southern Minas Gerais

Entre pais, filhos e netos, congada mantém tradição viva há 246 anos no Sul de Minas

In the small town of Silvianópolis in southern Minas Gerais, Brazil, a vibrant Afro-Brazilian tradition has been passed down through families for nearly two and a half centuries. The Festa de Nossa Senhora do Rosário, first celebrated in 1780, centers on the congada—a cultural expression combining music, dance, and spiritual devotion. Today, the Terno de Congada Nossa Senhora do Rosário brings together more than 80 participants spanning four generations, from young children to elders, all helping to keep this living tradition alive. At the heart of this continuity is Marco Aurélio Valentim, the group's captain, who has participated in the festivities for 52 of his 56 years. Raised by congadeiro parents, he now watches his own daughters and grandchildren follow the same path. "We are born into the congado," he explains, describing how the tradition flows naturally from ancestors to descendants. In 2026, the group received national recognition when Brazil's Ministry of Culture certified it as a Point of Culture, officially registering it within the Cultural Heritage of Kingdoms and Congados of Minas Gerais. This certification connects the group to a nationwide network, opening doors to cultural exchanges and collaborative programming. What makes this story quietly remarkable is its testament to cultural resilience and intergenerational commitment. The congada is not merely a performance but a year-round practice of preserving Afro-Brazilian history, musical traditions, and collective memory. In an era when many traditions fade, this community's dedication—spanning 246 years and counting—offers a gentle reminder of how culture survives: through families who choose, generation after generation, to carry it forward.

Christchurch Catholic Diocese unveils plans for new $80m Cathedral of the Blessed Sacrament

Fourteen years after earthquakes left it beyond repair, Christchurch's Catholic community has unveiled designs for a new Cathedral of the Blessed Sacrament. The $80 million project will return to the Barbadoes Street site where the original 1905 cathedral stood before its demolition in 2020. Drawing on Romanesque and Gothic architectural traditions, the new design by Warren and Mahoney aims to honor centuries of ecclesiastical building while serving contemporary needs. The complex will include an 800-seat cathedral, a perpetual adoration chapel, and a community center. The financing reflects both pragmatism and community participation. About $45 million will come from insurance payouts and asset sales, while the remaining $35 million is being raised through donations—of which $19 million has already been secured. Bishop Michael Gielen has called on Catholic families across Canterbury and the West Coast to contribute, echoing the generational effort that funded the original cathedral. Lead architect Joseph Hampton, whose ancestor contributed to that first building, describes the project as "grounded in tradition but built for Christchurch today." This story offers a quiet window into how communities rebuild not just structures, but symbols of continuity after disaster. It will be New Zealand's first new Catholic cathedral in 120 years, and construction will begin once half of the remaining $16 million is raised. For a city still healing from seismic trauma, the cathedral represents both an act of faith and a tangible commitment to future generations—a beacon meant to endure long after those funding it are gone.

Moacir Santos, maestro who would turn 100, conducted Brazil's music with black gold extracted from the sertão

Moacir Santos, maestro que faria 100 anos, regeu a música do Brasil com o ouro negro extraído a partir do sertão

A Brazilian composer, arranger, and maestro who would have turned 100 this July, Moacir Santos remains a quietly towering figure in music—revered by fellow musicians yet largely unknown to the general public. Born in 1926 in Flores, a small town in the semi-arid sertão of Pernambuco, Santos began his musical journey with local philharmonic bands before moving to Rio de Janeiro in 1948, during the golden age of radio. He eventually settled in the United States, where he lived until his death in Pasadena in 2006. Santos is best known for his 1965 album "Coisas," considered a cornerstone of Brazilian music for its innovative fusion of Afro-Brazilian rhythms with jazz and classical rigor. Trained by classical masters yet also a teacher to legends like Baden Powell and João Donato, Santos wove together baião, maracatu, samba, and jazz into a sound both sophisticated and deeply rooted. Poet Vinicius de Moraes honored him in song, noting that Santos "is not just one, but many"—a tribute to his ability to blend diverse musical traditions with grace and invention. This story reminds us that some artists shape the course of their art form without commanding the spotlight. Santos extracted what admirers call "black gold" from the rich soil of Brazilian music, building bridges between folklore and improvisation, Africa and the Americas, popular and classical. His centenary offers a chance to rediscover a maestro whose legacy lives on in the work of countless musicians, even if his name remains unfamiliar to many. It's a portrait of genius that thrived in collaboration, fusion, and quiet mastery.

The notorious Cameroonian prison with its own record label

Inside New Bell, a notoriously overcrowded prison in Douala, Cameroon, inmates have created something unexpected: their own music studio and record label. Jail Time Records emerged from the facility's unusually autonomous internal culture, where prisoners run their own messenger services, market stalls, and informal systems of order. What began as a freestyle rap session during a dance class in 2018 has evolved into a creative outlet that has given incarcerated musicians audiences stretching from South Africa to Argentina, with one artist amassing tens of thousands of YouTube views. The studio itself is modest—a small recording booth and a single computer—but it has become a space of profound expression for prisoners serving lengthy sentences, many of whom maintain their innocence. Former inmate Steve Happi, who co-founded the label with Italian artist Dione Roach, describes the music as "an instrument of resistance" that allows inmates to transcend their physical confinement. The project gained international recognition when a documentary about the studio, filmed over six years, won three prizes at New York's Tribeca Film Festival, including best documentary feature. Executive producers included Oscar-winning director Taika Waititi and singer Rita Ora. This story quietly challenges assumptions about creativity, dignity, and rehabilitation. A symbol of summer, this Japanese dessert brings sweet relief

In the long and storied tradition of Japanese sweets that stretches back more than two millennia, anmitsu is a relative newcomer—but one that has captured hearts and taste buds for nearly a century. This beloved summer dessert was born in 1930 at Wakamatsu, a wagashi shop in Tokyo's Ginza district, when second-generation owner Hanjiro Mori listened to his customers' requests for something sweeter than his existing offerings. Mori's innovation was elegantly simple: he took his existing mitsumame—a refreshing mix of cubes of kanten (agar jelly), soft glutinous rice dumplings, boiled red peas, and brown sugar syrup—and added a generous serving of anko, the smooth adzuki bean paste central to so many Japanese confections. The result was a dessert that balanced textures and flavors in a way that resonated with sweet-seekers of the early Showa Era and continues to offer cool relief on warm days. This story reminds us that culinary tradition is always evolving, and that sometimes the most enduring creations come from simply listening to what people want. Anmitsu may be young compared to ancient treats like dango and mochi, but in less than a century it has earned its place as a symbol of Japanese summer—a testament to how a thoughtful addition can transform the familiar into something new and lasting.

Why these Sydney neighbours built a backyard wildlife corridor

In the forested hills of Sydney's north-west fringe, a group of neighbors has quietly created something remarkable: a 40-hectare wildlife corridor linking two national parks. Led by residents like Lesley Hulbert and Polly Bennett, the effort grew from a mix of love for the land and concern over what development might take away. Hulbert recalls the eerie silence that followed the devastating 2019–2020 bushfires, when the gang-gang cockatoos, koalas, and wallabies that once frequented her backyard seemed to vanish. In recent years, trail cameras have captured their gradual return—possums, wallabies, signs of life reclaiming the forest. The corridor, formalized through a Land for Wildlife program, involved painstaking work: identifying native plants, removing invasive species, and committing to long-term conservation. Bennett's property alone contributes 30 hectares of Sydney turpentine ironbark forest, a rare and precious ecosystem. But the neighbors' efforts exist in tension with the pressures of urban expansion. Nearby, a 40-hectare residential development recently cleared habitat trees based on a 2010 assessment that found no koalas—despite local knowledge that koalas have long lived there. Another proposal, a 126-hectare tourism project approved decades ago, looms on the horizon. This story is worth attention because it captures a grassroots response to a challenge facing cities everywhere: how to make room for both people and the wild creatures that share the land. It's a quiet act of stewardship, born not from policy but from neighbors who decided their backyards could become bridges for the animals coming home.

Fifty years of weather records help farmers plan ahead

In the sun-scorched reaches of western Queensland, a careful analysis of half a century's worth of weather data is offering farmers new ways to work with a shifting climate. Scientists at the University of Southern Queensland examined 50 years of temperature, rainfall, and heat records from eight towns across the region, uncovering patterns that are quietly reshaping how livestock producers plan their year. The findings reveal warmer summers stretching into what were once cooler months, fewer cool nights, and notably hotter conditions in the shoulder seasons of autumn and spring. For wool producer Peter Thomas and his wife Kimble, who manage 12,000 merino sheep near Longreach, these changes prompted a rethink of their breeding calendar. After noticing poor lamb survival during May lambing—when pregnant ewes endured the hottest months—they shifted their schedule so lambs arrive in August instead, allowing pregnancies to unfold during cooler weather. The result: a 20 percent improvement in lamb survival rates, enough to warrant continuing the experiment. What makes this story quietly remarkable is its practicality. Climate trend analysis is being translated into actionable farming decisions through tools developed by regional research hubs and natural resource groups like Desert Channels Queensland. The data shows some towns now experience eight or nine additional days per month above 35 degrees Celsius during October, and warmer nights mean heat stress lingers longer, affecting fertility and weight gain in livestock. For producers navigating an uncertain future, this half-century archive offers something invaluable: the ability to adapt with confidence, guided not by guesswork but by the patient record of five decades under the Australian sun.
