In the deepest parts of the ocean, where sunlight never reaches, up to 80% of complex organisms create their own light through bioluminescence. This living glow is essential for survival—helping creatures find food, attract mates, and navigate the dark. Now, researchers are raising concerns that plastic pollution may be dimming this natural light show, with potentially far-reaching consequences for the ocean's least-explored ecosystems. The threat comes from ingested plastics and their chemical additives, which could disrupt the delicate biological processes that produce bioluminescence. Take the anglerfish, made famous by Finding Nemo: the female uses a glowing lure powered by symbiotic bacteria to attract prey in the darkness. If toxic plastic compounds interfere with these bacteria, the lure's light could fail. Similar disruptions across multiple species could ripple through deep-sea food webs in ways scientists are only beginning to understand. The challenge lies in studying these fragile organisms in their inaccessible habitats—researchers need more than evidence of plastics and creatures in the same place; they must establish clear cause-and-effect relationships under extreme deep-sea conditions. While experts agree that better long-term data are essential, there's growing optimism about what new technologies might reveal. Advanced remotely operated vehicles are making it increasingly feasible to study not just whether plastics are present in the deep ocean, but how they move through ecosystems and affect the organisms living there. This story matters because it highlights an invisible frontier where human pollution meets ancient biological systems, reminding us that even the most remote parts of our planet are not beyond our reach—or our responsibility.

Did you solve it? Isaac Newton’s tower drop puzzle

A centuries-old physics puzzle posed by Isaac Newton in 1679 reveals something surprising about how objects fall. When dropped from a high tower, where does an object land—considering Earth's rotation? Most would guess directly below, or perhaps slightly west as the planet spins eastward beneath it. The answer turns out to be counterintuitive: the object lands slightly to the east. The explanation involves the same principle that makes figure skaters spin faster when they pull their arms inward. As the object falls, it's already rotating with Earth. But as it drops closer to the planet's center, its distance from Earth's axis decreases. Conservation of angular momentum means that as this distance shrinks, the object's rotational speed must increase—causing it to spin faster than the ground below and land east of the tower's base. On the equator, an object dropped from the height of the Eiffel Tower would drift about eleven centimeters eastward. This puzzle showcases how physics can surprise us even in familiar scenarios. Newton's question to Robert Hooke demonstrates that careful thinking about rotation and momentum reveals hidden dynamics in everyday phenomena. It's a reminder that the world operates according to elegant principles that aren't always obvious at first glance, and that puzzles centuries old can still teach us to question our assumptions about how things move through space.

Kidney transplanted from mother to daughter makes history and reaches 111 years of biological age

Rim transplantado de mãe para filha faz história e chega aos 111 anos de idade biológica

A kidney transplanted in 1969 from mother to daughter in Brazil has reached 111 years of combined biological age, making it the world's oldest transplanted organ still functioning in a living person. Maria Célia received the kidney from her mother Guiomar when she was young and suffering from renal failure, at a time when Brazil performed fewer than 50 kidney transplants annually. The transplant took place just five years after Brazil's first kidney transplant, in an era without ultrasound, CT scans, or MRI, when surgical options and anti-rejection medications were severely limited. Remarkably, Maria Célia never experienced organ rejection or complications in the 57 years since her surgery, requiring only three dialysis sessions before the transplant. Her mother Guiomar, who donated the kidney at age 54, lived healthily until age 97 with no complications from having only one kidney. Nephrologist José Medina Pestana notes that while mother-to-child donations have higher success rates, a transplant lasting 57 years remains exceptional—most last between 20 and 30 years. Brazil has since become the world's second-largest country for kidney transplants, performing approximately 6,000 annually, all funded by the public health system. This story offers a touching reminder of medicine's quiet miracles and the enduring bond between mother and daughter. As Maria Célia poetically observes, she spent nine months inside her mother, and now her mother lives inside her—a relationship that has lasted far longer than gestation and shows no signs of ending.

How young officer's brief interaction left 'big impression' on Sydney local

A young police officer's death in Sydney has prompted a quiet tribute from a community member whose brief encounter with her left an unexpectedly deep mark. Constable Mia Lay, just 20 years old and 16 months into her policing career, died when the marked police vehicle she was traveling in crashed into a tree while responding to reports of a car theft in Redfern early Friday morning. What makes this story particularly poignant is the memory shared by local resident Tim Cochran, who recalled calling emergency services earlier this year when he found an unresponsive man in a park. Within minutes, Constable Lay arrived on the scene. Cochran described her as "confident and capable," watching as the young officer calmly handled the situation, answering questions he couldn't and tending to the man until an ambulance arrived. In a neighborhood where relationships with police can sometimes be strained, Cochran felt she made his community "better" and was grateful to have someone like her nearby. He never forgot her, which made seeing her photo in news reports feel like "a gut punch." This story matters because it captures something quietly profound about the everyday moments that define service and community. A single emergency call, a stranger in need, and a young officer doing her job with skill and compassion—these brief interactions can leave impressions that outlast the moment itself. As officers across the country prepare for Police Remembrance Day, Cochran's tribute offers a window into the impact Constable Lay had on those who knew her only in passing, and hints at the deeper loss felt by those who knew her well.

Mary River proves Australia's most degraded catchments can be restored

The Mary River in south-east Queensland was once labeled one of Australia's most degraded waterways, churning brown with sediment during floods and threatening the southern reaches of the Great Barrier Reef. What makes this story remarkable is not the damage, but the quiet, decades-long effort to undo it. In the 1980s, dairy farmers Peter and Elke Watson were considered oddities for planting trees and fencing cattle away from creek banks. Today, their pioneering work represents a broader transformation across the 9,595-square-kilometre catchment, led by community groups, landholders, and volunteers. A key breakthrough has been pile field technology—rows of hardwood poles driven into eroded banks, reshaped and replanted with native vegetation. These engineered interventions have stabilized sites once thought beyond repair, preventing an estimated 27,000 tonnes of sediment annually from reaching the reef and protecting vital seagrass meadows for fish, turtles, and dugongs. The Mary River Catchment Coordinating Committee has worked with landholders on over 850 projects, while volunteers regularly test water quality to track progress. The catchment is also home to several endangered species, including the Mary River turtle and the nearly extinct Mary River cod, whose recovery depends on continued habitat restoration. This story is worth a reader's time because it offers something increasingly rare: evidence that environmental damage can be reversed through patience, collaboration, and innovation. It's a reminder that large-scale ecological repair is possible—not through grand proclamations, but through the steady accumulation of small, community-driven actions over thirty years.

Cheetah cubs at Prague Zoo are doing well after a period of strong storms and their mother’s illness

Five cheetah cubs born at Prague Zoo in mid-June have overcome a remarkably challenging start to life, navigating severe storms and their mother's serious illness in their first months. The quintuplets—four males and one female—arrived during a period of intense weather, with their mother, Daenerys, choosing to give birth in an outdoor enclosure just as storms descended on the Czech capital. Despite being a first-time mother, Daenerys demonstrated exceptional maternal instincts, protecting her vulnerable newborns from torrential rains by carefully licking water from their fur to keep them dry. The challenges didn't end with the weather. In August, Daenerys developed an intestinal infection, adding another layer of concern for zookeepers already aware that cheetah cubs face high mortality rates in the wild due to predators and susceptibility to diseases like pneumonia. Curator Pavel Brandl noted that while the cubs appeared lively and healthy by September, they would need to survive a full year before zoo staff could be confident they'd made it through the most critical period. This story matters beyond one family's struggle. With fewer than 7,000 cheetahs remaining in the wild, scientists emphasize that every cub represents a vital contribution to saving the species from extinction. The world's fastest land animals, capable of reaching speeds up to 75 mph, are disappearing at an alarming rate. What makes this tale quietly remarkable is the intersection of maternal devotion, expert zoo care, and sheer resilience—a small but meaningful victory in the broader effort to preserve a species teetering on the edge.

Global coral reefs get less time to recover as oceans heat up, report finds

The world's coral reefs are facing a double challenge: declining health and vanishing recovery time. A comprehensive new assessment tracking more than 21 million observations across 123 countries reveals that global hard coral cover has dropped 9.5% compared to a baseline period from 1980 to 2009. The report, released by the Global Coral Reef Monitoring Network, represents the most extensive survey of reef health ever conducted, offering a sobering snapshot of ecosystems under mounting thermal stress. The timing is particularly alarming. Between 2023 and 2025, reefs endured their fourth global bleaching event—episodes where heat-stressed corals expel the algae they depend on for survival. This latest event triggered the steepest single-year decline on record: an 8.9% drop in just twelve months. While reefs demonstrated resilience after the previous bleaching event, bouncing back by about 6% between 2017 and 2019, the intervals between these crises have shortened dramatically. Marine heat waves now strike roughly every four to six years, down from once per decade, compressing the window corals need to rebuild. What makes this story quietly urgent is its clarity. Hard corals aren't just beautiful; they're the architects of reef ecosystems, and their cover serves as a straightforward, globally comparable measure of reef vitality. Scientists emphasize that thoughtful local management can help fortify reef resilience, but without rapid reductions in greenhouse gas emissions, even the most robust conservation efforts may not be enough. This report offers a rare, data-rich view into an ecosystem racing against a clock that keeps speeding up.

‘Hope is a built-in characteristic’: Margaret Atwood on birds, conservation and optimism for the future

Margaret Atwood has donated C$1 million to Western University in Ontario to establish a chair in avian biology, honoring her late partner, novelist Graeme Gibson, who shared her deep love of birds. The gift will support research into how birds navigate, adapt, and behave in a rapidly changing environment—questions that continue to puzzle even leading scientists. Atwood and Gibson's connection to birds ran deep: they once dressed as Odin's ravens for a party, co-founded a bird observatory on Canada's southernmost point at Pelee Island, and wove avian themes throughout their literary work. Even as dementia took Gibson's ability to journal about birds in his final years, he watched them daily with undiminished wonder, telling a friend he no longer knew their names but noting the birds didn't know his either. The donation reflects Atwood's longtime commitment to environmental protection and her belief in science's power to answer deceptively simple questions about the natural world. How do birds sense magnetic fields and navigate thousands of kilometers? Researchers at Western University—home to the world's first hypobaric climatic wind tunnel for studying bird flight—admit these mysteries remain largely unsolved despite significant advances. The university has become a global leader in understanding how migratory birds fuel and sustain themselves on epic journeys. This story offers a touching portrait of how personal loss can transform into scientific legacy, and how curiosity about the everyday—sparrows at a backyard feeder, the mechanics of migration—can lead to profound questions. It's a reminder that hope, as Atwood suggests, might indeed be built-in, and that paying attention to the natural world remains both an act of love and a path toward understanding.

Coffee, Siriri and Cururu: Free Programming Animates Weekend in Cuiabá

Café, Siriri e Cururu: programação gratuita movimenta fim de semana em Cuiabá

A shopping center in Cuiabá, Brazil, is hosting a weekend celebration that brings together two distinct cultural experiences: a coffee festival and traditional dance workshops for children. The events, both free to the public, offer a glimpse into the region's gastronomic scene and its rich folk heritage. The Coffee Festival runs Saturday and Sunday afternoon, showcasing specialty coffees alongside regional cheeses, sweets, and related products from local and national vendors. Visitors can sample everything from pour-over coffee to cappuccinos and take home beans or ground coffee. Meanwhile, on Saturday, children aged six and up can participate in workshops learning Siriri and Cururu—traditional rhythms and dances native to Mato Grosso state. The sessions are led by Grupo Flor Ribeirinha, a folk ensemble that recently claimed its fifth international championship at the World Folklore Championship in Istanbul, Turkey, where their performances of these very traditions captivated judges and audiences alike. This story captures something quietly remarkable about cultural preservation: how a shopping mall weekend can become a bridge between everyday pleasures like a good cup of coffee and centuries-old traditions passed down through music and movement. It's a reminder that culture thrives not only in competition halls abroad but also in local spaces where children learn the steps their ancestors danced, and communities gather over shared flavors and rhythms.

Tiny beach on São Paulo coast hides cave with corals and fish that live in darkness

Praia minúscula no litoral de SP esconde caverna com corais e peixes que vivem na escuridão; conheça

Tucked along Brazil's São Paulo coast, a tiny 90-meter stretch of sand called Sangava Beach holds a remarkable secret beneath its calm, clear waters. This hidden gem in Guarujá—accessible only by boat or hiking trail—features a submerged cave system where corals and fish thrive in near-darkness, creating an unexpected underwater ecosystem just offshore from one of the state's most visited coastal cities. The beach itself is a study in contrasts with its more developed neighbors. Giant boulders dot the soft sand, Atlantic Forest vegetation crowds close to the shore, and there's not a single kiosk, restaurant, or commercial structure in sight. The remoteness is deliberate: visitors must either navigate a moderate hiking trail from nearby Góes Beach or arrive by boat from Santos. This isolation means planning ahead—bringing your own water, food, and carrying out all trash. Yet for divers and stand-up paddleboarders, the effort reveals something special: a cave system in the rocky coastal formations where marine life has adapted to low-light conditions, an unusual find in these waters. What makes Sangava quietly remarkable is how it exists as a pristine pocket of wilderness within one of Brazil's busiest beach destinations. While Guarujá draws crowds to its urban beaches, this miniature shore remains largely untouched, offering a glimpse of coastal Brazil as it once was. For those willing to make the journey, it's a reminder that even in well-traveled places, nature can still surprise us with hidden worlds—some requiring only a dive below the surface to discover.

Born without arms, Reiner learned to drive using his feet with the help of a specially adapted vehicle. The journey required passing a medical evaluation, finding a driving school with the right adaptations, and overcoming his initial fear that he might not succeed. When he received his approval on August 5th, the emotion overwhelmed him. Reiner's relationship with adapted mobility began in childhood, when his godfather built him a custom bicycle he could steer with his trunk. Throughout his early years, his mother encouraged independence, and with support from therapeutic programs, Reiner learned to write, eat, dress, and manage daily tasks primarily with his feet. Now studying agronomy in Lavras and living independently, the driver's license represents another step in a lifelong journey toward autonomy that began with those early therapies and his family's determination to ensure he could participate fully in life. His story offers a quiet reminder of how determination, family support, and the right adaptations can open doors that might otherwise seem closed, and how victories that others take for granted can carry profound emotional weight for those who work harder to reach them.

Skylarks and sandwich terns among new birdies on reserve created from ‘boring old golf course’

A former golf course on the Lincolnshire coast has been transformed into a thriving nature reserve, and the results have been swift and striking. Sandilands, which once served a few hundred private club members, is now a two-kilometer stretch of wetlands, wading pools, and coastal scrub open to everyone. Before the ribbon was even cut, birds arrived: little ringed plovers and avocets nested as soon as fairways became shallow pools, while marsh harriers, sandwich terns, and short-eared owls have all made appearances. The National Trust took over the site after the golf club went into liquidation in 2018, saving the coastal land from potential development. What makes this story particularly heartening is the community response. Fifty local volunteers have already signed up to help manage the reserve, pulling ragwort and welcoming the natural succession of plants—including an explosion of thistles that brought painted lady butterflies in remarkable numbers. The reserve sits along the East Atlantic flyway, a major European migration route, positioning it to become a haven for birdwatchers and a refuge for rare migrants traveling between Arctic breeding grounds and southern wintering areas. This story offers a quiet reminder of nature's resilience and eagerness to return when given the chance. It also illustrates how conservation can serve both wildlife and people, turning an exclusive space into a communal asset for one of England's more deprived seaside towns. The transformation from "boring old golf course" to vibrant habitat happened in mere months, a testament to what's possible when land is allowed to remember what it once was.

Family singing Annie Crummer hit when their idol showed up

An Auckland family's heartfelt cover of a New Zealand classic led to an unforgettable moment when the original artist walked in on their recording session. Ese Aumalesulu and his daughters Tara, 13, and Sophia, 9, were at Radio New Zealand's studios capturing their winning version of 'See What Love Can Do' when Annie Crummer herself surprised them mid-session. The song, Crummer's 1992 rendition of an Eric Clapton original with The Herbs, had been nine-year-old Sophia's choice for the family's contest entry. The Aumalesulus beat nearly 200 other acts—including professional musicians—to win RNZ's Kiwi Cover Song contest. Their secret ingredient seems to be pure joy and family harmony, with Ese arranging and playing instruments while his daughters provide vocals. What the family didn't emphasize in their entry is that Ese is blind and both girls are visually impaired. They prefer to let their music speak for itself rather than focus on challenges. "We try not to let the world see us struggling," Ese explained, while Tara noted that music is simply "our language." Crummer was visibly moved, praising how beautifully they spread the song's message and requesting a larynx donation on the spot. This story offers a quiet reminder that art connects us across generations and circumstances. The timeless message of Crummer's song—that love brings people together—found new life through a family who embodies that very spirit, making music not despite their visual impairments but simply because it's who they are. It's a tale of talent meeting opportunity, wrapped in the warmth of family harmony.

First Samoan astrophysicist encourages Pasifika students to reach for the stars

Dr. Sean Richards has made history as the first Samoan person to earn a PhD in physics from the University of Auckland, joining just two other Samoan doctorate graduates in the university's Faculty of Science over the past two decades. Their achievement in astrophysics feels particularly meaningful: Richards sees it as continuing a tradition set by Pacific ancestors who navigated vast ocean distances using the stars thousands of years ago. Growing up in New Zealand's Bay of Islands, Richards has become a vocal advocate for creating pathways that help Pasifika students see themselves in fields that have historically excluded them. They emphasize the importance of representation, noting how students—whether queer, Māori, or Pasifika—draw strength from seeing academics who share their identities. For Richards, success isn't measured by being "first," but by creating conditions where more Pasifika students move through undergraduate studies into advanced degrees. They credit peer support programs as essential to their own journey and argue that science itself becomes stronger when diverse voices contribute. This story matters because it quietly reframes what success looks like. Rather than celebrating individual achievement alone, Richards focuses on building ladders for those who follow—a reminder that barriers in academia aren't just about individual perseverance, but about institutional structures that either welcome or exclude. The connection Richards draws between ancient Pacific navigation by starlight and modern stellar physics offers a beautiful bridge between heritage and innovation, suggesting that some paths home lead through the cosmos.
