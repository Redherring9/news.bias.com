How a world-leading health study in a small town helped shape modern medicine

In 1966, a regional Australian doctor had an unusual idea: ask an entire town to participate in a lifelong health study. Dr. Kevin Cullen approached residents of Busselton, a community 220 kilometers south of Perth, and more than 90 percent agreed to be measured, tested, and tracked over the course of their lives. What began with medical caravans rolling through this small West Australian town has become one of the world's longest-running population health studies, now celebrating its 60th anniversary. The Busselton Health Study has collected data from over 20,000 participants and published 500 scientific papers. Its findings have shaped global clinical guidelines for heart disease, sleep apnea, diabetes, and asthma. Dr. Cullen's approach was revolutionary for its time: he insisted participants receive their test results immediately, a departure from standard research practice that some feared would create a "population of hypochondriacs." Instead, his belief in preventative medicine proved transformative. For participants like Dawn Townsend, the impact has been deeply personal—the study detected a dangerous arterial blockage in her husband, likely saving his life. As Busselton has grown from a sleepy town into a thriving regional city, researchers face a new challenge: encouraging newcomers to join. Volunteers like Jean Chant, who has dedicated over 20 years to the study, work to maintain the community spirit that made the research possible. The study's 60th anniversary is being marked with a mobile caravan exhibit featuring archival materials. This story reminds us how a single community's commitment to science can ripple outward, quietly reshaping medical understanding for generations worldwide.

What Japanese says when family obligations take an unexpected turn

A Japanese phrase reveals how marriage traditions continue to shape modern obligations in unexpected ways. While "till death do us part" suggests a marriage ends when one partner dies, Japan's cultural understanding of marriage has historically extended beyond the relationship between two individuals—and vestiges of that understanding persist today. The traditional family household system, known as ie seido, was formalized in 1898 and lasted until postwar reforms abolished it in 1947. Under this framework, marriage wasn't simply a union between husband and wife, but an integration into an entire family line. The system emphasized household continuity across generations, with the eldest son typically inheriting the family name, property, and the legal duty to support aging parents financially and practically. This differed significantly from Western concepts of marriage as primarily a personal partnership. Though the ie seido was officially dismantled more than seventy years ago, cultural expectations around family obligations haven't entirely disappeared. This glimpse into Japan's marriage terminology offers a quiet reminder of how language preserves history and how deeply rooted social structures can outlive their formal legal frameworks. For anyone interested in how tradition and modernity coexist, or how family ties are understood differently across cultures, this story illuminates the subtle ways the past continues to influence contemporary Japanese life—even after death has ended the marriage itself.

Destructive and delicious: how an invasive deer in Hawaii is being turned into dinner

A gift of eight axis deer to Hawaii's King Kamehameha V in 1868 seemed innocent enough, but their later introduction to Maui for hunting in the 1950s has spiraled into an ecological crisis. Today, an estimated 60,000 axis deer roam three Hawaiian islands, breeding year-round and devastating native plants, endangered species, and local agriculture. Unlike their white-tailed cousins, these South Asian deer have no natural predators in Hawaii and reproduce constantly, stripping vegetation to the roots and consuming farmers' crops—even forcing one sausage maker to discontinue a product because the deer ate all the local green onions. Enter Maui Nui Venison, a company that has turned this environmental problem into a sustainable food source. Working with private ranches and under USDA oversight, their hunters conduct nightly operations using night-vision technology, infrared drones, and military-grade equipment to harvest 40 to 55 deer per night. The meat becomes steaks, jerky, bone broth, and health supplements. Local chefs like Sheldon Simeon are embracing the venison, using it in dishes like wontons to help shift perceptions about gamey wild meat. The culling is carefully tracked to monitor the deer population while providing a high-quality protein source. This story offers a quietly compelling example of adaptive problem-solving—a case where ecological responsibility meets culinary innovation. With Hawaii's governor having declared 25 states of emergency related to axis deer damage, including drought conditions they've worsened, the situation is serious. Yet Maui Nui's approach demonstrates how communities can turn invasive species management into something productive, addressing environmental harm while creating local food security and changing how people think about what's on their plates.

Chongqing, a sprawling metropolis in southwestern China, offers visitors a disorienting experience where traditional notions of ground level become fluid. What seems impossible is simply everyday life in this vertical city. The explanation lies in geography and necessity. Built across mountainous terrain, Chongqing had little flat land to accommodate its growing population, so urban planners worked with the dramatic topography rather than against it. Buildings rise from different elevations of the hillsides, creating a layered cityscape where "ground floor" depends entirely on which entrance you use. Some residential towers climb 24 stories without elevators, requiring residents to navigate long flights of stairs as part of their daily routine. The city's commercial life adapts accordingly, with shops, pharmacies, and street vendors scattered across these various levels. This dramatic transformation happened remarkably recently. Before the 1990s, the slopes now occupied by gravity-defying skyscrapers were worked by laborers who hauled coal on their backs, washing it by the river before sale. The "White Elephant" building, completed in 1992, symbolizes this shift from manual labor to vertical living, part of an urban renewal that relocated older communities and reshaped how people lived and worked. For readers fascinated by how cities adapt to challenging landscapes, Chongqing offers a compelling example of architecture that embraces constraint and turns geographic difficulty into distinctive urban character.
