Chemistry Nobel awarded for solving mystery of life's asymmetry

The Nobel Prize in Chemistry has been awarded to Henri B. Kagan of France and Kenso Soai of Japan for solving one of chemistry's most enduring puzzles: why life exclusively uses one form of certain molecules rather than their mirror images. Their work explains a phenomenon called chirality, where molecules exist in two forms that are mirror images of each other, much like our left and right hands. This discovery has profound implications for understanding life itself. All living organisms on Earth use only one version of amino acids, the building blocks of proteins, even though both mirror-image forms exist. In laboratory settings, chemical reactions typically produce equal amounts of both versions, making the natural world's selectivity all the more mysterious. Kagan's breakthrough came in 1986 when he developed methods to create an excess of one mirror image. Soai then took this further, achieving in 2003 what the Nobel Committee called a feat matched only by life itself: a chemical reaction producing exclusively one mirror-image form. The practical applications are particularly important in medicine, where one version of a molecule might fight disease while its mirror image could be ineffective or even harmful. This research matters because it bridges fundamental science with everyday human concerns. Beyond helping chemists design better pharmaceuticals, it offers insights into life's basic architecture—explaining quirks as varied as why our hearts sit on the left side of our bodies and why snail shells tend to coil in one direction. The work represents more than a technical achievement; it's a window into understanding why the chemistry of life took the particular path it did, turning an ancient mystery into actionable knowledge.

How to assemble science

In an era when roughly 19,000 scholarly articles are published daily, science journalist Siobhan O'Connor explores a troubling paradox: we're drowning in research but struggling to understand what it all means. The problem isn't just volume—it's that individual studies are reported in isolation, creating whiplash narratives where coffee is alternately good and bad for us, and critical health guidance flip-flops dangerously. O'Connor points to a stark example: when the FDA announced in 2025 that acetaminophen during pregnancy raised autism risk, global headlines sparked widespread alarm. Yet a comprehensive review months later found no clear link, leaving countless women needlessly frightened about one of pregnancy's safest painkillers. The root cause, O'Connor argues, is systemic. Scientists chase publications to advance careers. Journalists pursue flashy headlines for clicks. Almost no one prioritizes the unglamorous work of synthesizing what we already know—what physician Iain Chalmers called science's "scandalous failure to cumulate evidence scientifically." This gap becomes especially concerning when diverse forms of knowledge, from lived experience to Indigenous insights, rarely make it into scholarly databases at all. The essay traces a pivotal moment in addressing this crisis: statistician Gene Glass's 1976 presentation of an innovative technique for integrating findings across multiple studies, a method so transformative it would reshape how science assembles knowledge. This story matters because it reveals something quietly radical: for many of our toughest challenges, humanity may already possess the knowledge needed to solve them. The real work isn't generating more studies—it's learning to assemble what we know, and then acting on it.
