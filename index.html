"It's a succession of miracles": the incredible rescue of two workers after 9 days trapped in a tunnel by the avalanche on the Nepal-Tibet border

"Es una sucesión de milagros": el increíble rescate de dos trabajadores tras 9 días atrapados en un túnel por la avalancha en la frontera entre Nepal y Tíbet

In a rescue operation described as "a succession of miracles," two workers were pulled alive from a hydroelectric tunnel in Nepal after nine days trapped underground. Sanjaya Shah and Kabir Maharjan survived the ordeal following a devastating flash flood that swept through the Himalayan region along the Nepal-Tibet border. They were airlifted directly to a hospital in Kathmandu with injuries to their hands and legs, likely sustained while crawling through the tunnel in search of escape. The rescue was extraordinarily complex. The entrance to the Trishuli 3A hydroelectric plant had been completely buried by the avalanche, leaving rescuers to first locate it in a transformed landscape using satellite imagery, aerial footage, old topographic maps, and triangulation techniques. Once found, teams faced a tunnel choked with mud and car-sized boulders. They carefully used explosives to clear debris—a risky strategy that could have endangered both survivors and rescuers—while simultaneously pumping out floodwater. Arnold Dix, a tunnel expert advising the operation, praised the Nepalese army for managing the delicate work with precision. The broader disaster has been catastrophic: over 1,200 bodies have been recovered, more than 5,000 people remain missing, and at least 150 are feared trapped in other hydroelectric facilities across Nepal. This story stands out not only for the against-the-odds survival of two men in unimaginable conditions, but for the ingenuity, courage, and determination of the rescuers who refused to give up—a quiet testament to human resilience in the face of nature's overwhelming power.

This is a story about absence, yes, but equally about the presence of those who walk alongside us through incomprehensible grief. Between 1949 and 1976, approximately 250,000 unmarried women in the United Kingdom were forced to give up their newborn babies for adoption—a systematic practice driven by social stigma, class dynamics, and institutional pressure. The BBC documentary "Stolen Babies" brings these long-hidden stories into the light, revealing a painful chapter in which the state, social workers, and religious organizations collaborated to separate working-class single mothers from their children, often transferring them to middle-class adoptive families. Marion McMillan's story illustrates the cruelty of the system. At seventeen, pregnant and in love with a Pakistani immigrant, she was sent far from her Scottish home to a Salvation Army maternity home in Newcastle. There, she endured harsh treatment—including being slapped during labor—and spent nine agonizing months with her baby before he was taken for adoption. The staff told her that mixed-race children "rarely succeed in adoption," prolonging her time with her son but ultimately delivering the same outcome. Another woman, Ann Keen, plainly described the class dimension: working-class girls' babies given to middle-class families. In July 2023, UK Prime Minister Keir Starmer offered a formal apology, telling these mothers that the shame belongs not to them but to the nation. This story matters because it uncovers a widespread injustice hidden by decades of silence and personal shame. It's a reminder of how societal judgment and institutional power can devastate lives, and how acknowledgment—however late—begins the process of healing and historical reckoning.

'Miracle' eagle with broken wing spotted back above Scottish coast

A young sea eagle on Scotland's Isle of Mull has defied the odds by surviving to adulthood despite a severely broken wing sustained as a chick. The bird, affectionately nicknamed "Kinky" for the distinctive kink visible in its wing, fell from its nest during a storm in 2023. Wildlife experts monitoring the island's eagle population didn't expect the injured chick to make it, making its recent sighting soaring above the coastal waters all the more remarkable. What makes this story particularly touching is the extraordinary parental care that gave Kinky his chance at life. His white-tailed eagle parents did something experts had never witnessed before: they skipped an entire breeding season to continue caring for their year-old offspring, extending their nurturing well beyond the typical timeframe. That devoted attention helped the young eagle survive into what Dave Sexton, a retired RSPB officer with over two decades of sea eagle experience, calls the "sub-adult" phase—a potentially dangerous period when established adult pairs may no longer tolerate his presence in their territories. This tale of resilience carries added significance given the species' troubled history in Britain. Sea eagles were hunted to extinction in the UK, with the last native bird killed in 1918. A reintroduction effort beginning in 1975 has slowly rebuilt their numbers, and they returned to Mull in the 1980s. Kinky's survival against the odds serves as a quiet testament to both nature's tenacity and the success of conservation efforts—a reminder that sometimes, with a bit of help and a lot of determination, even the most vulnerable can find a way to soar.

Traditional owners say national park can 'breathe again' after handback

After decades of campaigning and protest, Boodjamulla National Park in outback Queensland has been formally returned to the Waanyi people, who have maintained connection to this land for more than 30,000 years. The 247,000-hectare park, known for its dramatic gorges and World Heritage-listed fossil deposits at Riversleigh, attracts over 20,000 visitors annually. For traditional owners, the handback represents what Hayley Iles, chair of the Waanyi Prescribed Body Corporate, calls "a long-awaited homecoming" — a moment when "the land breathes again." The journey to this moment stretches back through generations of struggle. After being dispossessed of their country, Waanyi people staged sit-in protests in 1994 and 2002, demanding recognition and a voice in managing both the park and nearby mining operations. Those actions helped build momentum toward formal negotiations that began in 2020. The land transfer occurred progressively in three stages, culminating in January of this year. Under the agreement, the park is leased back to the state, with a board of equal representation ensuring Waanyi and Queensland Parks rangers work side by side, blending cultural knowledge with land management expertise. This story matters because it illustrates patience and persistence across generations. While elders who began this fight have passed away, their vision is now realized in a model that honours both sovereignty and shared stewardship. For visitors, little will change on the surface, but beneath lies a profound shift: Boodjamulla, meaning rainbow serpent country, is once again recognized as Aboriginal land, where ancient connection and modern conservation walk together.

The place that never freezes: the Arctic habitat where global heating threatens more ice, not less

In the high Arctic between Canada and Greenland lies a remarkable phenomenon: a vast area of open water that persists year-round, even in the depths of polar winter. Known as the North Water to 19th-century whalers, Pikialasorsuaq to Greenlandic Inuit, and Sarvarjuaq to Canadian Inuit—"the place that never freezes"—this polynya spans 85,000 square kilometers and supports an extraordinary web of life. From algae blooms to whales, polar bears, seals, and millions of seabirds, the open water creates an oasis in one of Earth's harshest environments. The polynya's existence depends on a delicate mechanism: an ice arch that typically forms across Nares Strait to the north, blocking sea ice from drifting south from the Arctic Ocean and keeping the water clear. But climate change is disrupting this system in a counterintuitive way. The ice arch now frequently fails to form, and when it does fail, more ice drifts into the polynya, threatening to cover the open water. Researcher Terry Noah, working from a shipping container lab in Grise Fiord—Canada's northernmost hamlet—collects data on these changes, lowering instruments through seal breathing holes to track what's happening beneath the ice. Meanwhile, a 2017 proposal to create an Inuit-managed protected area spanning both Canadian and Greenlandic waters has seen progress on the Canadian side but stalled in Greenland, where concerns about Indigenous rights complicate conservation efforts. This story offers a window into how climate change operates in unexpected ways, where warming doesn't always mean less ice. It's a reminder that the Arctic's transformations affect not just wildlife but the Indigenous communities whose cultures have adapted to these ecosystems over millennia.
