The team's work, supported by several conservation institutes, will culminate in a comprehensive bird guide expected in 2027, offering visitors new opportunities to appreciate the rich biodiversity of this crucial Atlantic Forest remnant. It's a quiet reminder that even well-studied places can still surprise us with hidden inhabitants.

Recycled glass offers a potential new tool to restore a vanishing coastline

Louisiana is losing land at an alarming rate—a soccer field's worth every 100 minutes—due to coastal erosion accelerated by sea level rise and human activity. Over the past century, the state has lost 1,800 square miles of coastland, leaving communities, ecosystems, and a $319 billion seafood industry increasingly vulnerable to storms and floods. Traditional restoration efforts use offshore sand and river sediment to rebuild barrier islands and shorelines, but suitable materials are running short. Enter an innovative solution: sand made from recycled glass bottles. Since 2020, a New Orleans company called Glass Half Full has been experimenting with transforming discarded glass into sand and gravel for small-scale coastal restoration projects. To understand whether this material is safe for local wildlife, the company partnered with Tulane University on Project ReCoast, a five-year research initiative. A recent study published in Restoration Ecology tested how five economically important native species—including blue crabs, eastern oysters, and Atlantic croakers—fared when exposed to recycled glass sand. After monitoring the animals for about a month in aquariums, researchers found no significant differences in survival rates compared to traditional sediment types. While the findings are promising, researchers acknowledge that recycled glass sand has limitations: supply is finite, and scalability remains a challenge. Still, this story offers a hopeful glimpse of how creative thinking and scientific rigor can converge to address environmental crises. It's a quiet reminder that solutions to large-scale problems sometimes begin with small experiments—and that even discarded bottles might have a second life protecting coastlines.

Menopause: why doctors say the fear of hormone therapy is greater than the actual risk

Menopausa: por que médicos dizem que o medo da terapia hormonal é maior que o risco real

Brazilian doctors are noticing a persistent disconnect: many women fear hormone replacement therapy (HRT) for menopause far more than the evidence warrants. When patients initially hear the suggestion, their first question is almost always about cancer risk. Yet three months into treatment, these same women often return amazed at the relief they've found, wishing they'd started sooner. One gynecologist describes patients saying the medication eliminated hot flashes "like magic"—though she's quick to point out it's simply effective hormone therapy doing what it's designed to do. The stakes are significant in Brazil, where approximately 33 million women are currently between ages 40 and 65, the window when most experience menopause symptoms. Yet a 2022 study found that only 22% of Brazilian women who sought medical help for menopause symptoms were offered HRT. The therapy replaces estrogen and progesterone, hormones that drop sharply during menopause and cause hot flashes, night sweats, insomnia, and mood changes. While certain cancer risks exist—estrogen alone can affect uterine tissue, and HRT is contraindicated for some breast cancer survivors—recent research suggests these risks are generally smaller than previously thought. This story matters because it highlights how outdated fears, rooted in a flawed early-2000s study, continue to shape medical practice decades later. In February, the U.S. FDA removed its most severe safety warnings from HRT labels, reflecting newer evidence. Brazilian physicians hope this shift will help update perceptions at home, potentially improving quality of life for millions of women navigating a natural but often challenging life transition.

How intuitions work

Everyday intuitions—the gut feelings that tell us someone seems "off" or a deal feels suspicious—often get dismissed as irrational hunches. Yet these immediate, wordless judgments frequently guide us wisely, even when we can't articulate exactly why. A philosophical exploration in Aeon examines why these intuitive reactions deserve to be taken seriously as rational tools for navigating the world. The essay bridges two seemingly distant types of intuition: the everyday kind that keeps us from clicking suspicious links, and the rarefied "rational intuition" philosophers discuss when pondering abstract truths in logic or mathematics. Both, the author argues, work similarly to perception—they directly present something to us as having a certain character. Through famous thought experiments like the trolley problem (where most people intuitively feel that pushing someone to save five others is wrong) and the fake-barn scenario (which challenges our understanding of knowledge), the piece reveals how intuition functions as a specialized cognitive capacity rather than mere guesswork. This exploration matters because it rehabilitates a way of knowing that modern thinking often undervalues. By understanding intuition as akin to perception—a faculty that can directly apprehend reality rather than merely guess at it—we gain appreciation for why trusting that "creepy vibe" or sensing something is "off" isn't irrational at all. It's a legitimate form of engagement with the world, one that operates beneath conscious reasoning but remains indispensable to sound judgment. The essay offers a thoughtful defense of those moments when we simply know something without knowing how we know it.

These striking white birds, as large as grey herons with lemon eyes and orange beaks, moved through the brown landscape hunting voles rather than their usual fish prey, their long necks extending and retracting like snakes as they stalked their quarry. The presence of great egrets in Britain represents a stunning recovery from near-extinction. Victorian demand for feathers in haute couture nearly wiped out the species, but gradual protection allowed them to recover and expand across Europe. They first colonized British shores at the beginning of this century, bred for the first time in 2010, and by 2019 had reached 24 nesting pairs. Winter populations may now number in the thousands—a transformation few could have predicted just decades ago. Already teeming with bitterns, water voles, and marsh harriers, the site's archaeological record reveals Neolithic bones of beavers and dalmatian pelicans. This story matters because it shows how landscapes can be restored and species can return from the brink—a quiet reminder that even in an era of climate crisis, thoughtful conservation can yield unexpected wonders.

‘Once-in-a-lifetime chance’: Japan launches historic mission to collect rocks from Mars’s moon Phobos

Japan has launched an ambitious mission to collect rocks from Phobos, one of Mars's two small moons, in what scientists are calling a historic first. The Martian Moons Exploration spacecraft lifted off aboard an H3 rocket from Tanegashima island in the pre-dawn hours, watched by thousands via livestream. If successful, the mission will return at least 10 grams of soil and rock from Phobos to Earth by 2031, marking the first time any nation has attempted to land on either of Mars's potato-shaped moons. The five-year, $578 million mission is a collaborative effort involving five space agencies, including NASA and European partners. After reaching Mars orbit in August 2026, the spacecraft will deploy a French-German rover named Idefix, which will bounce across Phobos's surface for 100 days to scout landing sites. The main lander will then spend just two hours drilling and collecting samples before launching back into space. Scientists hope the samples will help settle a fundamental question: were Phobos and Deimos formed by a giant impact with Mars, or are they captured asteroids? The answer could reveal important clues about how habitable environments arose on Earth and other terrestrial planets. This mission arrives amid a broader race to return samples from the Martian system. While NASA's Mars sample return has been halted and China's Tianwen-3 mission won't launch until 2028, Japan's focus on Phobos offers a unique window into the early solar system. For the engineers and scientists involved, it represents a career-defining moment—a chance to touch a world no human probe has ever visited and bring home answers to questions billions of years in the making.

Cómo es Fobos, la luna de Marte que es el destino de una nueva y revolucionaria misión espacial

The spacecraft is scheduled to depart from Japan and reach Phobos by August 2027, with samples expected to return to Earth's surface in 2031. Phobos is a peculiar object—just 20 kilometers in diameter, irregularly shaped like a space potato, and orbiting extremely close to Mars. Landing on such a small body presents unique challenges, as it has minimal gravity of its own while sitting next to a massive planet with much stronger gravitational pull. The mission will deploy a small rover to scout the terrain before the main spacecraft touches down to collect approximately 10 grams of lunar dust using robotic arms and sample collectors. Though modest in quantity, scientists say even individual grains can yield significant discoveries. The samples promise to solve a long-standing mystery: did Phobos and its companion moon Deimos form from debris after a massive collision with early Mars, or are they captured asteroids from the outer solar system? Chemical and mineral analysis should provide a clear answer. There's an added bonus too—Phobos orbits so close to Mars that grains from the Martian surface may be embedded in the moon itself, potentially offering direct insights into the Red Planet's composition and history without ever landing there.

Uncovering the secrets of New Zealand’s elusive ‘jungle penguins’

In the remote fjords of New Zealand's South Island, researchers are unraveling the mysteries of one of the world's most peculiar penguins. The Fiordland crested penguin, known by its Māori name tawaki, has proven so elusive that scientists describe it as a "ghost." These half-meter-tall birds with striking yellow eyebrows dig elaborate tunnel systems to escape capture, sometimes taking researchers days to retrieve tracking devices. Until 2014, almost nothing was known about them. The Tawaki Project, founded by Dr. Thomas Mattern and his wife Ursula Ellenberg, has mapped over 1,200 nests across nine fjords in twelve years, revealing a population roughly three times larger than previously thought. What makes these penguins truly unusual is their complete rejection of typical penguin behavior. While most penguins nest in coastal colonies, tawaki build solitary burrows deep in dense rainforest, sometimes at high elevations, earning them the nickname "jungle penguins." Their hunting habits vary unpredictably between fjords, with some groups fishing locally while others venture to the open ocean. Perhaps most baffling is their pre-molting migration. Each December, after breeding season ends and despite abundant local food, these exhausted penguins embark on a 5,000 to 7,000 kilometer journey to sub-Antarctic waters to feed before their annual feather replacement. Mattern likens it to "running a marathon while trying to gain weight." He suspects they're following ancient instincts shared with other crested penguin species. This story reminds us how much remains unknown even about creatures living in well-studied regions, and how patient observation can reveal nature's delightful refusal to follow our expectations.
