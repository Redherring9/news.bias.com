The British island that made a deal with nature

Lundy Island, a windswept outcrop in the Bristol Channel between England and Wales, has become one of the United Kingdom's most remarkable conservation success stories. Two decades ago, the island's seabird population had plummeted to around 6,000 birds, devastated by accidentally introduced rats that preyed on eggs and chicks in their burrows. Today, following a successful rat eradication program launched in the early 2000s, the seabird population has soared to approximately 48,000, including puffins, razorbills, guillemots, and Manx shearwaters. What makes Lundy's conservation approach particularly noteworthy is its collaborative spirit. Rather than simply excluding human activity, the island's marine protected area was designed with input from local fishermen, conservationists, and business owners who all depend on these waters. In 2003, Lundy became home to the UK's first no-take zone—a carefully designated area where all fishing is prohibited—while surrounding waters allow sustainable activities like recreational fishing and lobster potting. The restrictions on destructive practices like trawling and dredging have helped restore the island's diverse underwater habitats, from kelp forests and rocky reefs to sandy bottoms and caves. This story matters because it offers a blueprint for conservation that works with communities rather than against them. At a time when many British seabird populations continue to decline, Lundy demonstrates that recovery is possible when local stakeholders come together with a shared commitment to protecting their environment. The island now thrives as a place where wildlife, fishing traditions, and tourism coexist—proof that nature and human livelihoods need not be at odds.

In a small town in Espírito Santo, Brazil, a mysterious phenomenon has captivated believers and curious visitors for three decades. Thousands of leaves, delicately perforated point by point, display religious images and biblical passages so intricate they require a magnifying glass to fully appreciate. The patterns were first discovered in 1995 and attributed to ants, which locals dubbed "embroiderer ants" or "formigas bordadeiras." With support from Catholic Church representatives, including a bishop and a renowned Marian scholar, she established the Sanctuary of Our Lady of Tears. Over twenty years, an estimated 8,000 leaves were "embroidered" before the phenomenon ceased in 2015. The messages, according to those who've studied them, offer guidance on human behavior, charity, and faith. Scientific attempts to identify an insect capable of creating such precise perforations have come up empty, leaving the origin unexplained. The site now functions as a museum and pilgrimage destination, with families sharing stories of healing and answered prayers, including accounts of medical recoveries that devotees attribute to their visits. This story offers a window into how unexplained natural phenomena can become woven into community faith and identity. Whether the work of industrious insects or something else entirely, the embroidered leaves represent decades of hope, devotion, and the human need to find meaning in the mysterious corners of our world.

At 192cm tall, Tiny Tim could be a national cattle record breaker

In a paddock south of Perth, an eight-year-old Holstein-Friesian steer named Tiny Tim stands 192 centimeters tall and weighs roughly 1,400 kilograms—about the size of an SUV and twice the weight of an average steer. Now he lives peacefully alongside his best mate, a long-horned steer, in a front paddock where passing tourists regularly stop to photograph the pair. This story offers a quiet reminder that sometimes the most surprising records are set not through ambition, but through the simple, unhurried unfolding of nature. Hatching a plan to ensure Australia's native turtles survive and thrive

Australia's 1 Million Turtles campaign is making quiet but remarkable progress in protecting the country's native freshwater turtles. Launched in 2021 with the ambitious goal of helping a million turtles survive over 30 years, the citizen science project has already logged nearly 40,000 turtle sightings and helped rescue more than 3,000 individual turtles from roads and other dangers. Volunteers have also discovered and protected nearly 2,500 nests, potentially safeguarding around 50,000 eggs. The program focuses on three species—the broad-shelled turtle, eastern long-necked turtle, and Murray River short-necked turtle—and relies on everyday Australians to report sightings through a mobile app called TurtleSAT. The data helps researchers understand turtle ecology and address critical knowledge gaps, particularly during the vulnerable period after hatching. By radio-tracking 50 hatchlings, scientists discovered that most young turtles are eaten by native birds during what researchers call "the missing years" before they re-emerge at around three years old. This finding reveals both the dangers hatchlings face and their important role as a food source in the broader ecosystem. This story offers a heartening example of how community science can make a tangible difference for wildlife. The wet spring currently underway in southeastern Australia is especially promising, as more water means more habitat and nesting opportunities. What makes this project quietly remarkable is its dual nature: while helping individual turtles survive today, it's simultaneously building the scientific knowledge needed to protect these ancient reptiles for generations to come.

Striking snakes released into the wild in historic fight to save species from extinction

In a carefully orchestrated conservation effort, 62 San Francisco garter snakes—born and raised at the San Francisco Zoo—have been released into the wild for the first time this autumn. These non-venomous reptiles, distinguished by their vivid red heads, turquoise bellies, and bold red-and-black stripes, are found nowhere else on Earth but the San Francisco peninsula. Listed as endangered for more than fifty years, the species has faced a cascade of challenges: habitat loss from urban development, illegal poaching, prey decline, drought, disease, and dwindling genetic diversity. The release marks a milestone in a "head-starting" program, a novel approach in which pregnant snakes were brought to the zoo in 2025, their offspring raised in captivity during the most vulnerable months of life, and then returned to carefully selected sites in San Mateo County. The mothers were released back into the wild shortly after giving birth. Each young snake underwent genetic testing to ensure it was returned to the habitat best suited to its lineage—a detail that underscores the precision and collaboration behind the project, which involved state and federal agencies, nonprofits, and zoo scientists. More than 130 additional snakes born at the zoo this year are slated for release in 2027. This story is a quiet testament to what patience, science, and cooperation can achieve. It reminds us that even species on the edge of disappearing can find a path back—one young snake at a time.

Floating wind is building ‘A lot of momentum’ toward commercialization

Floating wind turbines—anchored in deep ocean waters rather than fixed to shallow seabeds—represent an emerging frontier in renewable energy. Though still in its early stages, this technology is gaining traction in countries like China, Japan, South Korea, and across Europe, particularly in places where shallow coastal waters or available land make conventional wind farms impractical. For these nations, floating wind could become essential to meeting clean energy commitments. Currently, the global floating wind industry consists mainly of pilot projects, producing around 300 megawatts of power—a fraction of the nearly 100 gigawatts generated by traditional fixed offshore turbines. Norway's Hywind Tampen, the largest floating wind farm to date, powers oil and gas platforms with 11 turbines. While experts acknowledge floating wind won't dominate the offshore sector, it offers a vital option for regions constrained by geography. The technology itself is sound, but scaling it commercially remains complex. High costs, underdeveloped supply chains, and the need for specialized port infrastructure pose significant hurdles. Economic headwinds like inflation and rising interest rates have further slowed momentum across the entire offshore wind industry. What makes this story quietly remarkable is the promise it holds for unlocking renewable energy in places previously thought unsuitable. As innovation progresses and policy support strengthens, floating wind could transform how we think about harnessing the ocean's power—offering a glimpse of a future where deep waters become vast, sustainable energy reserves.

Is it genuine love – or obsessive passion? Kierkegaard, the father of existentialism, had some thoughts

When a new relationship feels all-consuming, it can be hard to tell whether it's the beginning of something beautiful or a warning sign of something unstable. A counselor and philosopher writing for The Conversation turns to 19th-century Danish thinker Søren Kierkegaard to explore the difference between genuine love and obsessive attachment. Kierkegaard, often called the father of existentialism, distinguished between two types of love: "aesthetic" love, which feels like falling under a powerful spell, and "ethical" love, grounded in choice, trust, and respect. Aesthetic love, Kierkegaard argued, is driven by intense desire and emotion—a force that seems irresistible but lacks the stability needed for a healthy relationship. It centers on personal feelings rather than principles, and can resemble addiction: the lover may feel compelled to stay despite knowing the relationship is harmful. The author describes a counselee who was willing to upend his entire life for a few months of passionate intensity, only to spiral into despair when it ended. In such cases, the beloved becomes an object meant to fill an inner void, rather than a person with their own needs. This kind of love, Kierkegaard wrote, is rooted in a form of self-rejection or escape from oneself. By contrast, ethical love expands freedom rather than restricting it. It's built on virtue, deliberate choice, and a strong sense of self—qualities that allow both people to flourish. The piece offers a thoughtful lens for anyone trying to understand their own relationships, reminding us that love at its best isn't about losing ourselves in someone else, but about choosing connection from a place of inner wholeness.

My grandmother was a seal

In 1948 Ireland, an 80-year-old storyteller shared a tale that echoes through Celtic folklore: his ancestor had married a woman who was once a seal. The story follows a familiar pattern—a farmer discovers a seal shedding its skin to become human, steals the skin to prevent her return to the sea, and claims her as his bride. She bears his children, who carry webbing between their toes, while she gazes longingly at the ocean, never smiling. These shapeshifters, now commonly called selkies, appear throughout folklore from Ireland to Scotland to Sweden, described variously as sea maidens or mermaids who transform by shedding their skins. The tale was collected by the Irish Folklore Commission, which became a 3-million-page archive at University College Dublin. Following centuries of colonialism, famine, and the near-extinction of Irish language and culture, collectors raced to preserve these stories before they disappeared with their tellers. What makes the selkie tales particularly striking is their intimate, local quality—storytellers name specific beaches and families, treating these encounters not as myth but as genealogy. Multiple families claimed descent from human-seal unions, their stories blending the fantastic with the matter-of-fact detail of hard fishing lives. This story offers something quietly profound: a window into how communities preserved identity and memory through folklore, even when colonialism and catastrophe threatened to erase them. The selkie tales—neither quite human nor animal, caught between two worlds—may have resonated especially deeply with people whose own culture existed in a precarious in-between state, holding onto something ancient while navigating forces of assimilation and loss.

Mysterious Mercury is about to get a visitor - after spacecraft's eight-year epic voyage

After an eight-year odyssey covering nearly six billion miles, the spacecraft BepiColombo is preparing to enter orbit around Mercury, the Solar System's smallest and least-explored planet. The mission, which launched in 2018, has had to fight the Sun's gravitational pull the entire way, taking a winding route that looped around Earth once, Venus twice, and Mercury six times just to slow down enough to arrive. A propulsion issue added an extra year to the already epic journey. Now comes the most perilous moment: an 11-minute maneuver to slip into orbit, with no room for error. Mercury is a world of extremes and mysteries. Temperatures swing from 430°C to -180°C, and despite being the closest planet to the Sun, ice hides in its polar craters. Thousands of strange, shallow depressions called "hollows" dot the surface, their origin unknown. The planet also harbors an outsized metal core, leading scientists to wonder if Mercury was once much larger and lost its outer layers in ancient collisions, or simply formed in a metal-rich pocket of the early Solar System. Time itself behaves oddly there: a year lasts just 88 Earth days, while a single day stretches to 59. Only two spacecraft have visited Mercury before, both from NASA, leaving much still to discover. BepiColombo's mission is to unravel these puzzles and deepen our understanding of how planets form and evolve in extreme environments. It's a reminder that even in our own cosmic neighborhood, profound mysteries remain—and that patient, carefully orchestrated journeys can still unlock them.

Wreck of first Australian-built warship found off Far North Queensland coast

A team of archaeologists has discovered the wreck of HMCS Spitfire, the first purpose-built Australian warship, lying off the coast of Far North Queensland exactly 128 years after it sank. Built in Sydney's Darling Harbour in 1855 from Australian timber, the vessel was originally commissioned by the New South Wales colonial government amid fears that Russia might invade Australia during the Crimean War. Despite being designed as a warship, the single gun intended for the 19-metre vessel was never installed, and it spent most of its service life on humbler duties—patrolling coastlines, delivering supplies to lighthouses, and eventually working as a pilot vessel before being sold to private owners as a fishing boat. After more than four decades of service, the aging Spitfire met its end on August 20, 1898, when a fierce gale opened the seams between its planks during a voyage near Lockhart River. The crew, composed largely of Aboriginal, Torres Strait Islander, and South Pacific Islander people, abandoned ship but all survived, reaching a nearby island before being rescued by a Japanese steamship. Finding the wreck proved challenging—conflicting historical records made pinpointing its location difficult, and the rapid marine growth characteristic of the Great Barrier Reef area had obscured it for over a century. Archaeologists James Hunter and Celeste Jordan, working with the crew of HMAS Diamantina, finally located it this August by searching for the telltale straight lines and round shapes that don't occur naturally. This discovery offers a rare window into Australia's early maritime history and the diverse communities who worked its waters. With much of the crew's belongings still aboard, next year's planned expedition promises to reveal intimate details about life at sea in nineteenth-century Australia.

The long reach of El Niño impacts on Southeast Asia’s primates

A new review published in the journal Oryx reveals troubling knowledge gaps about how El Niño-driven droughts and fires affect Southeast Asia's primates, both immediately and for decades afterward. While researchers know that 94% of the region's primate species face extinction threats, they found only 15 published papers examining El Niño's impact—covering just seven of the region's 105 primate species, all in Indonesia. The effects documented so far are predominantly negative. Habitat loss forces primates to change their diets and behaviors, crowds populations into smaller forest areas, and can lead to localized disappearances. Long-term studies of siamang gibbons following the catastrophic 1997-98 El Niño fires showed that these apes avoided heavily burned areas for nearly two decades, even after forests regrew with altered compositions. More concerning still, researchers found dramatically higher infant mortality rates persisting for years after fires ended, potentially due to reduced habitat quality, stress, or toxin exposure—factors that make it harder for already small, isolated populations to bounce back. This story matters because it illuminates a hidden dimension of climate events. The dramatic images of fires may fade, but their echoes ripple through primate communities for generations in ways scientists are only beginning to understand. With El Niño events expected to intensify, and so little research covering such a small fraction of affected species, conservationists face the challenge of protecting primates from threats they can't yet fully measure. It's a quiet reminder that survival isn't just about escaping the flames—it's about what happens in all the years that follow.

Vatican Displays Three Works by Aleijadinho to the World

Vaticano exibe ao mundo 3 obras de Aleijadinho

Three wooden sculptures by Aleijadinho, Brazil's most celebrated 18th-century artist, are making history as the first works by the master to be exhibited outside the country. The pieces—depicting São Manuel, Sant'Ana Mestra, and São Joaquim—have left their home parishes in Minas Gerais to be displayed at the Vatican Museums beginning this week. The move represents a remarkable moment for Brazilian cultural heritage on the world stage. The sculptures required meticulous preparation for their journey to Italy. After undergoing a five-month restoration process in 2021 supported by Brazil's National Institute of Historic and Artistic Heritage, each piece was carefully wrapped in layers of foam and paper to protect the delicate wood during transport. The image of São Manuel, featuring a body pierced with nails, had been a centerpiece of devotion at the Parish of Nossa Senhora do Bom Sucesso in Caeté. Local communities have responded with pride rather than a sense of loss, with one cultural manager noting that the works are now in condition to cross borders and share Brazil's artistic history with other peoples. This exhibition offers readers a glimpse into how art can serve as a bridge between communities separated by an ocean. For the residents of small towns in Minas Gerais, seeing their sacred images honored at the Vatican creates a profound connection—as one local council member observed, it feels as though they themselves have made the journey to Rome. It's a quiet testament to how craft, faith, and cultural pride can transcend borders.

The golden disc with messages for extraterrestrials that Voyager 1 carries (and how they could decipher it)

El disco de oro con mensajes para los extraterrestres que transporta la sonda Voyager 1 (y cómo podrán descifrarlo)

Nearly five decades ago, NASA's Voyager 1 and 2 spacecraft embarked on an unprecedented journey carrying golden records—humanity's message in a bottle to the cosmos. This week, Voyager 1 is expected to become the farthest human-made object from Earth, carrying with it a disc designed to last a billion years, long after we might be gone. The mission itself was born from ingenious problem-solving. In the 1970s, rockets lacked the power to escape the Sun's gravitational pull, so graduate students proposed using planetary orbits as cosmic slingshots. The plan required a rare planetary alignment that occurs only once every 175 years—a window NASA seized. Astronomers Carl Sagan and Frank Drake saw this once-in-a-lifetime voyage as the perfect opportunity to reach beyond our solar system. They assembled a small team, led by design director Jon Lomberg, who had mere weeks and almost no budget to create something extraordinary. Unlike earlier attempts at extraterrestrial communication, the golden records could store an enormous amount of data—images, music, and information compressed onto a phonograph disc. What makes this story quietly remarkable is its ambitious optimism. Humanity crafted a time capsule not knowing if anyone will ever find it, or if they could decipher its contents. It's a gesture of hope and curiosity, a testament to our desire to be known and understood across the vast loneliness of space. The golden record represents both our technological ingenuity and our fundamentally human need to reach out and say: we were here, and this is who we are.

There's Silicon Valley - but ever heard of Superconductor Valley?

In New Zealand's Hutt Valley, a quiet hub of innovation is taking shape around superconducting technology — the science of moving electricity with almost no resistance while generating powerful magnetic fields. Since the late 1980s, researchers have been developing applications for this phenomenon, and now academics and industry leaders are calling their ecosystem "Superconductor Valley," a nod to Silicon Valley that's meant to represent a collaborative mindset as much as a physical place. The recent Superconductor Valley event in Wellington showcased where this technology is heading. Among the most tangible applications is hydrogen-electric aviation. Dana Goodwin of Crux Aerospace outlined plans for the Southern Hemisphere's first crewed hydrogen-electric flight next year, with commercial aircraft expected by 2031. Passengers would hardly notice the difference — flights might just be a bit quieter — but the environmental impact could be significant. Goodwin emphasized urgency: delays in developing this technology could cost New Zealand both economically and environmentally, while early success could position the country as a problem-solver the world turns to. Meanwhile, professor Randy Pollock discussed how superconducting technology could make space travel more fuel-efficient, reducing space debris and making orbital activities more sustainable. This story offers a refreshing glimpse at how specialized science, quietly pursued over decades, can suddenly converge into practical solutions for some of our most pressing challenges — from decarbonizing aviation to cleaning up space. In Zimbabwe, trusted local guardians help communities coexist with lions

In rural Zimbabwe, a conservation biologist has found a remarkably effective way to help people and lions live side by side. Moreangels Mbizah, founder of Wildlife Conservation Action, has pioneered a "community guardian" model that has reduced human-wildlife conflict by 98% in some areas over just six years. The key to this success lies in trust: local guardians, known as "Batabilili," come from the communities themselves—neighbors, siblings, children of farmers—making them credible advocates for both lion conservation and human safety. These guardians help farmers build secure livestock enclosures, track lion movements, and hold community meetings. But perhaps most importantly, they provide something often missing in conservation work: emotional support. When a conflict occurs, someone shows up who genuinely cares about the people affected, not just the wildlife. This simple act of acknowledgment has helped reduce retaliatory killings of lions, as community members no longer feel ignored by distant wildlife authorities. Mbizah's approach recognizes that effective conservation must address human needs alongside animal protection. Beyond her fieldwork, Mbizah mentors young female university students entering conservation—a path she found challenging as one of the few Black Zimbabwean women in the field when she started. Her efforts earned her a Whitley Award this year, sometimes called the "Green Oscar" of conservation. This story is worth attention because it demonstrates how conservation succeeds when it places community trust and human dignity at its center, creating lasting change through relationships rather than enforcement alone.

New Zealand-made superconductor returns from trip to space

A New Zealand-built superconducting magnet has returned to Earth after spending six months aboard the International Space Station, marking a quiet milestone in space propulsion research. Created by scientists at Victoria University's Paihau-Robinson Research Institute, the disk-shaped device was the first high-temperature superconducting magnet of its kind to be tested in orbit, part of the Hēki Space Mission that launched in September 2025. For Randy Pollock, the institute's chief scientist and engineer for space research, the homecoming was particularly meaningful—the first time in his 30-year career that his work physically returned from space. The magnet, designed to eventually help propel spacecraft using superpowered magnetic fields, performed well during its extended mission, managing its magnetic field and power consumption as Earth's shadow cycled past. The mission also carried cultural significance: named Hēki (egg in te reo Māori) and adorned with art by Māori artist Reweti Arapere, the protective cover told the story of how the kōkako bird got its blue wattle—a nod to the thruster's distinctive blue glow. While the six-month test couldn't replicate the 10 to 15 years required for long-duration missions, bringing the equipment home allows testing to continue on Earth. The story offers a gentle reminder that innovation often unfolds quietly, requiring patience and collaboration across borders. It's a tale of technical ambition grounded in cultural storytelling, where cutting-edge physics meets traditional narrative—and where something as seemingly mundane as slightly sun-browned plastic cables tells the story of a journey beyond our atmosphere.

Why conservation needs independent journalism (commentary)

Independent journalism serves as a vital but underfunded partner in conservation work, according to this commentary from Mongabay's founder. While billions flow annually into conservation fieldwork and policy advocacy, far less supports the kind of investigative reporting that can expose problems, amplify community voices, and hold decision-makers accountable. A 2021 investigation into a Malaysian carbon credit deal illustrates the point. When Mongabay received a tip about Sabah officials negotiating a 2-million-hectare rainforest project with an inexperienced Singapore company—without meaningful consultation with Indigenous communities whose ancestral lands were affected—their reporting brought the opaque deal into public view just as Malaysian delegates prepared to market credits at a U.N. climate conference. The subsequent attention helped derail those plans, sparked legal action by Indigenous leaders, and ultimately led to the agreement being blocked by 2024. The journalists didn't save the forest themselves, but they created the transparency that allowed others to act. The commentary argues that conservation philanthropy's focus on quantifiable metrics—hectares conserved, trees planted, animals counted—misses something essential. These numbers reveal outcomes but obscure the decision-making processes, power dynamics, and community engagement that determine whether conservation efforts are just and sustainable. Independent reporting fills that gap, especially in places where governance is weak and information scarce. The story offers a quiet reminder that sometimes the most effective conservation tool isn't a scientific instrument or a policy document, but a journalist willing to ask difficult questions and a source brave enough to trust them with answers.

Your gut bacteria helps your body keep track of time, and when you eat influences how much nutrition you get from food

Scientists have discovered that the trillions of microbes living in the human gut serve as biological clocks, coordinating when and how efficiently the body absorbs nutrients from food. This research, published in Cell Host & Microbe, reveals that gut bacteria don't rely on light like the brain does—instead, they use feeding times as their signal to synchronize the body's digestive rhythms. The mechanism centers on phytate, a compound found in grains, beans, nuts, and seeds. Gut microbes metabolize phytate and convert it into molecules that activate nutrient transport genes in intestinal cells. When we eat, phytate levels rise and microbial activity increases, boosting nutrient absorption. During fasting, the opposite occurs. In experiments with mice, this microbial timekeeping system proved remarkably important: undernourished young mice given specific gut bacteria or their metabolic products grew taller and healthier than those without, suggesting the system evolved to help maximize nutrition during times of scarcity. But there's a catch. The same mechanism that helped ancestors survive lean times can backfire in today's calorie-abundant environment. Mice fed high-calorie diets experienced accelerated obesity when this microbial pathway remained active, absorbing excess fat more efficiently than their bodies needed. The finding illustrates what researchers call "evolutionary mismatch"—a biological adaptation that once offered survival advantages now contributing to modern health challenges like obesity. This quiet revelation about the hidden timekeepers in our intestines offers a fresh perspective on why meal timing matters and how deeply our ancient microbial partners shape our metabolism, for better or worse.

Red crabs form crimson carpet in migration on Christmas Island; SEE

Caranguejos formam tapete vermelho em migração na Ilha Christmas; VEJA

On a remote Australian island in the Indian Ocean, the annual journey of millions of red crabs has transformed roads and trails into a living crimson carpet. Christmas Island, home to an estimated population of over 100 million red crabs, is witnessing its famous seasonal migration as the endemic crustaceans march from the island's interior toward the sea. Images released by Parks Australia this week capture the remarkable scene: waves of bright red crabs moving across rocks, lawns, and temporarily closed roads in a spectacle that draws visitors and researchers from around the world. The migration is driven by reproduction. Male and female crabs make the trek to the ocean where mating occurs. Females then remain in burrows near the beach to incubate their eggs before releasing them into the sea—each capable of depositing up to 100,000 eggs. Meanwhile, males return inland. To protect the crabs during this critical period, authorities close portions of roadways and restrict vehicle traffic, a coordinated effort managed by the national park that covers much of the island. This story offers a window into one of nature's most dramatic mass movements, a reminder that even small creatures can command an entire landscape when acting in concert. It's a vivid example of ecological balance and the lengths taken to preserve it—proof that coexistence between human activity and wild rhythms is both possible and worth witnessing.

Dazzling Leonid meteor shower to peak on Tuesday night

The Leonid meteor shower reaches its annual peak this Tuesday night, offering sky watchers across the UK a chance to witness one of nature's faster celestial displays. These streaking lights are debris from Comet 55P/Tempel-Tuttle, entering Earth's atmosphere at speeds up to 44 miles per second—making them the fastest meteors visible from our planet. Observers may spot between 10 and 15 meteors per hour during the peak viewing window. Timing and location matter for catching the show. The meteors appear to radiate from the constellation Leo, which doesn't rise until around midnight, making the hours between midnight and dawn ideal for watching. No special equipment is needed—just the naked eye, though experts recommend allowing 20 to 30 minutes for eyes to adjust to darkness. Finding a dark spot away from street lights will improve the experience considerably. This year's peak comes with a viewing advantage: the Moon sets around 11:30 PM, reducing light pollution in the critical post-midnight hours. Weather will play the deciding role in who sees what. The far north of Scotland, including Orkney and Shetland, is forecast to have the clearest skies with only broken cloud. Parts of Northern Ireland, Wales, and southern England will see intermittent clear spells mixed with cloudier periods. For anyone willing to brave a November night, this story offers a reminder that some of the cosmos's quiet wonders require nothing more than patience, darkness, and looking up at the right moment.

Quick wallaby outruns fox in rare Queensland beach chase

Wildlife volunteers monitoring sea turtle nests at Haliday Bay in north Queensland witnessed an unexpected drama: a fox chasing a wallaby across the beach in broad daylight. The pursuit, captured on video, lasted several minutes as the wallaby zigzagged across the sand, narrowly escaping the smaller predator's jaws. After an initial retreat, the fox made a second attempt, closing in on the marsupial before ultimately giving up and vanishing into the brush. For the volunteers from Mackay and District Turtle Watch, the boldness of the daytime hunt was particularly striking. While foxes do hunt wallabies, wildlife biologist Ben Allen notes such encounters typically occur at night, and larger wallabies usually prove too challenging for foxes, which prefer smaller prey like mice, rats, and bandicoots. This marks the first documented interaction between a fox and wallaby at Haliday Bay, an area near Cape Hillsborough National Park known for its beach-visiting marsupials. Beyond the dramatic chase, the sighting highlights a growing concern: foxes are increasingly threatening coastal ecosystems in the region. A 2022 necropsy found sea turtle hatchling remains in a fox's stomach, and conservationists worry about vulnerable nesting turtles becoming easy targets. This encounter offers more than wildlife spectacle—it reveals the quiet expansion of an invasive species with an estimated 1.7 million foxes now living across Australia. As fox populations shift northward, aided by growing rabbit numbers and abundant coastal food sources, their impact on native species intensifies. The story captures a moment where predator and prey cross paths in unexpected daylight, reminding us how invasive species reshape the landscapes they inhabit.
