Day or night, Jasper reveals attractions in all seasons of the year

De dia ou à noite, Jasper revela atrações em todas as estações do ano

Jasper National Park, nestled in the northern reaches of the Canadian Rockies, offers a rare combination of dramatic mountain landscapes and some of the darkest skies in North America. Spanning over 11,000 square kilometers of protected wilderness in Alberta, the park has been part of a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1984. What sets Jasper apart from many destinations is its year-round appeal—equally compelling under daylight and after dark, with experiences that shift beautifully with the seasons. During winter and spring, visitors can ski at Marmot Basin, explore the ice-sculpted walls of Maligne Canyon, and spot wildlife like elk and wolves drawn to lower elevations by snow. Summer and fall open up access to glacial lakes and high-altitude trails, including boat tours to Spirit Island on Maligne Lake, a site held sacred by Indigenous peoples. But when the sun sets, Jasper transforms into something entirely different: Canada's largest accessible Dark Sky Preserve. Recognized in 2011 for its efforts to reduce light pollution, the park offers exceptional conditions for stargazing, aurora viewing, and astrophotography, especially during the long, clear nights of winter. This story is worth a reader's time because it quietly celebrates a place that refuses to be just one thing. Jasper reminds us that nature's rhythms don't pause for convenience—they invite us to return, again and again, to witness the same landscape under entirely new light.

Can we stop ships from killing the Mediterranean’s last great whales?

The Mediterranean Sea, despite accounting for less than one percent of the world's ocean surface, hosts fifteen percent of global shipping activity—and this congestion is proving deadly for the region's endangered sperm and fin whales. Marine biologist Lisa Stanzani has witnessed the grim reality firsthand: standing over a young sperm whale in Ostia, Italy, she identified the telltale propeller wound that had caused its slow, agonizing death. The animal had been spotted injured weeks earlier, illustrating how vessel strikes often don't kill instantly but leave whales suffering before they succumb. Both whale species face particular vulnerability in Mediterranean waters. Sperm whales sleep vertically just below the surface, their brains half-inactive, making it difficult to detect approaching vessels. Analysis of 2025 vessel tracking data reveals nearly 2,800 large ships traveled above fifteen knots—the speed threshold at which strikes become almost always fatal—through key whale habitats including the Pelagos Sanctuary and Hellenic Trench. The stakes extend beyond individual animals: these slow-maturing giants play outsized ecological roles, fertilizing phytoplankton through nutrient-rich waste and sequestering carbon in their bodies throughout their long lives. Conservationists describe them as crucial warriors against climate change, supporting organisms that produce half the world's oxygen. This story matters because it reveals how invisible maritime corridors intersect with wildlife in ways most of us never consider. Emerging solutions—speed restrictions, rerouted shipping lanes, and whale-alert systems—offer hope that we might share these ancient waterways more carefully with creatures that have inhabited them far longer than our cargo ships have crossed them.

The bombarding of childhood

A researcher observing a young boy at an airport watching cartoons on an iPad notices something striking: the child's body is still, but his eyes dart frantically across the screen as scenes, emotions, and characters shift in rapid succession. This moment crystallizes a concern that has been largely overlooked in discussions about children's media—not what children watch, but how quickly they're forced to process it. While debates about screen time typically focus on content quality, educational value, or age-appropriateness, this essay argues that pace may be equally consequential. Even programs with genuinely rich subject matter often rush from joke to song to visual shift before a child has time to absorb any single idea. Different media make different temporal demands, but the crucial question is whether the pace allows children to actually engage with what's being presented: to follow a story, understand a concept, identify with a character, or process an emotion. When a cartoon character's tears instantly transform into a smile, or when a lesson about how eyes work blazes past in a flurry of stimulation, children may register the surface without grasping the deeper patterns—the anticipation that makes a joke funny, or the emotional repair that teaches empathy. This essay offers a quiet but radical reframing: pace isn't a minor design choice, but a statement about how we imagine childhood itself. Are children consumers to be retained through constant stimulation, or people whose capacity for attention is still forming and needs time to arrive? It's a question worth sitting with—slowly.

Biodiversity corridor payments boost household incomes in Vietnam’s Annamite mountains

In Vietnam's misty Annamite mountains, a conservation experiment is quietly rewriting assumptions about protected areas and the people who live within them. Researchers studying two biodiversity corridors—stretches of land designed to connect isolated nature reserves—found that households living inside these corridors earned substantially more income than their neighbors just outside the boundaries. The difference wasn't accidental: families within the corridors received payments for protecting forests and maintaining ecosystem services like clean water and healthy pollinator populations. The study, which interviewed 120 families across four villages, revealed a trade-off that seems to be working. While corridor residents earned less from direct forest extraction due to land-use restrictions, conservation-linked payments more than compensated for the loss. Outside the corridors, households relied more heavily on plantations and harvesting forest resources. The two corridors, established in 2018, cover nearly 2,000 square kilometers of watershed forests and valleys, linking several flagship protected areas near the Laos border. This connectivity is crucial for some of the region's most elusive wildlife, including the rarely seen saola ox and the large-antlered muntjac—species so difficult to study that maintaining vast, connected habitats may be their best hope. This story matters because it offers a practical answer to one of conservation's thorniest questions: how to protect wild places without impoverishing the people who live there. The findings suggest that when designed thoughtfully, financial incentives can align ecological health with human wellbeing, turning potential conflict into partnership. It's a model worth watching, quietly proving that conservation and community prosperity need not be at odds.

A clownfish’s last stand

Twenty years ago, a scientist dove into the waters off Singapore to document a coral reef before it disappeared forever. Among the vibrant life at Terumbu Bayan, she encountered a false clownfish fiercely defending her eggs, charging and biting the diver despite the size difference. Minutes after the team surfaced, barges began dumping rock and fill into the channel, burying the reef—and the clownfish family—under sediment and stone. Today, satellite images show only industrial towers and roads where the reef once thrived. This memory has resurfaced as Singapore considers merging several southern islands into a new "Western Island" for industry and infrastructure, raising concerns about popular dive sites like Pulau Hantu. Singapore has already lost about 65% of its coral reef habitat and more than 45% of its seagrass meadows to coastal development, even as the nation's land area has grown by over a fifth. Underwater visibility has plummeted from ten meters in the 1960s to less than two today, and the types of corals that can survive have shifted, affecting the broader marine ecosystem. This story matters because it offers a rare glimpse beneath the surface of development—a reminder that land reclamation doesn't just reshape coastlines but erases entire living communities. The Himalayas are melting faster - and India's economy is 'at risk'

The Himalayas are experiencing an accelerating crisis that few are fully tracking. New research reveals that glaciers across the world's highest mountain range are now losing mass 65% faster than they were just a decade ago, yet only about 21 of an estimated 40,000 glaciers in the region are being consistently monitored. This surveillance gap comes at a particularly dangerous moment: as glaciers retreat, they leave behind unstable lakes dammed by rock and ice, while warming temperatures destabilize permafrost and mountain slopes, creating conditions for cascading disasters. The human cost is already visible. Recent floods in Nepal and Tibet killed more than 1,300 people, while glacial lake outburst floods in India's Sikkim and Uttarakhand states in 2023 and 2021 claimed hundreds of lives and destroyed critical infrastructure. The Indian government now classifies 56 glacial lakes as "very high risk." Beyond catastrophic floods, a quieter threat looms: most Himalayan river basins are expected to reach "peak water" by mid-century, after which glacier runoff will decline as ice reserves diminish. These rivers sustain hundreds of millions of people across South Asia, underpinning an estimated 20% of India's GDP through agriculture, hydropower, and other industries. What makes this story particularly significant is the combination of scale and agency. Economist Lord Nicholas Stern calls the Himalayas "the Third Pole," but unlike the Arctic or Antarctic, this one has hundreds of millions living in its shadow. The research offers a glimmer of actionable hope: roughly a third of glacier melt comes from black carbon—soot from sources like brick kilns—a pollution stream that regional governments can directly address, unlike diffuse global emissions.

The exceptional cases of people immune to HIV (and what role they play in a possible cure)

For over three decades, Loreen Willenberg defied what scientists understood about HIV. The California landscape designer tested positive in 1992, yet the virus remained dormant in her body without medication, allowing her to live a normal life. Willenberg belonged to an extraordinary group called "elite controllers"—roughly 0.5% of HIV-positive individuals whose immune systems somehow keep the virus suppressed without treatment. Her case became even more remarkable when, after a stage IV cancer diagnosis in 2022, aggressive immunosuppressive therapy failed to reactivate her HIV. When researchers examined her cells, they found no detectable trace of the virus. In 2025, Professor Xu Yu announced at the International AIDS Society conference that Willenberg had likely cleared HIV from her body entirely—a stunning achievement against one of the century's most devastating infectious diseases. Though Willenberg passed away in April 2026 from cancer, her legacy offers profound hope. Another elite controller, known as "Patient Esperanza" from Argentina, is also believed to be cured, suggesting that under rare circumstances, the human immune system can eradicate HIV on its own. This story matters because it transforms what once seemed impossible into a roadmap for the future. Scientists are now studying the biology of elite controllers, searching for clues that could lead to next-generation treatments for the 40.8 million people living with HIV worldwide. Willenberg's exceptional immune response may hold the key to developing a cure—proof that the body's own defenses, when understood deeply enough, might teach us how to win battles we've long thought unwinnable.

This question leads to a fascinating exploration of warm-blooded versus cold-blooded animals, and the evolutionary journey that separates them. The terms themselves—"cold-blooded" and "warm-blooded"—are somewhat misleading. Cold-blooded animals, or ectotherms, regulate their temperature externally, relying on their environment. Warm-blooded creatures, or endotherms, generate heat internally. A desert lizard basking in the sun can actually be warmer than many mammals, making the labels relative rather than absolute. The language of "cold blood" has curious roots in ancient Greek medicine, particularly the Theory of Humors proposed by Hipocrates and Galen. They believed bodily fluids controlled temperament, and that blood literally heated up during strong emotions—hence expressions like "blood boiling." Writers in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries adopted this medical metaphor to describe human behavior, coining phrases like acting "in cold blood" long before scientists formally classified reptiles and amphibians as cold-blooded in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. All animals originally descended from ectothermic fish, but some evolved fur or feathers that enabled them to retain body heat, giving rise to endotherms—still a minority among Earth's creatures. This story offers a window into how evolution shaped our biology and how ancient medical theories continue to color our everyday language. It's a quiet reminder that understanding our own bodies connects us to both natural history and cultural heritage, revealing the remarkable systems that keep us functioning optimally at a precise temperature.
