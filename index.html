Three researchers studying brain diseases win the Nobel Prize in Medicine

Três pesquisadores que estudam doenças do cérebro ganham o Prêmio Nobel de Medicina

The 2026 Nobel Prize in Medicine celebrates a discovery that began with a deceptively simple question: how does a single-celled alga always swim toward light? Nearly three decades ago, German scientist Peter Hegemann and colleague Georg Nagel found the answer in a light-sensitive protein within the alga. What seemed like a modest curiosity about pond life would spark a revolution in how we understand the brain. American researcher Karl Deisseroth took the discovery further by introducing this protein into rat neurons, making them responsive to light. By shining light through an optical fiber inserted into the skull, he could trigger precise reactions—like the movement of a mouse's whiskers. This breakthrough birthed optogenetics, a technique that allows scientists to switch specific nerve cells on and off with remarkable precision. For the first time, researchers could test exactly what happens when individual neurons fire or fall silent, moving beyond identifying broad brain regions to understanding the role of specific cells in memory, emotion, and behavior. This story matters because it shows how fundamental curiosity can lead to transformative applications. Optogenetics is now being explored for medical uses, including psychiatric disorder research and efforts to restore vision in blind individuals. Hegemann's advice to young scientists—stay curious and work on something new—echoes through this achievement. It's a reminder that today's questions about the smallest organisms might unlock tomorrow's solutions to some of humanity's most complex challenges.

Tooling up to stop the next 'massive weed' before it gets started

New Zealand has launched a free digital tool that could transform how the country tackles invasive plants before they become unstoppable problems. The Smart Weed Alert Tool, or SWAT, automatically scans thousands of daily plant observations from local and international databases, alerting users when potentially dangerous species appear in new locations. Its creator, Dr. Kate McAlpine from the Department of Conservation, believes the system might have stopped notorious invaders like tradescantia—commonly known as Wandering Willie—if it had existed decades ago. The open-source platform allows anyone to set customized alerts for specific weed types and locations, then continuously monitors biodiversity databases for new sightings. When a concerning plant turns up somewhere unexpected, SWAT notifies relevant users—whether they're councils, community groups, or museum curators. Te Papa's botany curator, for instance, was recently alerted to a wetland fern appearing in Taranaki, far from its usual northern range. The tool provides detailed, zoomable maps that help groups decide which invasive species to prioritize in their area. With 25,000 exotic plants currently sitting in New Zealand gardens, any of which could escape into the wild, early detection matters enormously. What makes this story quietly remarkable is its elegant simplicity: using existing data in a smarter way to address a billion-dollar problem. SWAT represents the kind of practical innovation that catches international attention—McAlpine will present it at biodiversity talks in Armenia just days after launch. In a world where environmental challenges can feel overwhelming, here's a tool that empowers ordinary people to become early responders in protecting their local ecosystems, one unwanted plant at a time.

Women suffer more pain than men across the whole body, major global study finds

A sweeping global study has revealed that women report experiencing more pain than men across every part of the body researchers examined. Drawing on data from more than 6 million people spanning 118 countries and collected over 35 years, the research represents one of the most comprehensive efforts yet to understand how pain is distributed across populations and lifespans. The findings add weight to ongoing concerns about a gender pain gap, where women often receive less adequate pain treatment than men. The study tracked pain across eleven bodily sites, from head to knee, and found women consistently reported higher pain levels, particularly for headaches, facial discomfort, and abdominal pain. For both genders, pain increased most steeply before age 55 and peaked at or beyond 75, with back pain affecting roughly 40% of participants. Interestingly, different types of pain followed distinct patterns: headaches and abdominal pain peaked earlier in life before declining, while lower-body musculoskeletal pain continued rising with age. The research also uncovered significant global inequities, with people in lower-income regions experiencing nearly twice the rate of low back pain in older age. Lifestyle factors including smoking, obesity, and low household income accounted for about 18% of the global pain burden, though this varied considerably by region. What makes this study quietly remarkable is its focus on self-reported pain rather than medical diagnoses alone, potentially capturing experiences of people who lack healthcare access. By mapping when different types of pain emerge and intensify across a lifetime, the research offers a foundation for targeting prevention and care where they're needed most—a small but significant step toward understanding pain more equitably across gender, age, and geography.

Today, it stands as a rare anchor of place in a city often designed to resemble elsewhere for filming purposes. Griffith Park itself has been designated a Significant Ecological Area by UCLA researchers, serving as a vital corridor connecting nearby mountain ranges and supporting approximately 200 bird species, 50 butterfly species, and 40 mammal species. Scientists are now preparing to equip the letters with AI-powered cameras to detect wildfire smoke, addressing the area's greatest risk as climate pressures mount. This story reminds us that even in one of America's densest urban landscapes, nature persists and adapts in surprising ways. The Hollywood sign has become more than a cultural icon—it's a quiet testament to the resilience of wildlife and the unexpected sanctuaries that emerge when humans step back, even briefly, from the spaces they've claimed.

‘The future is bright’: breeding programme brings world’s heaviest insect back from the brink

New Zealand's wētāpunga, a cricket-like creature believed to be the world's heaviest insect, is making a remarkable comeback. Thanks to an ambitious fifteen-year breeding programme, more than 11,000 of these extraordinary insects have been released into the wild, prompting conservationists to upgrade their status from "nationally endangered" to "nationally recovering." Female wētāpunga can weigh as much as a mouse, with bodies reaching 10 centimeters in length and leg spans stretching to 20 centimeters. Once widespread across New Zealand's North Island, the wētāpunga fell victim to introduced predators and habitat loss, becoming presumed extinct on the mainland by the 1990s. Their survival hinged on small populations clinging to predator-free islands. But two institutions—Butterfly Creek wildlife facility and Auckland Zoo—developed innovative husbandry techniques to breed the insects in large communal groups. The secret to their success includes a carefully balanced diet of fresh foliage, fish flake protein, and leaf litter, with the protein proving critical for the youngest nymphs. Once mature enough, the insects are relocated to protected habitats across 45 separate sites. Monitoring reveals that populations on at least two islands are now self-sustaining, with fourth and fifth generations thriving independently. Dr. Chris Green, who documented the programme's success, believes this represents a rare conservation victory in a country struggling with species decline due to high endemism and invasive pests. The story offers something quietly hopeful: proof that thoughtful, patient conservation work can reverse what seemed inevitable, and a blueprint that other institutions might follow to protect their own threatened species.

To cull or not to cull: Greenland debates fate of world’s slowest shark

In the waters off Greenland's west coast, an ancient conflict is coming to a head. Fishers targeting halibut and cod are losing thousands of hooks and tens of thousands of kroner each season to Greenland sharks—slow-moving giants that can live over 400 years and grow up to seven meters long. Earlier this year, the Avannaata municipality approved a controversial plan to cull 1,000 of these remarkable creatures, offering bounties for each shark killed. The plan was eventually dropped after the government questioned its legality, but the tension between livelihoods and conservation remains unresolved. The Greenland shark is one of the least understood animals on Earth: the world's slowest shark, the second-largest carnivorous species after the great white, and possibly the longest-lived vertebrate known to science. They don't reach sexual maturity until around 150 years old, which means populations recover extraordinarily slowly from any decline. Marine conservationists warn that culling would be disastrous for a species still recovering from intensive fishing that ended in the 1960s. Meanwhile, conservation groups argue that culls wouldn't even solve the problem—migratory sharks from elsewhere would simply move in to replace those killed. This story offers a window into one of conservation's most difficult dilemmas: how communities balance immediate economic needs with the protection of species whose timescales dwarf our own. It's a reminder that some of the planet's most ancient creatures are still navigating a world rapidly reshaped by human activity, and that finding solutions requires understanding both the biology of four-century-old sharks and the realities of fishers losing their gear.

There are only six of these trees left in the wild. Conservationists are cloning them

Deep in the forests just south of Canberra, six ancient eucalyptus trees represent the last survivors of an ice-age species. The Eucalyptus recurva, known as the Mongarlowe mallee or ice-age gum, is so rare that only a handful of people have ever laid eyes on it. The largest specimens are estimated to be between 3,000 and 6,000 years old, their survival spanning millennia while the world around them transformed. Conservationists face a delicate challenge: the six remaining trees are too far apart to naturally reproduce, and they produce no wild seeds. Over decades, teams from the Australian National Botanic Gardens and NSW's Saving Our Species program have been painstakingly cloning the trees by grafting wild cuttings onto hybrid rootstock. They've successfully created multiple copies of four of the six wild plants, establishing a crucial insurance population. The next hurdle is even more intricate—getting the cloned trees to flower and produce genetically pure seeds. Flowering is unpredictable and can take up to twenty years, with individual trees blooming at different times. The team is experimenting with growth regulators while preparing to hand-pollinate the flowers and protect them from insects that might introduce pollen from other eucalyptus species, creating unwanted hybrids. This story offers a glimpse into the patient, meticulous work of conservation—a reminder that saving a species sometimes means committing to a project that will outlast careers and span generations. It's a quiet testament to the determination of people who refuse to let Australia's rarest eucalyptus slip into extinction without a fight.

Peter the Great famously spent a month there in 1717, drinking over twenty cups daily to treat digestive ailments. The transition to bathing began in the mid-16th century, and by the 1700s, Spa had reinvented itself as one of Europe's most fashionable resorts. This story offers a fascinating glimpse into how a Belgian hillside town became synonymous with self-care worldwide. "Mitbestimmung": how Germany requires large companies to give workers a voice in decision-making (and what impact it has on the economy)

Germany has a distinctive approach to corporate governance that might surprise observers from other market economies. Under a system called "Mitbestimmung"—or co-determination—German law requires large companies with more than 2,000 employees to give workers half the seats on their supervisory boards. These aren't token positions: employee representatives, chosen through direct election, sit alongside shareholder representatives to make crucial decisions about company direction, leadership appointments, and long-term strategy. Giants like Volkswagen, Mercedes-Benz, and Deutsche Bank all operate under this model, holding four mandatory board meetings each year, scheduled a year in advance. The system is designed to encourage consensus rather than confrontation. Before official board meetings, informal preliminary discussions allow workers and shareholders to negotiate separately, then come together with compromise solutions already in mind. Workers might agree to restructuring in exchange for investment commitments or employment guarantees.
