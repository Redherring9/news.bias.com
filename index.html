'Pretty cool' fish with see-through head discovered in Fiordland

In the deep, dark waters of New Zealand's Fiordland lakes, a researcher has discovered something unexpected: tiny, translucent fish living far deeper than anyone thought possible. Dr. Roe Stuart from the University of Otago used a two-kilometer line baited with Marmite—yes, the savory spread—to lure specimens from depths of 460 meters in Lakes Manapouri and Te Anau. The find is remarkable not just for the depth, but because it shatters previous records; before this, fish had only been found as deep as 90 meters in New Zealand's freshwater lakes. The fish themselves are delightfully odd. About the size of whitebait, they sport enormous, bulging eyes and large sensory holes in their snouts, adaptations for navigating a world with almost no light. Some specimens are so transparent that their brains are visible through the tops of their heads. The lack of pigment makes sense in an environment where sunlight never reaches, and Dr. Stuart finds them endearing—"cute," even. DNA testing is underway to confirm whether this is indeed a new species, and if so, the researcher hopes to name it "Galaxis googly eye." What makes this discovery particularly significant is what it suggests about the unseen. An entire ecosystem may exist in these abyssal lake depths, complete with connections and interactions scientists hadn't previously considered. Dr. Stuart plans to send cameras down next, curious about what larger creatures might be lurking even deeper. This story is worth a reader's time because it's a reminder that even in well-studied places, mystery persists—and sometimes all it takes to unlock it is curiosity, a very long fishing line, and a jar of Marmite.

Tasmanian devils lived alongside humans in Pilbara for millennia, Juukan Gorge fossils reveal

Fossils discovered at Juukan Gorge in Western Australia's Pilbara region reveal that Tasmanian devils once thrived alongside humans in the arid northwest for tens of thousands of years. The ancient rock shelter, tragically destroyed by Rio Tinto in 2020, yielded jaw and teeth fragments dating between 7,000 and 46,000 years old—filling a significant gap in our understanding of where these now-iconic marsupials once lived. The findings challenge common perceptions of devils as creatures suited only to Tasmania's cool, wet forests, showing instead their remarkable adaptability to diverse environments, from tropical wetlands to ice-age aridity. What makes this discovery particularly fascinating is the evidence of a long relationship between devils and people. Bite marks and acid-digested bones suggest the marsupials regularly scavenged human camp scraps, acting as a "garbage compactor on four legs" that helped keep settlements clean and disease-free. As human populations grew over millennia, devils may have become increasingly reliant on these camps as a food source—a mutually beneficial arrangement that persisted through dramatic climate shifts. The partnership likely ended only with the arrival of dingos around 3,200 years ago, which may have displaced devils as the primary scavengers both in camps and in the wild. This research offers a poignant reminder of what was lost when Juukan Gorge was destroyed. The site contained an irreplaceable record of coexistence—not just devil fossils, but stone tools, shell beads, and even a human hair belt—illustrating how people, animals, and landscapes evolved together. It's a story of adaptation, interdependence, and the deep connections that can form between species over time.

Why it took over a century for Auckland to get its CRL

Auckland's City Rail Link, opening this weekend after a decade of construction, represents the culmination of a vision first proposed more than a century ago. The underground rail tunnel has been on the drawing board since 1912, when the Northern Railway League first suggested connecting Auckland to Morningside. Railway historian Graeme Carter notes that remarkably, subsequent proposals in 1918 and 1923 all followed essentially the same route that trains will now travel. The project's long delay reflects decades of policy choices favoring motorways over public transit. From the 1950s through the 1990s, Auckland looked to Los Angeles as a model and deliberately allowed its commuter rail system to deteriorate while investing heavily in roads. The city came closest to underground rail in the mid-1970s with "Robbies Rapid Rail," an ambitious plan that would have connected the airport and North Shore, but the Muldoon government canceled it in favor of more motorways. It wasn't until Mayor Len Brown secured agreement with Prime Minister John Key's government that construction finally began, though the journey proved turbulent, with costs rising from an initial $3.4 billion to $5.5 billion and significant disruption to downtown businesses. Despite the painful gestation, even skeptics like former Transport Minister Simon Bridges—now leading Auckland's Chamber of Commerce—believe the link will prove transformative. Brown's reflection offers a quiet lesson in urban planning: inevitable infrastructure only grows more expensive with delay. After 112 years of proposals, postponements, and finally progress, Auckland is about to discover what it might have had generations earlier.

Feather discovered in fossilised dinosaur poo is first of its kind

A palaeontologist crawling up a rocky outcrop in Montana made an unexpected discovery: a beautifully preserved feather entombed in fossilised dinosaur faeces. The find, dating back 66 million years to the end of the dinosaur age, represents the first feather ever discovered in a coprolite and offers an unusually pristine 3D specimen rather than the typical flat impressions found in rock. What began as a routine search for fish fossils has opened a surprising new avenue for understanding ancient life. Micro-CT scanning revealed the coprolite contained not just one feather but multiple feathers, tiny fish scales, and small bird bones. The researchers believe a large predator—possibly a Tyrannosaurus rex—consumed a small aquatic bird that had recently eaten fish, capturing two different food web interactions in a single fossil. The feather likely belonged to a hesperornithiform, a type of flightless diving bird that didn't survive the mass extinction event. Interestingly, these birds were closely related to modern birds but lacked certain plumage characteristics that their surviving cousins possessed. This discovery hints at why some bird lineages endured while others perished during the catastrophic event that ended the age of dinosaurs. The researchers suggest that advanced feather structures may have provided crucial advantages for survival. Perhaps most intriguingly, they're calling on scientists to scan other fossilised faeces—specimens that have been collected for over a century from sites like Hell Creek—wondering what other hidden treasures might be waiting inside. It's a reminder that even the most humble fossils can preserve extraordinary stories about life, death, and the fragile thread connecting ancient creatures to the birds outside our windows today.

Medieval mental health

A 12th-century account of a bishop performing an exorcism on a restrained, convulsing man seems to confirm every stereotype about medieval superstition and cruelty toward mental illness. But looking beyond dramatic tales of demonic possession reveals a surprisingly nuanced medieval understanding of mental health—one that resonates with contemporary concerns in unexpected ways. Medieval medical texts, particularly popular health guides called regimens, placed emotional wellness at the center of physical health. Working within the humoral system—which sought balance among four bodily fluids—physicians identified six external factors affecting health, including diet, exercise, sleep, and crucially, the emotions. Fourteenth-century doctors warned that anger, worry, and irritation could severely harm the body, while emotional dysregulation disrupted eating, exercise, and rest. These weren't abstract theories: in 1395, an Italian merchant attributed his high fever directly to getting upset over election results. The advice sounds strikingly modern—manage your stress, guard your emotional equilibrium, or face physical consequences. This story matters because it challenges our assumptions about the past and present alike. Yes, medieval people sometimes interpreted mental distress through a supernatural lens, but they also developed sophisticated frameworks linking emotional and physical health centuries before modern psychology. Their recognition that responsibility and status increased health risks, and that feelings directly impacted bodily wellness, echoes today's conversations about stress, work-life balance, and the mind-body connection. The medieval world may have lacked our vocabulary and treatments, but the fundamental insight—that mental health is inseparable from overall wellbeing—was already there, quietly documented in medical texts across Europe.

Japan wants to plant no-pollen trees to solve its hay fever crisis

Japan is grappling with an unexpected legacy of its post-World War II rebuilding efforts: a nationwide hay fever epidemic affecting more than 40% of its population. The culprits are two fast-growing tree species—sugi and hinoki—that were planted extensively to provide timber for millions of homes destroyed during the war. Today, these trees dominate two-thirds of Japan's cultivated forests, releasing pollen that triggers months of sneezing, itchy eyes, and runny noses for millions of people from January through early May. The problem has intensified with climate change, as warming temperatures cause sugi trees to release pollen earlier and extend the allergy season. In response, the Japanese government unveiled an ambitious plan in 2023 to cut down 20% of sugi trees across 10.6 million acres of forest by 2033. Trees in accessible locations will be replaced with new pollen-free or low-pollen strains developed by Japanese researchers—more than 30 varieties now exist. Remote forest areas will be replanted with other native species. One prefecture has already planted 100,000 no-pollen saplings in a single year. Yet significant hurdles remain. Japan's forestry industry faces severe labor shortages, making large-scale planting difficult. Meanwhile, the decline of the home construction industry has left officials searching for new markets for the harvested timber. This story captures a fascinating intersection of environmental history, public health, and innovation—showing how well-intentioned decisions can create unforeseen consequences generations later, and how a nation is working to literally replant its way to a solution.

Led by Trinidad and Tobago native Diva Amon, a deep-sea ecologist, the monthlong expedition aboard the research vessel Falkor (too) explored waters that remain largely inaccessible—nearly 93% of the country's marine territory lies beyond recreational diving depth. The team's findings were remarkable: they mapped over 11,000 square kilometers of previously uncharted seafloor, discovered 25 cold seep ecosystems formed by methane and gases rising from the ocean floor, and collected more than 700 specimens. Amon estimates at least 20 will prove to be new species, including potentially new genera—among them a Graneledone octopus she first spotted in 2014 but could finally collect and genetically analyze. The expedition also captured vivid moments of deep-sea life, including a black swallower fish with a grotesquely distended stomach from swallowing prey larger than itself, and crabs and worms feeding on a Mobula ray carcass on the seafloor. Located near Chan Chan, the former capital of the Chimú kingdom about 500 kilometers north of Lima, the discovery offers a rare window into pre-Incan burial practices and elite society along Peru's Pacific coast. The burial platform, constructed from mud brick and measuring 17 meters on each side and five meters high, contains five chambers housing the remains of at least 38 individuals. Unlike many previous finds from the colonial period that were looted or destroyed, this tomb remains as it was originally arranged, allowing researchers to study burial patterns and artifacts in their original context. For archaeologists seeking to understand how the Chimú honored their elite and organized their society, this undisturbed site represents an exceptional opportunity—a time capsule offering authentic insights into a sophisticated civilization that thrived centuries before European contact.

The trail of clues that our planet might be heating faster than we realised

A heated social media debate over climate science has inadvertently highlighted a fascinating and concerning wrinkle in our understanding of global warming. While carbon dioxide from fossil fuels is undeniably the primary driver of climate change, those same fuels also produce sulphur pollution—tiny particles that reflect sunlight back into space and have been quietly masking some of the warming we might otherwise have experienced. As countries implement clean-air policies to reduce harmful sulphur emissions, scientists are discovering that the loss of this unintended cooling effect may be accelerating the pace of warming. Researchers like Reto Knutti at ETH Zurich have been working to separate natural temperature fluctuations from the underlying warming trend, and their findings suggest the rate of warming has indeed picked up speed. Sulphate aerosols, though harmful to human health and responsible for acid rain, act like dust on greenhouse windows—reducing the amount of sunlight that reaches Earth's surface. They also brighten clouds and make them last longer, further cooling the planet. For decades, this pollution has been partly counterbalancing the heat-trapping effects of carbon dioxide, but as we clean up our air, that accidental shield is disappearing while greenhouse gas emissions remain near record highs. This story matters because it reveals an unsettling paradox: doing the right thing for air quality may be exposing us to faster warming than climate models initially projected. It's a reminder that Earth's climate system is more complex than simple cause-and-effect, and that understanding these hidden dynamics is essential for preparing for what comes next.

It's a quietly astonishing example of how evolution can blur the lines we draw between species, showing nature's capacity for strategies far stranger than we might imagine.
