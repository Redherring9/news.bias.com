‘Birds know no borders’: Interview with BirdLife Africa’s Alex Ngari

Twice each year, millions of birds undertake extraordinary journeys between breeding grounds in Europe and Asia and their wintering habitats across Africa. Yet scientists still know surprisingly little about the exact routes many species take or the dangers they face along the way. Alex Ngari, who manages BirdLife Africa's migratory birds program, emphasizes a fundamental truth: these travelers recognize no political boundaries, making their protection a shared global responsibility that demands cooperation across nations. Traditionally, researchers tracked birds through leg banding, a method that depended on chance encounters and reported sightings to piece together migration patterns. Modern electronic tags now offer more precise data, but they remain expensive, especially for smaller species. This knowledge gap makes it difficult to assess how climate change will affect bird populations or to predict where conservation efforts are most urgently needed. Meanwhile, critical stopover sites across Africa face mounting pressures. Since the 1970s, roughly 22% of African wetlands have vanished due to human encroachment, climate shifts, and unsustainable water use. Lake Chad, a vital oasis after the grueling Sahara crossing, has lost 90% of its surface area. Kenya's Tana River Delta and Rwanda's Nyabarongo Wetlands face similar threats from agriculture and proposed development. This story matters because it reminds us that nature operates on a scale beyond our borders, requiring us to think collectively about the spaces we share with other species. The quiet struggle of migratory birds—and the scientists working to understand their needs—offers a window into the interconnectedness of ecosystems and the collaborative spirit needed to protect them.

‘So big and so close’: going on a bear hunt in northern Spain

In the mountains of northern Spain, Cantabrian brown bears are staging a remarkable comeback. During the late 1980s, only about 60 of these bears remained across the Cantabrian range, their population decimated by hunting and habitat loss. Villagers near Pola de Somiedo reported sightings of what seemed like the last local family: a three-legged mother, her cubs, and two adult males. Today, nature guide Luis Frechilla estimates their numbers may have climbed to over 500, though they'll remain classified as endangered until the population reaches 1,000. The recovery owes much to the establishment of Somiedo natural park in 1988 and a hunting ban introduced in 1973. Conservation groups like Fundación Oso Pardo have constructed "bio-corridors"—strategic plantings of trees and bushes laden with berries, acorns, and chestnuts—to sustain the bears through seasons when food is scarce. These Cantabrian bears differ from their American grizzly cousins: they're smaller, lighter, and adapted to mountainous terrain that requires constant climbing. They also hibernate less deeply and for shorter periods, emerging in spring to forage for insects under rocks and strip grass from hillsides. This story offers a quiet lesson in persistence and careful stewardship. Watching a distant bear move across a ridgeline through a spotting scope may not feel like a dramatic encounter, but it represents decades of conservation work and the resilience of a species that nearly vanished. In a region where the cuisine is hearty, the altitude demanding, and snow sometimes falls in summer, the return of these bears is a reminder that ecosystems can heal when given protection and patience.

‘We have really cool sharks’: three-year project to lift lid on Britain’s little-understood species

Britain's coastal waters are home to more than 40 species of sharks, yet scientists know surprisingly little about most of them. Now, a new three-year research project aims to change that by investigating three of England's most vulnerable shark species: the porbeagle, which resembles a miniature great white; the tope or school shark; and the blue shark. Led by researchers at the University of Exeter with nearly £1 million in funding from Natural England, the project will combine historical records, electronic tagging, and recreational fishing data to understand where these sharks feed, mate, and migrate. What makes this project particularly noteworthy is its collaboration with Britain's 3 million recreational anglers, who have become unexpected allies in shark conservation. Since the shift to catch-and-release practices, these fishing enthusiasts have been documenting their encounters through databases like the Pat Smith Database, which dates back to 1953. Researchers compare these anglers to bird ringers—passionate citizen scientists whose thousands of hours on the water provide data that professional researchers simply couldn't gather alone. The team will conduct workshops and interviews with anglers across England, building on years of mutual trust developed during previous tagging projects. Sophisticated electronic tags, each worth £1,500, will track sharks for about 45 days to reveal survival rates after catch-and-release and identify critical habitats. This story offers a refreshing perspective on an often-misunderstood relationship between recreational fishing and conservation. It reveals how local waters harbor surprising biodiversity and how communities with deep knowledge of the natural world can become essential partners in protecting it, even when their role might seem counterintuitive at first glance.

Decade-long experiment in outback NSW changing the landscape

In a remote corner of outback New South Wales, a remarkable ecological experiment is reversing more than a century of extinction. The Wild Deserts project, launched in 2016 in Sturt National Park, has successfully reintroduced seven native species that vanished from the region due to introduced predators like cats, foxes, and rabbits. Among the returning residents are bilbies, bandicoots, burrowing bettongs, and western quolls, all now breeding in their ancestral home. The project's success required meticulous preparation. Led by husband-and-wife ecologist team Bec West and Reece Pedler, the effort began with constructing two 2,000-hectare fenced exclosures and painstakingly removing every predator inside—including mapping roughly 1,000 rabbit warrens on foot. Once declared feral-free in 2019, the exclosures became safe havens where these "ecosystem engineers" could thrive. These animals don't just repopulate the desert; they transform it, turning over soil as they forage and burrow, which promotes plant growth and revitalizes the entire landscape. The reintroduced western quoll plays a particularly crucial role as the top native predator, keeping herbivore populations in check. The ultimate test comes in a surrounding "wild training zone" containing low densities of feral cats, where animals learn survival skills their species lost during their absence. While one species, the stick-nest rat, couldn't adapt, the project offers hope in a country with the world's worst extinction record. This quiet work of restoration—turning back the tide of extinction one burrow at a time—reminds us that ecological damage, though severe, isn't always permanent.

Ant 'clones' males of another species to produce its own workers

Formiga ‘clona’ machos de outra espécie para conseguir produzir suas próprias operárias

Scientists have discovered a remarkable reproductive strategy in Iberian harvester ants (Messor ibericus) that challenges fundamental assumptions about species boundaries. Queens of this species don't just produce their own offspring—they also generate genetic clones of males from an entirely different species, Messor structor, which split from them evolutionarily over 5 million years ago. Researchers analyzing 390 individuals across five European Messor species found that all 164 worker ants examined were first-generation hybrids, with roughly equal genetic contributions from both species. The puzzle deepened when these hybrid workers were discovered in regions where M. structor doesn't even exist—some more than a thousand kilometers away from its known range. The answer lay within the colonies themselves: queens were producing two distinct types of males, one group genetically belonging to their own species and another that was pure M. structor, despite having no M. structor fathers in the environment. Through a process called androgenesis, the queens use their eggs to propagate the nuclear genome of M. structor males, essentially maintaining a captive clonal line within their own nests. This discovery, published in Nature, reveals what researchers describe as a form of "sexual domestication"—a species breeding and maintaining males of another species solely to produce the hybrid workers essential for colony function. It's a quietly astonishing example of how evolution can blur the lines we draw between species, showing nature's capacity for strategies far stranger than we might imagine.
