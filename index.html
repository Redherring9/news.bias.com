How Virunga Park protects people and gorillas from Ebola in eastern DRC

In eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo, Africa's oldest national park is playing an unusual role in public health: acting as a natural barrier against the spread of Ebola. Virunga National Park, a vast stretch of protected forest spanning 300 kilometers across North Kivu province, has established six health checkpoints and deployed 40 paramedics alongside 80 rangers to monitor the current outbreak, which has claimed more than 1,400 lives. The initiative aims to protect both human communities and the park's endangered mountain gorillas, who are vulnerable to the disease. This isn't Virunga's first Ebola response. During the DRC's devastating 10th outbreak between 2018 and 2020, which killed nearly 2,300 people, health officials credited the park as a significant factor in preventing the virus from spreading to the city of Goma and beyond. Park director Emmanuel de Merode explains that the protected area's uninhabited wilderness creates a geographical buffer, while checkpoints on roads crossing the park allow for temperature screening, handwashing stations, and first-level isolation centers. The park has also supplied health services with protective equipment, body bags, and vehicles, addressing critical shortages in affected provinces. This story quietly illustrates how conservation and public health can intersect in unexpected ways. In a region marked by decades of armed conflict, where more than 800 rangers already protect some of the world's most threatened species, Virunga demonstrates that preserving wild spaces can serve multiple purposes. The park's contribution to disease surveillance and community protection offers a compelling reminder that environmental conservation isn't separate from human welfare—sometimes the two are inseparably linked, each strengthening the other in the face of crisis.

Fifty years ago, a radical idea saved historic mining town from dust

For nearly a century, the Australian mining town of Kalgoorlie-Boulder endured ferocious dust storms that forced residents indoors and brought daily life to a standstill. The problem began in the 1890s gold rush, when desperate miners stripped the surrounding landscape of trees to fuel their industry, destabilizing the region's fine topsoil. Mine tailings added to the misery, creating vast piles of dust that the wind carried straight into town. Many locals resigned themselves to the problem as an unavoidable feature of outback life. Then in 1973, a newcomer named Julian Grill arrived with what seemed like a far-fetched solution. Working with the agriculture department and volunteers, Grill convinced skeptical officials to try an ambitious greening project. The plan involved spreading wood chips across desert floors, planting saplings in arid shrubland, and covering mine tailings with rocks. Six forest reserves were established north of the city, eventually spanning 3,500 hectares. The effort faced resistance—vandals destroyed hundreds of saplings early on—but volunteers persisted, even fencing off areas without official authority. Fifty years later, those forests still stand, and the dust storms have become a distant memory. What once seemed like an inescapable burden of geography turned out to be solvable through community determination and ecological restoration. It's a quietly remarkable story of how a struggling town refused to accept its fate, proving that environmental problems created by human activity can sometimes be reversed by the same hands that caused them.

Genocide of the Roma: How Central and Southeastern Europe Remembers

Völkermord an den Roma: So gedenken Mittel- und Südosteuropa

Each year on August 2nd, Europe remembers a tragedy that took decades to fully acknowledge: the Nazi genocide of Roma and Sinti people, which claimed an estimated 500,000 lives during World War II. The date marks the night of August 2-3, 1944, when thousands of Roma and Sinti prisoners were murdered at the Birkenau camp in Auschwitz. What began as a Polish Roma-led ceremony in 1994 gradually grew into an official European memorial day, recognized by the European Parliament in 2015. Across Central and Eastern Europe—regions where Roma communities were heavily targeted—countries have developed their own traditions of remembrance. Poland designated August 2nd an official memorial day in 2011, with government officials regularly traveling to Auschwitz; the village of Szczurowa, site of a 1943 massacre, erected Europe's first Roma genocide memorial back in 1956. The Czech Republic transformed a controversial former pig farm in Lety into a memorial site, opened by the country's president in 2024, acknowledging the concentration camp that once stood there. Slovakia has observed the day since 2005, holding ceremonies at its Museum of the National Uprising that bring together government representatives and Roma organizations. This story matters because it traces how collective memory evolves—how acknowledgment can take generations, and how communities persist in ensuring their histories are honored. The commemorations also serve a dual purpose in some countries, addressing not only historical atrocities but contemporary anti-Roma discrimination, connecting past to present in a way that makes remembrance both backward- and forward-looking.

David Page wrote about the mountains and worked to protect them

David Page, executive director of Winter Wildlands Alliance and an award-winning outdoor writer, died at 57 in a traffic accident on Wyoming's Teton Pass in July. His dog Rio, a frequent companion on mountain adventures, also perished in the crash. Page's death represents the loss of someone who understood both the policy machinery that shapes how Americans use public lands and the lived experience of moving through winter wilderness—a rare combination that made his voice distinctive in conservation advocacy. Page came to environmental work through journalism. After studying history, literature, and creative writing at institutions including Dartmouth and Berkeley, he wrote for publications like the New York Times and became a senior correspondent for POWDER magazine. His reporting was notable for its attention to people and careful detail: one article examining avalanche decision-making became required reading in safety courses, while another illuminated the lives of immigrant workers in ski towns. He also authored a guidebook to Yosemite and the southern Sierra that went through three editions. When he joined Winter Wildlands Alliance in 2016, eventually becoming executive director in 2022, he brought this storytelling sensibility to work on forest planning, winter travel regulations, and public participation in land management. What made Page meaningful to those who knew him was his ability to hold contradictions—he was rigorous about policy yet irreverent at ski tests, fluent in multiple languages and committed to careful listening, deeply serious about conservation while understanding that joy matters too. His pandemic-era "Ski Kind" campaign captured this spirit, asking people to treat newcomers and winter landscapes with equal consideration. The tributes published after his death suggest he raised the standards of everyone around him, not through severity but through the example of a life built thoughtfully around family, mountains, and the work of protecting wild places.

"Amicable numbers", the strange mathematical pairs that have captivated lovers, mystics and scientists for 2,500 years

Los "números amigos", las extrañas parejas matemáticas que han cautivado a amantes, místicos y científicos por 2.500 años

For over 2,500 years, a curious mathematical phenomenon has captivated philosophers, mystics, and mathematicians alike: amicable numbers. These are pairs of numbers where each holds the exact identity of the other through their divisors. Take 220 and 284—the divisors of 220 sum to 284, and the divisors of 284 sum to 220. This elegant reciprocity, first documented by the Syrian philosopher Iamblichus and attributed to Pythagoras, seemed to embody the ancient saying "a friend is another self." But these numbers didn't remain confined to mathematical treatises. Across cultures and centuries, people infused them with mystical meaning. Jewish Kabbalists noted that when Jacob sent his estranged brother Esau a peace offering, it included precisely 220 goats and 220 sheep—interpreted as a numerical charm to invoke forgiveness and fraternal love. Islamic scholars and medieval grimoires prescribed talismans inscribed with 220 and 284 to forge friendship or romantic bonds between two people. The belief persisted that these numbers could not merely symbolize affection but actually conjure it. What makes this story quietly remarkable is how a purely mathematical curiosity became a canvas for human longing across vastly different traditions. The same pairs of numbers that intrigued rational mathematicians also carried the hopes of lovers and the prayers of brothers seeking reconciliation. It's a reminder that even in the abstract world of number theory, people have always searched for patterns that reflect our deepest relationships—and sometimes dared to believe that understanding those patterns might help us shape them.

Country Life: Hydroponics in Horowhenua

In the Horowhenua region of New Zealand, Tony and Lisa Dale-Low traded corporate careers and city life for something entirely different: growing salad greens and herbs using hydroponics. With no prior growing experience, the couple took over an existing operation near Levin in 2018, learning from the previous owners before making the business their own. What makes their story compelling is the thoughtful simplicity of their system—and the pristine quality of what it produces. Hydroponic growing replaces soil with nutrient-rich water, which flows through channels dubbed "gullies"—repurposed downpipes drilled with holes where seedlings are planted. The entire setup is automated and self-sustaining: rainwater collected from greenhouse roofs is recirculated through the system, with computers monitoring pH and nutrient levels. Gravity does much of the work, drawing liquid down the channels and back to holding tanks. The plants, started from seedlings on flood-and-drain tables, grow for about two months before harvest. Because they're grown in a controlled, soil-free environment, they require no washing and are never touched by human hands until they're picked—crispy, clean, and ready to eat. The farm has also recently added solar panels, which power operations and even charge an old Nissan Leaf used for maintenance. This story offers a glimpse into how a family with no background in agriculture built a sustainable, efficient food-growing system from scratch. It's a reminder that career changes, even radical ones, can lead to innovative solutions—and that sometimes the freshest food comes from the most unexpected journeys.

Should you take the plunge? How to tell how clean the sea is

As concerns about water quality make headlines across the UK, beachgoers are increasingly seeking reliable ways to assess whether it's safe to swim. The question of clean water has become more pressing, with sewage spills, agricultural runoff, and other pollutants potentially affecting coastal waters—though the picture is more nuanced than it might first appear. Several systems now exist to help swimmers make informed decisions. The Environment Agency rates designated bathing waters from "excellent" to "poor" based on bacteria samples collected over four years, testing for E. coli and intestinal enterococci that can cause illness. However, this official monitoring only runs from mid-May through September and doesn't reflect day-to-day conditions. Water companies provide their own alert systems, like South West Water's WaterFit Live, which notifies users of potential sewage impacts for specific periods after spills. Meanwhile, the campaign group Surfers Against Sewage has developed an app that aggregates data from multiple sources and maintains pollution alerts for 48 hours as a precautionary measure. The Blue Flag scheme offers another benchmark, assessing beaches against strict international criteria. This story matters because it highlights both progress and limitations in public information about water safety. While more tools exist than ever before, gaps remain—official testing is seasonal, alerts vary between systems, and a sewage spill doesn't automatically mean unsafe conditions. For anyone planning a coastal swim, understanding these different resources and their constraints offers a clearer path to making thoughtful choices about when and where to take the plunge.

Dry July for Western Australia as stunning pictures tell story

Western Australia experienced one of its driest Julys on record in 2026, despite the month typically being one of the wettest of the year. Perth received just 56.8 millimeters of rain—less than half its long-term average of 147 millimeters—while the broader South West region saw its lowest July rainfall since records began, averaging only 39.7 millimeters. The unusual dry spell was caused by stubborn high-pressure systems that settled over southern Australia, blocking the cold fronts that normally sweep in from the Southern Ocean to deliver winter rain. Each time a storm approached, another high-pressure system would arrive, steering the weather south and leaving much of Western Australia in calm, settled conditions. The clear skies brought frosty mornings to inland areas, with some locations dropping below zero multiple times. Mid-month brought an unseasonably warm day on July 15, when Jurien Bay hit 26.9°C—its highest July temperature on record—and Perth reached 25.7°C, its warmest July day in 32 years. ABC readers captured the unusual weather through their lenses: double rainbows between brief showers, eerie fog blanketing Perth, and crisp night skies perfect for astrophotography. This story offers a vivid portrait of climate variability, told through the eyes of residents who documented their changing skies. It's a reminder that weather patterns can surprise us, and that communities notice when the rhythms of the seasons shift in unexpected ways.

Scientist's lifelong obsession with mystery of elusive 'pink zebra' seaweed

Off the coast of Cornwall, beneath waters most associate with the Great Barrier Reef's fame, lies a quieter marvel: maerl beds, ancient carpets of calcified seaweed that host some of the ocean's richest life. Forty years ago, marine scientist Christine Maggs discovered a tiny fragment of pink-striped seaweed living on maerl during her PhD research. It was unlike anything in the scientific literature, and she suspected it might be new to science. But proving that required a larger sample—something that would elude her for decades. Throughout her career, Maggs caught only fleeting glimpses of the elusive "pink zebra" seaweed, never enough to confirm its identity. Then, two years ago, specialist divers surveying the health of Cornish maerl beds brought up samples that independent marine biologist Francis Bunker thought might be what Maggs had been seeking. He sent the specimens to her in London, where she nurtured them in a wine fridge—the perfect cool, dark environment—until they grew large enough for DNA sequencing. The molecular analysis, conducted with colleague Beatriz de Barros Barreto, confirmed what Maggs had hoped: the seaweed was indeed a species new to science. A sample now resides in the Natural History Museum's official collection, and the findings will soon be published in the journal Phycologia. This story reminds us that discovery doesn't always require distant expeditions or high-tech laboratories. Sometimes it asks for patience, a magnifying glass, and four decades of quiet determination. In an age of rapid information, Maggs' lifelong pursuit of one small seaweed offers a different rhythm—one where curiosity, persistence, and wonder reveal that even well-studied waters still hold secrets waiting to be named.

Drifting tuna fishing gear enter half of marine protected areas: study

Industrial tuna fishing gear is drifting into marine protected areas across the globe, carrying consequences that ripple far beyond the fishing grounds where the devices were first deployed. A recent study estimates that drifting fish aggregating devices—floating rafts equipped with satellite trackers that help vessels locate tuna—have affected more than half of the world's protected marine areas. Over a 16-year period, researchers documented at least 6,300 strandings of these devices across 174 protected sites in 53 countries, some traveling thousands of kilometers before washing ashore or sinking. The devices, known as dFADs, are used by purse-seine vessels to attract skipjack, yellowfin, and bigeye tuna. While they're designed to be tracked and retrieved, many are abandoned, deactivated, or simply lost, continuing to drift on ocean currents long after they've served their purpose. When they strand in protected areas, they can damage coral reefs, entangle marine wildlife including sharks and turtles, and leave small island nations—which recorded roughly 30 percent of the documented strandings—with the difficult and costly task of removal. Hotspots were identified in the western and central Pacific, the western Indian Ocean, and the Caribbean, with French Polynesia, Seychelles, and the Maldives bearing the heaviest burden. This story offers a window into an often-invisible problem: the way fishing practices in one part of the ocean can affect ecosystems and communities thousands of miles away. It's a reminder that conservation boundaries drawn on maps can't stop the movement of debris on currents, and that protecting marine life may require rethinking how fishing gear is tracked, retrieved, and regulated across international waters.

The UK has already seen seven days above 35°C this year, surpassing previous records, and preliminary estimates suggest heat-related deaths may reach their highest levels since tracking began. This combination of drought and heat offers a sobering glimpse into how shifting weather patterns affect daily life, from water use to public health—a story worth watching as it quietly reshapes the rhythms of an entire region.

The lake whitefish, once so abundant in the Great Lakes that it made Sandusky, Ohio, the world's largest freshwater fish market in the 1880s, is now facing a precipitous decline that could see it disappear from parts of the region within five years. For centuries, Indigenous communities built their lives around this fish, and settler towns later grew prosperous from its popularity. But beginning in the 1990s, something started going wrong—fewer young fish were making it to adulthood, and researchers began searching for answers. The culprit turned out to be an ecological chain reaction triggered by invasive zebra and quagga mussels, which arrived in the Great Lakes via transatlantic shipping vessels. These mussels consume the phytoplankton that feed diporeia, small shrimp-like crustaceans that young whitefish depend on after hatching. Without this crucial food source, fingerlings starve before they can grow large enough to shift to other prey. The mussels now blanket lakebeds for hundreds of miles, and while they've helped clean the water of agricultural and industrial pollutants, their overwhelming numbers have devastated the food web. Commercial harvests have collapsed—from 200,000 pounds in Ohio alone in 2009 to just 185,000 pounds across all of Lake Erie last year. This story matters because it illustrates how a single invasive species can unravel an entire ecosystem and the human communities that depend on it. For fishers like William Ortiz of the Little Traverse Bay Bands of Odawa Indians, the whitefish decline represents not just lost income but the erosion of treaty rights and traditional ways of life. Scientists are now racing to find solutions, including exploring whether native freshwater drum fish might help control mussel populations, offering a glimmer of hope in an otherwise sobering ecological transformation.
