Sexual assault survivors’ stories may shift over time – a psychologist explains why it doesn’t mean they’re lying

When sexual assault survivors' accounts shift over time or initially downplay what happened to them, it doesn't mean they're fabricating their experience—it reflects how trauma affects the brain and body. Research shows that 60 to 70 percent of female rape survivors, particularly college students, don't immediately identify their experience as rape, often describing it instead as "bad sex" or "miscommunication." This pattern recently drew public attention in a lawsuit filed by a Cornell University student who initially didn't characterize her experience as assault when discussing it with a friend, only coming forward weeks later after others labeled it as gang rape. The explanation lies in neurobiology and psychology. Immediately following sexual assault, survivors often experience rape trauma syndrome—characterized by self-blame, emotional dysregulation, fear, and physical symptoms like sleep problems and muscle tension. During this acute stress period, the brain enters survival mode, activating the limbic system and hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis. These systems trigger fight, flight, freeze, or fawn responses while simultaneously disrupting the brain's ability to think clearly, plan, or make sense of events. This neurobiological upheaval prevents healthy coping and accurate processing of what occurred. Self-blame and denial, while seemingly counterintuitive, actually serve protective functions for survivors navigating this internal storm. Many prefer to act as if the assault never happened, driven by a strong desire to return to normalcy. Understanding these biological and psychological survival processes reveals why delayed or evolving accounts are not signs of dishonesty but rather perfectly logical responses to trauma. This story matters because it challenges common misconceptions about how "real" victims should behave—assumptions that can silence survivors and obstruct justice.

World Octopus Day: new discoveries about the world’s smartest invertebrates

In honor of World Octopus Day, celebrated each October 8th, recent discoveries remind us just how remarkable these eight-limbed creatures truly are. Known for their intelligence, tool use, and masterful camouflage, octopuses continue to surprise scientists with secrets both ancient and brand new. This year's research has ranged from colossal prehistoric predators to tiny newly identified species, each adding depth to our understanding of these enigmatic invertebrates. Perhaps most striking is the fossil evidence of Nanaimoteuthis haggarti, an extinct octopus that may have stretched up to 61 feet in length—nearly two-thirds the size of a blue whale. Japanese researchers analyzing ancient jawbones suggest these giants were apex predators prowling Cretaceous seas more than 70 million years ago. On the opposite end of the spectrum, researchers have documented baby Patagonian octopuses developing inside oyster shells, their eyes visible as black dots through translucent eggs. The mother guards these eggs so devotedly she stops eating entirely until they hatch. Meanwhile, scientists exploring the deep waters of the Galapagos in 2015 confirmed a golf-ball-sized blue octopus as a new species, though they're still puzzled by its unusually short arms and how such stubby appendages help it hunt. These stories offer more than scientific curiosity—they're windows into an underwater world that still holds countless mysteries. From the devoted parenting of modern octopuses to the sheer scale of their prehistoric relatives, these discoveries reveal creatures far more complex and varied than we might have imagined, quietly thriving in oceans we're only beginning to understand.

Seychelles giant tortoise numbers hit record 180,000 in conservation triumph

A remote atoll in the Seychelles has become home to approximately 180,000 Aldabra giant tortoises, the highest population ever recorded for this once-endangered species and a figure that now dwarfs the human population of the entire island nation. The count, conducted by the Seychelles Islands Foundation and the University of Exeter over five years, represents a remarkable turnaround for creatures that numbered fewer than 1,000 in the 19th century due to exploitation. Aldabra Atoll is one of only two places worldwide where giant tortoises roam freely in the wild, the other being the Galápagos Islands with a much smaller population. The recovery began in earnest when the atoll became a strict nature reserve in 1981 and a UNESCO World Heritage site the following year. Researchers faced significant challenges in producing this updated estimate, as the atoll's dense vegetation makes direct counting difficult in many areas. By modeling both observed and unobserved tortoises, the team created what they consider the most reliable baseline for tracking the population into the future. While the numbers tell a conservation success story for the Indian Ocean region, researchers caution that continued vigilance is essential. Climate change poses growing threats through longer droughts that reduce available food and shade, while rising sea levels endanger low-lying portions of the atoll. Invasive species like feral cats and black rats continue to prey on tortoise eggs and hatchlings. This story matters because it demonstrates what sustained conservation commitment can achieve, while also reminding us that even spectacular recoveries require ongoing attention. These ancient creatures, thriving against the odds, need stewards who understand that protecting their future means preparing for challenges still to come.

Pioneer of the program that took the US to the Moon dies

Morre a pioneira do programa que levou os EUA à Lua

Margaret Hamilton, the software engineer who led the team that programmed the computers for NASA's Apollo moon missions, has died at age 90. The Massachusetts Institute of Technology, where she worked from 1959 through the mid-1970s, announced her passing and described her as profoundly influential in software development, someone who "blazed new trails in computer science and engineering." Hamilton's journey began with mathematics studies before she moved to Boston in 1959 and joined MIT. In 1965, she applied for a position at a university laboratory seeking people to develop software to "put a man on the moon." She became the project's first programmer and the first woman to hold that role. Starting with unmanned Apollo missions, she was later promoted to lead the team developing flight software for crewed missions. By 1968, she directed the Command and Service Module team, overseeing more than 400 people working on Apollo software. As one MIT professor noted, calling her a pioneer understates her achievement—she was engineering software when the field was in its infancy, both writing advanced code herself and leading a team to create one of humanity's most complex systems. Hamilton later founded two software companies and published over 130 papers. In 2016, President Barack Obama awarded her the Presidential Medal of Freedom, the highest civilian honor in the United States. Her story reminds us that transformative technological achievements often begin with young people taking leaps into the unknown—Hamilton herself was in her twenties when this work began—and that the code written by dedicated hands can quite literally help humanity reach for the stars.

How to Win the Nobel Prize in Literature - A Look Behind the Scenes of the Selection Process

The Nobel Prize in Literature follows a carefully orchestrated year-long process that begins long before the October announcement. When Canadian writer Anne Carson was named the 2026 laureate, it marked the culmination of a selection journey involving hundreds of nominations, months of reading, and deliberations shrouded in secrecy that won't be fully revealed for half a century. Each September, the Swedish Academy sends nomination forms to qualified individuals worldwide—literature professors, previous laureates, and heads of representative writers' associations. Researcher Gisèle Sapiro notes that translation serves as a crucial measure of a work's universal reach; nearly all winners since 1990 had been translated into English, French, and German before receiving the prize. Previous major literary awards also significantly boost a candidate's prospects. The archive reveals patient trajectories—British author Doris Lessing waited 35 years between her first nomination and her 2007 win. This story offers a rare glimpse behind the curtain of one of literature's most prestigious honors, revealing how subjective ideals meet practical criteria in recognizing writing that transcends cultural boundaries. It's a reminder that literary greatness, while difficult to define using Alfred Nobel's mandate to honor work "in an idealistic direction," ultimately seeks voices that reshape how we understand human experience across languages and borders.

Scientists in Vienna and Beijing create world's first nuclear clocks

Cientistas em Viena e Pequim criam primeiros relógios nucleares do mundo

Two research teams working independently in Vienna and Beijing have achieved a milestone nearly five decades in the making: the world's first operational nuclear clocks. These devices represent the next generation beyond atomic clocks, which have been the gold standard for precision timekeeping since 1949. The breakthrough, published simultaneously in the journal Nature, marks a significant leap in humanity's ability to measure time with extraordinary accuracy. Unlike atomic clocks that monitor electrons jumping between energy levels in an atom's outer shell, nuclear clocks track similar transitions within the nucleus itself—specifically, protons and neutrons in thorium-229 atoms trapped in calcium fluoride crystals. Because the atomic nucleus is far smaller than the electron shell surrounding it, scientists believe this approach could eventually achieve even greater precision than today's best atomic clocks, which would lose or gain only one second over billions of years. The Vienna team, led by physicist Thorsten Schumm, and the Beijing team, guided by Shiqian Ding, used different experimental approaches, demonstrating the robustness of the concept. Intriguingly, each team's strengths complement the other: Vienna has superior crystals while Beijing has a more powerful laser. While nuclear clocks don't yet outperform conventional atomic clocks, researchers are optimistic about refining the technology. The potential applications span satellite navigation, data synchronization, surveying, and fundamental physics research. This story is worth attention not just for its technical achievement, but for what it represents: a patient, collaborative pursuit of precision that could quietly transform technologies we rely on daily, from GPS to internet communications—proof that some of science's most practical advances emerge from dreams nurtured across generations.

Climate change strips island’s title of largest penguin colony, study shows

A remote volcanic island near Antarctica has lost its distinction as home to the world's largest penguin colony, following a dramatic population crash that researchers link to climate change. Zavodovski Island's chinstrap penguin population plummeted by nearly two-thirds—more than 680,000 birds—between 2011 and 2025, according to new research from the British Antarctic Survey. The colony that once numbered over a million breeding individuals now stands at roughly 402,000. The scale and speed of the decline surprised scientists, particularly because surveys as recent as 2011 had suggested the population was stable. While a 2016 volcanic eruption initially sparked concern, researchers found the ash cloud blew away from penguin nesting areas, and population losses had already begun before the eruption and continued steadily afterward. Instead, the evidence points to warming ocean temperatures pushing krill—the tiny crustaceans that chinstrap penguins depend on—southward into colder waters that the birds cannot easily reach. Meanwhile, macaroni penguins on the same island have actually increased in number, likely because their more varied diet makes them less vulnerable to krill shortages. This divergence represents the most striking shift in penguin community composition ever observed at a single Antarctic location. The findings carry weight beyond one island: they suggest chinstrap populations across the broader South Sandwich Islands may be in trouble, prompting researchers to recommend the species be reclassified from "least concern" to "endangered." This story offers a sobering example of how climate-driven changes ripple through ecosystems in unexpected ways—quietly reshaping even the most remote corners of our planet, one colony at a time.

Nobel Prize in Chemistry celebrates two scientists who unraveled a 4-billion-year-old mystery

Prêmio Nobel de Química celebra dois cientistas que desvendaram um mistério de 4 bilhões de anos

The 2026 Nobel Prize in Chemistry has been awarded to two scientists who solved a puzzle that had stumped researchers for as long as life has existed on Earth. French chemist Henri Kagan and Japanese scientist Kenso Soai were recognized for unraveling how nature creates specific molecular structures essential to life—a discovery that has paved the way for safer, more effective medications. At the heart of their work are chiral molecules, structures that exist in two mirror-image forms, like left and right hands. Though composed of identical atoms and bonds, these molecular twins don't behave the same way in the body. Nature almost exclusively produces just one version, while traditional lab chemistry generates equal amounts of both. This selectivity matters enormously in medicine: one molecular form might heal, while its mirror image could be inert or even dangerous. The drug thalidomide serves as a sobering reminder—prescribed in the 1950s to treat morning sickness, it caused severe birth defects in thousands of babies because of uncontrolled molecular forms, though it's still used today for treating certain cancers and complications from leprosy and HIV. Kagan began tackling this challenge in the 1980s, developing methods to favor production of a single molecular form. Soai built on this foundation, publishing breakthrough results in Nature in 1995 that dramatically improved the technique's efficiency. He received the Nobel phone call while heading to the grocery store, calling it the most incredible day of his life. The Nobel committee described the question they answered as fundamental—a mystery persisting for four billion years, since the very emergence of life on our planet.

Gene therapy can partly restore sight in blind people, researchers reveal

A pioneering gene therapy that uses light to restore partial vision in blind people has shown promising results in a clinical trial, offering hope to millions affected by degenerative eye diseases. The optogenetic approach, which builds on Nobel Prize-winning research, has now been successfully tested in ten participants with advanced retinitis pigmentosa—a group of genetic disorders affecting light-sensitive cells in the retina. The treatment involves a single injection into the eye containing a harmless virus that delivers genetic instructions to surviving retinal cells, making them light-sensitive. Patients then wear special goggles that capture visual information and convert it into light pulses, which activate the modified cells to create a monochrome image. Six of the ten participants experienced meaningful improvements in light sensitivity, and some could perform tasks like locating objects, detecting doors, and walking along lines when wearing the goggles. The therapy proved safe over a follow-up period of up to five years, with only one minor side effect. Importantly, the technique works regardless of the specific genetic cause of vision loss, potentially broadening its application. While the results represent significant progress—moving from one patient in 2021 to ten now—the researchers acknowledge current limitations. Patients cannot yet recognize faces, as the treated cells form a ring around the fovea, the area responsible for sharp central vision. Still, the study demonstrates that optogenetics can safely provide stable vision restoration lasting several years. With researchers aiming to achieve high-resolution vision within five to ten years, this quiet breakthrough offers a glimpse of how light itself might become a tool for seeing again.

Canada has announced it will indefinitely suspend plans to expand its medical assistance in dying program to people whose sole condition is mental illness.
