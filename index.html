Could we deflect a dangerous asteroid? Scientists release images of their first attempt

¿Podríamos desviar un asteroide peligroso? Los científicos publican imágenes de su primer intento

In 2022, NASA deliberately crashed a spacecraft into an asteroid called Dimorphos at 22,000 kilometers per hour. It wasn't a threat—it was a test. Could humanity deflect a dangerous space rock if one were ever headed our way? Now, four years later, the European Space Agency's Hera spacecraft has returned to survey the damage, and the images reveal something remarkable: a crater nearly as large as the asteroid itself. Dimorphos, about 150 meters wide, orbits a larger companion asteroid named Didymos. The collision successfully altered Dimorphos's orbit, shortening its trip around Didymos by 33 minutes. But the impact did more than nudge the asteroid off course—it fundamentally changed how it moves through space. Before the crash, Dimorphos rotated calmly, always showing the same face to its partner, much like our Moon does to Earth. Now, scientists say, it tumbles chaotically, like a barrel rolling down stairs. Meanwhile, detailed images of Didymos reveal a landscape strewn with car- and bus-sized boulders drifting across a world with gravity 40,000 times weaker than Earth's. This story matters because it's a quiet rehearsal for a scenario we hope never arrives. The Hera mission, likened to a detective returning to the scene, will spend six months studying both asteroids, deploying shoebox-sized satellites to examine the crater up close. It's science at its most practical and hopeful—a demonstration that with enough foresight and ingenuity, we might one day protect our fragile planet from a cosmic collision.

The floating classroom teaching kids to protect their environment

On the remote Fijian island of Totoya, children are learning to protect their environment by reconnecting with traditions that risk disappearing. As part of a programme run by Conservation International, 45 students recently spent three days learning aboard the Uto ni Yalo, a traditional double-hulled voyaging canoe. They wove food baskets with their elders, built models of their island, and learned the parts of the vessel from the last surviving sailor from Totoya—skills that had not been passed down in years because elders typically did the work themselves. The initiative responds to a quiet crisis: as island communities face climate change, declining fisheries, and migration, traditional knowledge is fading. The programme, modelled on a similar project launched in Samoa in 2018, seeks to reverse this by making lessons experiential and rooted in local culture. Teachers now plan to bring learning outside the classroom, using the school playground to teach mathematics and the ocean to teach stewardship. The approach has proven effective—children become noticeably more engaged when lessons connect to their own island, and the Samoan materials have already been integrated into that country's primary curriculum. The model is now being adapted for Fiji and the Cook Islands, with funding from New Zealand. This story is worth a reader's time because it offers a thoughtful alternative to conventional education, one that recognizes knowledge survives only when practiced. By honouring the navigation and biodiversity skills of Pacific ancestors—described as "our astronauts"—the programme ensures that cultural and environmental wisdom travel together across generations, keeping both alive.

New breed of black sheep has hair instead of wool

A family farm in New South Wales has developed an entirely new breed of sheep that challenges traditional expectations. The Martinmore, bred by the Gilmore family at Black Springs in the state's Central West, is striking for two reasons: it's completely black, and it grows hair instead of wool. Named after family member Martin Gilmore who died in 2018, these glossy animals are descendants of the Australian White sheep breed the family helped establish over a decade ago. At their market debut, the top Martinmore ram sold for nearly $22,000, almost double the average price of their pale predecessors. Beyond aesthetics, the breed's dark pigmentation offers a practical advantage in Australian conditions. These sheep show natural resistance to photosensitisation, a potentially fatal condition where certain plants make livestock's skin hypersensitive to sunlight, causing severe lesions and liver damage. Veterinary experts explain that darker skin provides inherent protection against the sun's harmful rays, much like natural sunscreen. However, this protective colouring comes with a trade-off: black animals don't handle extreme heat as well as lighter-coloured livestock, requiring more shade during hot summer months. This story captures an interesting intersection of tradition, innovation, and adaptation. It's a reminder that agriculture continues to evolve through careful breeding and that sometimes the solutions to modern farming challenges come wrapped in unexpected packages—in this case, a jet-black sheep that might just rewrite a centuries-old nursery rhyme. The Martinmore represents both a tribute to family legacy and a practical response to Australia's challenging climate conditions.

Ancient Cahokia was transformed amid droughts and flooding – climate change is reshaping modern communities in similar ways

Researchers are drawing unexpected parallels between the ancient city of Cahokia and the modern community of Centreville, Illinois—two settlements separated by centuries but connected by a shared struggle with climate and social change. Both sit on the same Mississippi River floodplain, and both have watched residents gradually depart as environmental pressures mounted. Cahokia, once home to perhaps 20,000 people around 1100 CE, was a thriving urban center with massive earthen mounds, public plazas, and communal ceremonies. But during the Medieval Climatic Anomaly, the city faced major floods and prolonged droughts. People began leaving in the 12th century, a slow exodus that continued for 200 years. As the population dwindled, communal practices faded—feasting stopped, mound-building ceased, and agricultural surplus moved from shared granaries into private hands. Modern Centreville tells a strikingly similar story. Once a stable working-class neighborhood, it has faced increasing rainfall—up 10 inches annually since the 1960s—and inadequate stormwater infrastructure. Flooding now brings raw sewage into homes and streets. Those who can afford to leave have done so, leaving behind an aging population and rows of abandoned houses. The community, researchers note, is essentially becoming an archaeological site in real time. This quiet comparison offers something rare: a long view of how communities respond when climate stress intersects with social and economic vulnerability. It's not the dramatic collapse of disaster movies, but a gradual unraveling that reshapes daily life and tests the bonds that hold people together. The story reminds us that climate change is as much about slowly shifting ground beneath our feet as it is about headlines and catastrophe.

Health surveillance reports show that unneutered male cats with outdoor access account for the majority of animal cases, while the state has also confirmed nearly 1,500 human infections. This story offers a window into how animal behavior, human care practices, and public health intersect. Dating back to the 19th century, Congo bands represent the layered cultural history of Brazil's southeastern coast. Men and women of all ages take on roles such as master, captain, queen, musicians, singers, and dancers, each band carrying its own repertoire of toadas (traditional songs) and practices that reflect their community's unique identity. The federal recognition follows a 2015 state-level designation and a formal petition submitted by eight Congo associations in 2024. During the approval process, officials emphasized that the ancestral roots of Congo—both Indigenous and African—are not relics of the past but living forces shaping possible futures. This story matters because it highlights how communities actively preserve traditions that honor complex histories, keeping them alive not as museum pieces but as evolving expressions of identity, faith, and collective memory.

How the construction, desecration and destruction of Jerusalem’s ancient temples shaped Judaism – and Hanukkah

The story of Hanukkah begins with a temple—or rather, with two temples, both built, both lost, and one reclaimed in a moment of triumph that still echoes today. In 167 B.C.E., the Greek king Antiochos IV desecrated Jerusalem's Jewish Temple, converting it to a shrine for Zeus and banning core Jewish practices. Three years later, Jewish forces recaptured the site and rededicated it. According to later rabbinic tradition, a single day's supply of sacred oil miraculously burned for eight days. The name Hanukkah itself means "dedication," underscoring the temple's profound importance in Jewish life and belief. This Second Temple stood on ground already heavy with memory. The First Temple, traditionally attributed to King Solomon in the 10th century B.C.E., was more than a place of worship—it was understood as the earthly home of God, a political center, and an economic hub akin to a modern bank. Ancient Israelites believed that so long as the divine presence dwelled there, Jerusalem could not fall. Yet when religious observance waned, biblical texts describe God departing, and Babylonian forces destroyed the Temple in 586 B.C.E. The people of Judah were exiled, their central sanctuary gone. But instead of vanishing, Jewish tradition adapted: synagogues emerged, prayers replaced sacrifices, and practices evolved in diaspora. This story matters because it shows how resilience and reinvention can be born from loss. The temples of Jerusalem were not just buildings—they were symbols of identity, continuity, and the belief that the sacred can be rebuilt. Understanding their construction, desecration, and dedication helps us see Hanukkah not merely as a festival of lights, but as a celebration of perseverance and the enduring power of a people to reclaim what is theirs.

How the pōhutukawa went global

The pōhutukawa, New Zealand's beloved Christmas tree, is more diverse and far-traveled than many might imagine. While red stamens carpeting car bonnets have become a quintessential signal of Kiwi summer, University of Auckland biologist Bruce Burns reveals there are at least 42 different cultivated varieties, alongside species imported from Tahiti and the Kermadec Islands that now flourish in New Zealand gardens. The iconic tree's natural range is surprisingly limited—originally confined to New Zealand's North Island from roughly Gisborne to Taranaki. Everything further south, including Wellington's coastal displays and South Island plantings, represents human intervention and subsequent naturalization. Even the tree's signature crimson blooms tell a more colorful story: a golden variety discovered on Motiti Island in 1940 grows in scattered locations, while rare white specimens exist, and wild pōhutukawa around Rotorua's lakes produce distinctive pink flowers. Individual trees bloom for just two weeks within the November-to-February season, though each tends to flower at the same time year after year, with peak displays conveniently timed around Christmas. This story offers a gentle reminder that even familiar landmarks hold surprises, and that New Zealand isn't immune to exporting invasive species—pōhutukawa have established themselves as unwelcome guests in California and South Africa. It's a quietly fascinating portrait of how a cherished native symbol has both diversified at home and complicated ecosystems abroad, inviting readers to look more closely at the trees that frame their summer memories.

Now, a local nonprofit called Faunagua is partnering with six communities to grow and plant nearly half a million Kewiña saplings since 2022. A life-threatening brain mass became an unexpected turning point for Emma Court, transforming her from a commuter enduring four-hour drives to a corporate Brisbane job into an award-nominated gourmet mushroom farmer. Nine years ago, after weeks of debilitating symptoms—chronic fatigue, blurred vision, and excruciating headaches—Court refused to accept dismissive diagnoses of anxiety and overwork. An eye test revealed the truth: an inflammatory mass had engulfed her pituitary gland and crushed her optic nerve. Emergency neurosurgery saved her life, and in recovery, she turned to growing nutrient-rich gourmet mushrooms to support her healing. What began as a health-focused hobby evolved into Rocky Creek Mushrooms, a thriving business producing around 100 kilograms of gourmet fungi weekly. Court's husband Wayne, a former McLaren F1 race engineer, helped design automated grow rooms and a sterile laboratory free from chemical pesticides. Court herself mastered the cultivation of over 30 mushroom varieties, attended hundreds of markets and restaurant deliveries, and developed value-added products like seasonings, teas, and extracts. Her marinated forest mushrooms even earned a silver medal at the Melbourne Royal Australian Food Awards. The operation runs on solar power, harvests rainwater, and recycles spent substrate—Court has even experimented with growing containers and surfboards from mycelium. This story offers a quiet testament to resilience and reinvention. In an industry facing a 12 percent production decline nationally, Court has built a profitable, sustainable enterprise by focusing on quality and innovation. Her journey reminds us that sometimes our most challenging moments can redirect us toward work that nourishes both body and soul.

How two refrigerators abandoned by the U.S. Army in Tuscany gave rise to Italian gelato

Como dois refrigeradores abandonados pelo exército dos EUA na Toscana deram origem ao sorvete italiano

Every summer, millions of travelers flock to Italy seeking gelato, but few realize that this iconic Italian dessert might never have become widespread without American military technology. The story traces back to a modest Tuscan dairy and a pair of abandoned U.S. Army refrigerators that changed everything. While Italians have been crafting frozen treats since Roman times—from snow-cooled wines to Renaissance cream concoctions—traditional gelato remained a hyper-local craft well into the 20th century. The process depended on manual churning, natural ice, and constant attention, making it impossible to produce in large quantities or transport far from where it was made. Even when electric refrigeration arrived in the 1920s, high costs kept production limited. After World War II, the Bagnoli family of Empoli transformed their small dairy bar into something revolutionary. Using salvaged American military-grade refrigerators acquired around 1946, along with scavenged commercial ice cream equipment, they solved gelato's fundamental challenge: unlike fatty American ice cream, Italian gelato requires rapid, consistent freezing to maintain its signature dense, smooth texture without ice crystals. This quiet fusion of Tuscan ingenuity and American engineering allowed the Bagnoli brothers to produce stable, creamy gelato in quantities large enough to distribute beyond their neighborhood while preserving its artisanal character. The story reminds us that beloved traditions often emerge from unexpected collaborations—and that sometimes the most authentically Italian experiences owe a hidden debt to wartime surplus and cross-cultural resourcefulness.

The hidden meanings in The Great Wave, one of the most famous works of Japanese art

Os significados ocultos na obra A Grande Onda, uma das mais conhecidas da arte japonesa

Katsushika Hokusai's iconic 1831 woodblock print, The Great Wave off Kanagawa, has captivated viewers for nearly two centuries with its dramatic depiction of towering blue waves. What makes this image endlessly fascinating is how much we think we see versus what's actually there. Even Vincent and Theo van Gogh, discussing the work in letters during the 1880s, remembered a single ship caught in the wave's claw-like grasp—when in fact, three elongated cargo boats struggle against the water. This collective misremembering points to the print's peculiar power to confuse even careful observers. The composition itself is deceptively simple: three boats, rising waves, an overcast sky, and in the distance, a small snow-capped Mount Fuji. Yet its meaning remains elusive. Is it about nature's sublime power against human insignificance, or humanity's courage facing disaster? Does the wave represent life's fleeting nature while the mountain symbolizes the soul's endurance? Hokusai created The Great Wave as part of a series called Thirty-Six Views of Mount Fuji, made during a profound personal reinvention after surviving a stroke near age seventy. Obsessed with the sacred mountain associated with immortality, he captured it from dozens of angles. The complete series of forty-six prints is now on display at London's Dulwich Picture Gallery—the first time all images have been shown together in the UK. This story matters because it reveals how even the most recognizable images can hold hidden layers. A work now ubiquitous on everything from emoji to currency continues to guard its mysteries, reminding us that truly great art resists easy interpretation and rewards patient, repeated looking.

BepiColombo, the mission to Mercury seeking to discover why the planet is shrinking (and other mysteries)

BepiColombo, la misión a Mercurio que busca descubrir por qué el planeta se está achicando (y otros misterios)

After an eight-year journey through the inner Solar System, the spacecraft BepiColombo is approaching Mercury to study one of our least understood planetary neighbors. Despite being relatively close to Earth, Mercury has received far less scientific attention than Mars—only two NASA missions have studied it closely, compared to more than fifty sent to the Red Planet. The disparity stems partly from public fascination with Mars's potential for life, but also from the genuine difficulty of reaching Mercury: the Sun's powerful gravitational pull requires spacecraft to constantly decelerate or risk plunging into our star. BepiColombo, a joint mission between the European Space Agency and Japan's JAXA, has taken a winding path involving nine flybys of Earth, Venus, and Mercury itself to use their gravity for braking rather than burning precious fuel. The mission honors Italian engineer Giuseppe "Bepi" Colombo, who first suggested using Venus flybys to reach Mercury in the 1970s. Scientists hope the mission will solve several puzzles, including why water ice appears to exist in some craters despite surface temperatures reaching 430°C, and why the planet's surface contains chemicals inconsistent with its current proximity to the Sun—suggesting it may have formed much farther out in the solar system. This story offers a reminder that some of the most accessible objects in our cosmic neighborhood remain deeply mysterious. BepiColombo's arrival promises to reveal whether Mercury is truly shrinking, as suggested, and to open a new chapter in understanding how rocky planets form and evolve—questions that reach far beyond this small, scorched world.

The 'socialist utopia' that took a group of idealistic Australians to Paraguay more than 130 years ago

A 'utopia socialista' que levou um grupo de australianos idealistas ao Paraguai há mais de 130 anos

In the late 1890s, a group of disillusioned Australian workers undertook an extraordinary journey to Paraguay, seeking to build a socialist utopia far from home. Led by English journalist William Lane, these laborers—including sheep shearers like William Wood—were fleeing economic crisis, drought, unemployment, and violent government crackdowns on strikes in Australia. Lane organized the New Australian Movement with a vision of creating an egalitarian society without government, bankers, or military oppression. Their destination was unexpected: Paraguay, a nation still recovering from the catastrophic War of the Triple Alliance (1864-1870), which had killed more than half its population and left a severe gender imbalance. The Australian settlers established two colonies, Nueva Australia and Cosme, bringing their dreams to a land they knew little about. The choice of Paraguay reflected both the country's demographic need for new settlers and Lane's search for a remote place where his radical social experiment might take root. More than 130 years later, descendants like Rodrigo Wood—grandson of those original pioneers—reflect on the remarkable courage and determination it took to leave everything behind for an uncertain dream. As one historian notes, this remains the only known case in Australian history where a large group organized specifically to emigrate. The story offers a quietly remarkable glimpse into how economic hardship and political idealism can drive people to cross oceans and continents, carrying hope for a better world to the most unexpected places.

3,000 years ago, workers in Egypt went on strike over delayed wages; learn the story

Há 3.000 anos, trabalhadores do Egito fizeram uma greve por salários atrasados; conheça a história

Three thousand years ago, in ancient Egypt, a group of skilled workers laid down their tools and walked off the job — not over working conditions or creative differences, but because their wages simply hadn't arrived. The episode, carefully documented on what's now known as the Turin Strike Papyrus, offers a surprisingly relatable glimpse into labor relations during the reign of Pharaoh Ramses III. The workers lived in Deir el-Medina, a desert village on the western bank of Thebes (modern-day Luxor), where they were responsible for constructing and decorating royal tombs in the Valley of the Kings and Queens. These weren't unskilled laborers but highly trained artisans, organized into two teams with foremen, scribes, and support staff. Their compensation came not in coins but in regular deliveries of grain, which they used to make bread and beer — staples of daily life. When those deliveries stopped arriving in the second month of the planting season, the workers marched to the nearby funerary temples, which doubled as storage and redistribution centers, and declared simply: "We are hungry." Their protest worked temporarily, but when the problem persisted, they escalated their demands all the way to the vizier and pharaoh. What makes this story quietly remarkable is how it bridges millennia with such immediacy. The workers didn't revolt against the system itself; they simply wanted what they'd been promised. Their ability to organize, withdraw their labor, and document their grievances reveals a level of agency and literacy that challenges simplistic narratives about ancient labor. It's a reminder that the fundamental tension between workers and administration is as old as organized society itself.
