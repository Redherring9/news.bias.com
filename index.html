Despite his ordeal, Tyson emerged remarkably uninjured, though noticeably thinner from days without food or water. This story is a quiet testament to the dedication of emergency services and the resilience of one very lucky dog. This story matters because it represents one of the most significant regulatory actions against a social media company over child safety, potentially setting a precedent for how platforms operate globally. While the settlement introduces new protections, it notably doesn't require Meta to end personalized recommendations or targeted advertising, leaving some experts questioning whether the changes go far enough to truly protect young users from the features that made these platforms concerning in the first place.

How decoding beluga whales’ chitchat may save them – and teach us more about ourselves

In the fog-shrouded waters of Canada's high Arctic, researcher Valeria Vergara sits in a tent listening through headphones to the underwater world of beluga whales. What she hears is a cacophony of clicks, whistles, and trills—the constant chatter that allows these social creatures to stay connected in murky, dim waters where visibility is poor. That moment of sitting blind in fog while surrounded by whale voices gave Vergara a profound insight: belugas live in a kind of perpetual visual fog too, relying on sound as the fabric of their lives. Vergara's research has revealed charming parallels between whale and human communication. Baby belugas, like human toddlers, must learn their repertoire over two to three years, babbling clumsily before mastering their pod's distinctive calls. One of their first accomplishments is developing an "acoustic name tag" to identify themselves. But there's a concerning catch: infant calls travel only a fraction of the distance that adult calls do—making them eighteen times more vulnerable to being drowned out by boat noise. When separated in large pods, baby belugas may become permanently lost from their mothers, unable to make themselves heard over human-generated sound. This research has already made a difference. In 2018, Canadian authorities closed key beluga gathering areas to boats, giving those "faint, tentative" baby voices a better chance of being heard. The story is a quiet reminder that listening—truly listening—can reveal not just the hidden lives of other species, but also surprisingly simple ways humans can help protect them in an increasingly noisy ocean.

Satellite proliferation puts astronomical observations at risk, study shows

Proliferação de satélites põe em risco observações astronômicas, mostra estudo

As satellite constellations multiply overhead, astronomers are sounding a quiet alarm about the future of cosmic observation. A new study from the European Southern Observatory examines what happens when tens of thousands—or even millions—of satellites cross the field of view of Earth's most powerful telescopes, leaving bright streaks across images of the night sky. The research, conducted at Chile's Paranal Observatory in the Atacama Desert, used simulations to model the impact of proposed satellite launches. Currently, about 15,000 satellites orbit Earth, but plans are underway to deploy more than 1.7 million additional ones, driven largely by internet services like SpaceX's Starlink and Amazon's Project Kuiper. Astronomer Olivier Hainaut explains that each satellite trail obscures a small portion of a telescope's view. One streak might be manageable, but as numbers climb into the hundreds of thousands, the cumulative effect could deprive some observatories of most of their data. The study found that 100,000 satellites would create a tolerable level of interference, but 300,000 could prove seriously disruptive. This story matters because it illuminates an emerging tension between two worthy goals: expanding global internet access and preserving humanity's window into deep space. The researchers emphasize that satellite internet services offer real benefits, particularly for underserved regions, and don't advocate abandoning them. Instead, they're calling for balance—a conversation about how technological progress and scientific discovery might coexist. The challenge, Hainaut notes, is that satellites are already launching while researchers are still assessing the consequences, making this a race between innovation and understanding.

China’s Tianmu Mountain reserve tackles modern conservation amid religious tradition

On Tianmu Mountain in eastern China, ancient religious traditions and modern conservation science are finding common ground. For over a millennium, Buddhist, Daoist, and Confucian beliefs have shaped local attitudes toward the forest, with temples once enforcing strict rules against harming trees and wildlife. Now, following a dramatic expansion in 2025, the area has become the 54,700-hectare Tianmushan-Qingliangfeng UNESCO World Biosphere Reserve—China's first expansion of its kind—and state regulations provide the formal legal framework for protection. Known as the "kingdom of big trees," the reserve shelters thousands of species, including endangered ginkgo trees, Chinese pangolins, and the rare Tianmu ironwood. What makes this conservation effort particularly noteworthy is its co-management approach: local communities helped develop the ten-year management plan, and residents in the transition zone's 41 villages hold decision-making power over ecotourism and sustainable development. This collaboration aims to resolve longstanding tensions, including a 2001 lawsuit over land-use restrictions. Park authorities and religious institutions are now encouraging younger generations to pursue ecotourism careers rather than traditional farming, aligning economic livelihoods with environmental health. Meanwhile, the reserve faces contemporary challenges like pine wilt disease and climate-driven shifts in forest composition. This story offers a quietly hopeful model for conservation: honoring the past while adapting to present needs. The belief that "every tree, flower, and bird carries a spirit" has kept the mountain thriving for centuries, and that spiritual foundation now supports a modern framework where protection and prosperity might grow together.

Yayoi Kusama, Japanese artist who turned hallucinations into art, dies at 97

Yayoi Kusama, the Japanese avant-garde artist who transformed her lifelong hallucinations into mesmerizing art, has died at 97. Known for her obsessive polka dots, whimsical pumpkin motifs, and immersive "infinity rooms" — mirrored spaces that project patterns endlessly — Kusama painted until her final days in August 2025. Her studio announced she "never set aside her brush," continuing her exploration of love and the eternal soul through her work. Born in 1929 in Matsumoto City, Kusama experienced visual and auditory hallucinations from childhood, hearing flowers speak and seeing dots everywhere. She left Japan at 27 for New York, where she quickly entered the avant-garde scene alongside Andy Warhol and other icons. Around 1977, she voluntarily entered a mental hospital in Japan, living there for decades while commuting daily to her studio. Art, she wrote, was the only method that relieved her pain and anxiety. Kusama's global influence reached a remarkable peak in Australia, where a 2024–2025 retrospective at Melbourne's National Gallery of Victoria became the most visited exhibition in the country's history, drawing over 500,000 people. Her ability to channel mental illness into joyful, participatory art that invites wonder rather than pity makes her story quietly extraordinary — a testament to resilience, creativity, and the transformative power of embracing one's unique vision.

The drug, daraxonrasib (marketed as Rasonque), targets a protein called RAS that drives tumor growth—a target researchers had struggled to reach for decades. The approval offers hope for adults with metastatic pancreatic adenocarcinoma who have already undergone at least one treatment or cannot tolerate combination therapies. Perhaps equally remarkable, more than 31 percent of patients experienced measurable tumor shrinkage, and only 1.2 percent had to stop treatment due to side effects, compared to over 11 percent in the chemotherapy group. These results were so robust that researchers declared the drug should become the new standard of care for second-line treatment. This story matters because pancreatic cancer has long been one of the most difficult cancers to treat, and breakthroughs have been rare. What are chagras, the rainforest 'farms' that challenge modern agriculture in Colombia and Ecuador

Qué son las chagras, las "granjas" en la selva tropical que desafían la agricultura moderna en Colombia y Ecuador

Deep in the Colombian Amazon, Kelly Johanna Yucuna tends a plot that might look chaotic to outsiders, but actually follows an ancient, carefully ordered system. Her chagra—a small farming plot of no more than two hectares—is part of an agricultural tradition that has shaped Indigenous life in the northern Amazon for at least 4,500 years. These plots are designed to work in harmony with the forest's ecological cycles, providing food for families while allowing wildlife to thrive and carbon to be efficiently captured. After five or six years of use, the land is returned to the forest. Creating a chagra is a deeply spiritual and communal process. Elders must approve each plot, and permission is requested from the forest spirits before any trees are cut. Communities carefully select areas, often prioritizing the removal of smaller trees and vines while preserving about half of the native tree species. The result is a system with considerably greater biodiversity than monoculture plantations like cacao, and one that stores carbon at levels comparable to secondary forests. Women plant the first seeds after a brief fallow period and controlled burn, with crops like yuca, plantain, and pineapple ready within a year. This story matters because chagras represent a sustainable alternative to modern industrial agriculture, one that has worked for millennia. Yet these systems now face threats from mining, deforestation, drug trafficking, and climate change. Understanding and protecting this Indigenous knowledge isn't just about preserving culture—it's about learning from an agricultural approach that feeds communities while nurturing the forest rather than destroying it.

How rewilding could help stop the spread of Britain’s wildfires

Britain's iconic purple moorlands, celebrated in literature as wild and timeless, are actually the product of recent human intervention—drainage, grazing, and controlled burning over roughly the past 150 years. This transformation has left nearly 80% of England's peatlands dry and degraded, making them far more vulnerable to catastrophic wildfires. Now, scientists and land managers are proposing that thoughtful rewilding could help reverse this trend by restoring the wetter, more varied landscapes that once existed. The key lies in rebuilding nature's fire defenses through deliberate ecological restoration. Rewetting degraded peatlands by blocking drains and reintroducing water-retaining Sphagnum mosses has already shown promise; after a 2019 Scottish wildfire, restored areas proved far more resilient than degraded peat. Restoring natural river patterns—allowing streams to meander and floodplains to reconnect—can create natural firebreaks and wildlife refuges. Early results from the North York Moors show that even a small number of beaver dams can significantly increase water storage. Reintroducing large herbivores like cattle, horses, and even bison could create a patchwork of varied vegetation rather than continuous stretches of flammable heather, further reducing fire risk. This story matters because it challenges simplistic narratives about rewilding and fire risk. Rather than abandoning land to accumulate fuel, strategic rewilding offers a way to work with natural processes, creating landscapes that are both ecologically richer and more resilient to the climate challenges ahead. It's a quiet revolution in how we think about managing Britain's uplands for an uncertain future.
