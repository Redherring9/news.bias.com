Caatinga respondeu por quase 40% das remoções de gases de efeito estufa no Brasil em 2022, aponta pesquisa da UFCG

Brazil's Caatinga, a semi-arid biome often overshadowed by the Amazon rainforest, has emerged as a quietly powerful ally in the fight against climate change. Research from the Federal University of Campina Grande reveals that in 2022, the Caatinga was responsible for removing 410 million tons of CO₂ from the atmosphere—nearly 40% of Brazil's total greenhouse gas removals that year. This figure surpassed both the Atlantic Forest and the Cerrado, challenging assumptions about which ecosystems contribute most to carbon sequestration. The Caatinga's effectiveness stems from its unique characteristics. During photosynthesis, the biome converts approximately 60% of absorbed carbon dioxide into biomass—leaves, branches, trunks, and roots—compared to just 30% in the Amazon, where ecosystem respiration returns more carbon to the air. The region's dramatic seasonal shifts also play a role: after months of drought, just a few days of rain can reactivate photosynthesis, transforming the landscape into an intense carbon sink. Scientists note that semi-arid ecosystems worldwide significantly influence the annual variation in the global carbon cycle. Despite this remarkable potential, the Caatinga remains underrepresented in Brazil's emerging carbon credit market, which became regulated in 2024. Most measurement methodologies were developed for humid environments and don't adapt well to semi-arid conditions. Researchers are working to develop new approaches that reflect the biome's unique dynamics and enable local communities and small producers to access certification. This story offers a reminder that climate solutions may be hiding in unexpected places, and that ecosystems long considered marginal might hold outsized importance in our environmental future.

Margaret Hamilton obituary

Margaret Hamilton, who passed away at age 90, was a pioneering software engineer whose work proved essential to the Apollo moon landings. In an era when NASA's space program was overwhelmingly male and white, Hamilton led the software engineering division at MIT's Instrumentation Laboratory, overseeing development of the Apollo Guidance Computer—a revolutionary system that allowed astronauts to navigate and control spacecraft with unprecedented precision. Hamilton's innovation replaced the joystick controls of earlier Gemini missions with computer-linked keypads, creating more accurate flight paths and freeing astronauts for other critical tasks. The system represented a technological leap: it was the first computer built on silicon-integrated circuits, the same technology that would eventually power everyday devices from smartphones to laptops. Her meticulous preparation proved vital during Apollo 11's historic descent in July 1969, when alarms threatened to abort the landing. Having run countless simulations, Hamilton immediately recognized the problem—a radar switch left active, an error she'd seen Buzz Aldrin make during training. Her prior instructions to mission controllers allowed them to reassure Neil Armstrong the alarm could be ignored, and minutes later, the Eagle landed safely. Born in Indiana in 1936 and trained in mathematics, Hamilton began her career developing weather-prediction software alongside chaos theory pioneer Edward Lorenz before joining the Apollo program in 1965. Her story offers a quiet reminder of how individual expertise and calm judgment can shape history's most dramatic moments, and how exceptional talent found its place even within the rigid hierarchies of the 1960s space race.

Japan to launch world-first mission to collect samples from Martian moon

Japan is preparing to launch an ambitious spacecraft mission that aims to do something no one has attempted before: bring back samples from Phobos, one of Mars's two small, irregularly shaped moons. The Martian Moons eXploration (MMX) mission, led by the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency with international support from NASA and European partners, is scheduled to lift off on a new H3 rocket and embark on a five-year journey that could answer longstanding questions about how these mysterious moons formed. Phobos and its smaller companion Deimos have puzzled scientists for decades. Despite numerous missions to Mars since the 1960s, these moons remain largely unexplored—previous Russian attempts to land on Phobos have all failed. Researchers aren't even certain what these crater-pocked bodies are made of or where they came from. Some believe they're debris from an ancient collision with Mars, while others think they may be captured asteroids from elsewhere in the solar system. The mission will spend three years flying near Phobos, deploying a French-German rover to scout the surface before touching down to collect at least 10 grams of soil using both American and Japanese sampling techniques. Scientists expect the samples to contain traces of Martian material as well, offering a dual window into planetary history. This mission matters because it represents a careful, collaborative effort to explore what project scientists call "the most important unclimbed mountains in the solar system." Beyond satisfying scientific curiosity about our celestial neighborhood, the data gathered could inform future human missions to Mars, potentially identifying these moons as staging areas for astronauts. It's a reminder that even in our own backyard of space, profound mysteries remain waiting to be solved.

Origin of animals may be up to 200 million years older

Origem dos animais pode ser até 200 milhões de anos mais antiga

One of evolutionary biology's enduring puzzles centers on the relatively sudden appearance of animals in the fossil record during the Cambrian period, roughly 539 to 487 million years ago. Though some evidence has hinted at earlier origins, the precise timing has remained elusive. Now, after analyzing microscopic organisms from ancient Mongolian deposits, researchers propose that the animal kingdom may have emerged up to 200 million years earlier than previously thought—between 800 and 700 million years ago, before Earth experienced its most severe ice ages. The study challenges long-held assumptions based on China's Weng'an biota, a 590-million-year-old fossil site renowned for its exceptional preservation of microscopic organisms. Scientists had reasoned that if animals existed before Weng'an, such a well-preserved site would surely have captured their fossils. But the newly studied Kheseen biota from Mongolia—40 million years younger than Weng'an and equally well-preserved—also contains no animal fossils, despite clear evidence that animals existed elsewhere at that time. This absence, researchers argue, likely reflects differences in habitat or chemical conditions rather than the non-existence of animals. Using molecular clock analysis alongside much older fossil deposits from Norway, Australia, and Arizona, the team pushed back estimates for animal origins by some 200 million years. This story offers a reminder that absence of evidence isn't always evidence of absence. It illustrates how scientific understanding evolves not through dramatic finds alone, but through careful reconsideration of what we haven't found—and why. For anyone curious about life's deep history, it's a quietly profound shift in perspective on when our fellow creatures first appeared on Earth.

Scientists unearth remarkable volcanic origins of Lord Howe Island

Lord Howe Island, a World Heritage site rising dramatically from the Tasman Sea 600 kilometers off Australia's east coast, holds a geological secret beneath its surface. The island's towering basalt peaks are the visible remains of a 7-million-year-old shield volcano, but it's merely the tip of a vast underwater mountain range. Two parallel chains of mostly submerged extinct volcanoes stretch for 1,500 kilometers along Australia's eastern seaboard, their unusual proximity puzzling scientists for years. New research published in Gondwana Research offers an elegant explanation for this geological oddity. As the Australian tectonic plate has drifted northward over tens of millions of years, a deep Earth hotspot—essentially a blowtorch of super-heated rock rising from the planet's interior—has punched through the crust, creating volcanoes. Each volcano is eventually carried away by the plate's movement, forming chains like breadcrumbs marking the continent's journey. Lead author Ben Mather and his team used 3D numerical modeling to demonstrate that the mantle plume likely encountered an obstacle deep underground—a slab of ancient seafloor that had sunk hundreds of kilometers into the Earth. Rather than stopping, the plume split into two branches, creating both the Lord Howe and Tasmantid seamount chains from a single source. This story quietly rewrites our understanding of how the Earth's interior shapes the surface we see. It reminds us that even familiar landmarks like Lord Howe Island are chapter markers in a planetary story written over millions of years, and that the ground beneath our feet—or beneath the waves—remains full of surprises waiting to be decoded.
