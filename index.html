Understanding the tradition of giving candy on Cosme and Damião Day and meeting the little-remembered triplet of the celebration

Entenda tradição de dar doces no Dia de Cosme e Damião e conheça o trigêmeo pouco lembrado da festa

Every September in Brazil, a colorful tradition unfolds: children wait expectantly for bags of candy, lollipops, and treats handed out in celebration of Cosme and Damião. The practice blends Catholic and Afro-Brazilian religious traditions, honoring twin saints from Christian history while also connecting to the Ibeji—twin deities associated with childhood in African diaspora religions. While the Catholic calendar marks the feast on September 26, Brazilian popular tradition centers on September 27, when devotees and terreiros distribute sweets to children across the country. What many don't know is that the celebration includes a third, often-forgotten figure: Doum. Unlike the twins Cosme and Damião, who appear in Christian tradition as brothers who practiced medicine and cared for the sick, Doum emerges from Afro-Brazilian oral traditions and religious practice. According to researcher and priest Oloirê Costa, Doum is sometimes understood as a younger sibling or as the child who completes and balances the pair. His presence carries particular significance: he represents the child who hasn't yet reached the table, the one at risk of being forgotten—children living in vulnerability, facing hunger, abandonment, or violence. The act of giving candy itself holds deeper meaning than simple sweetness. The treat symbolizes happiness and the wish for a sweeter life, functioning as a gesture of memory, welcome, and hope. As Costa explains, when we hand a child a piece of candy during this celebration, we're offering more than sugar—we're extending an invitation to joy and belonging. This quiet tradition asks an important question that resonates beyond the festival: who is still missing from our table?

This story matters not only as aviation history but as a portrait of quiet competence under unimaginable pressure. Fuzimoto was a devoted father of four, a private man who passed his passion for flight to his son Alexandre, and someone whose "silent generosity" deepened after personal loss. His split-second choices in 1973 represent both the weight pilots carry and the humble origins from which extraordinary courage can emerge.

A plan to save Bali’s lost fireflies

On the Indonesian island of Bali, a conservation effort is working to restore something that has quietly vanished from the night: fireflies. Wayan Wardika remembers childhood evenings when clouds of fireflies lit the rice fields around his village, before electricity arrived. Four decades later, he noticed they had almost completely disappeared. In 2020, he launched Bring Back the Light, Indonesia's only firefly conservation program, determined to reverse their decline. Fireflies across Southeast Asia are struggling due to habitat loss, light pollution, pesticide use, and climate change. Their disappearance matters beyond nostalgia: fireflies pollinate crops, and some species feed on snails that damage rice plants, offering natural pest control. At least four Southeast Asian species are nearing extinction. Working with a small team of scientists on his family farm, Wayan breeds fireflies through their delicate life cycle—from eggs to larvae (which can take up to two years to mature), then pupae, and finally adults that live just three weeks. The team has released nearly 20,000 fireflies, and local populations are slowly rebounding. But Wayan's mission has evolved beyond breeding. Fireflies are sensitive environmental indicators; their presence signals healthy ecosystems. He now focuses on habitat restoration, encouraging neighboring farmers to replace chemical fertilizers with organic compost made from cow manure. This story offers a reminder that conservation often starts small and local—one dedicated person noticing an absence, then patiently working to bring back the light, one firefly at a time.

Mary River proves Australia's most degraded catchments can be restored

The Mary River in south-east Queensland was once labeled one of Australia's most degraded waterways, churning brown with sediment during floods and threatening the southern reaches of the Great Barrier Reef. What makes this story remarkable is not the damage, but the quiet, decades-long effort to undo it. In the 1980s, dairy farmers Peter and Elke Watson were considered oddities for planting trees and fencing cattle away from creek banks. Today, their pioneering work represents a broader transformation across the 9,595-square-kilometre catchment, led by community groups, landholders, and volunteers. A key breakthrough has been pile field technology—rows of hardwood poles driven into eroded banks, reshaped and replanted with native vegetation. These engineered interventions have stabilized sites once thought beyond repair, preventing an estimated 27,000 tonnes of sediment annually from reaching the reef and protecting vital seagrass meadows for fish, turtles, and dugongs. The Mary River Catchment Coordinating Committee has worked with landholders on over 850 projects, while volunteers regularly test water quality to track progress. The catchment is also home to several endangered species, including the Mary River turtle and the nearly extinct Mary River cod, whose recovery depends on continued habitat restoration. This story is worth a reader's time because it offers something increasingly rare: evidence that environmental damage can be reversed through patience, collaboration, and innovation. It's a reminder that large-scale ecological repair is possible—not through grand proclamations, but through the steady accumulation of small, community-driven actions over thirty years.

Global coral reefs get less time to recover as oceans heat up, report finds

The world's coral reefs are facing a double challenge: declining health and vanishing recovery time. A comprehensive new assessment tracking more than 21 million observations across 123 countries reveals that global hard coral cover has dropped 9.5% compared to a baseline period from 1980 to 2009. The report, released by the Global Coral Reef Monitoring Network, represents the most extensive survey of reef health ever conducted, offering a sobering snapshot of ecosystems under mounting thermal stress. The timing is particularly alarming. Between 2023 and 2025, reefs endured their fourth global bleaching event—episodes where heat-stressed corals expel the algae they depend on for survival. This latest event triggered the steepest single-year decline on record: an 8.9% drop in just twelve months. While reefs demonstrated resilience after the previous bleaching event, bouncing back by about 6% between 2017 and 2019, the intervals between these crises have shortened dramatically. Marine heat waves now strike roughly every four to six years, down from once per decade, compressing the window corals need to rebuild. What makes this story quietly urgent is its clarity. Hard corals aren't just beautiful; they're the architects of reef ecosystems, and their cover serves as a straightforward, globally comparable measure of reef vitality. Scientists emphasize that thoughtful local management can help fortify reef resilience, but without rapid reductions in greenhouse gas emissions, even the most robust conservation efforts may not be enough. This report offers a rare, data-rich view into an ecosystem racing against a clock that keeps speeding up.

Zuenir Ventura, journalist and writer with seven decades of career, dies at 95

Zuenir Ventura, jornalista e escritor com sete décadas de carreira, morre aos 95 anos

Brazil has lost one of its most distinguished voices in journalism and literature. Zuenir Ventura, who died at 95, spent seven decades documenting the nation's most pivotal moments with a pen guided by both rigor and compassion. A member of the Brazilian Academy of Letters, he was known not only for his celebrated work but for his gentle mentorship of younger colleagues—a quality that made him beloved across generations of reporters and readers alike. Ventura's path to greatness was anything but linear. Born in 1931 in the small town of Além Paraíba, he worked as a wall scraper for his house-painter father, then as a bank clerk, cleaner, bar attendant, and elementary school teacher before stumbling into journalism. He once confessed his true passion was teaching and reading, not writing—yet fate and extraordinary talent led him to some of Brazil's most influential newsrooms. His career blossomed at publications like Tribuna da Imprensa, Jornal do Brasil, and O Globo, where his final column appeared in 2022, reflecting on pandemic grief, racism, and a brutal murder that shook Rio de Janeiro. What makes Ventura's story quietly remarkable is how his humble beginnings enriched rather than hindered his journalism. His landmark book, "1968: The Year That Never Ended," chronicled student uprisings against military dictatorship with such vitality that the year itself became a character. Another work documented the Vigário Geral massacre, for which he spent ten months in the favela listening and learning. Ventura understood that great reporting requires not just skill, but the patience and empathy he carried from his early years—qualities that transformed him into "Mestre Zu," a master whose legacy lives in the stories he told and the journalists he inspired.

While the overall world record of 2.76 seconds belongs to a nine-year-old boy from Poland, Lian's performance places her among an elite group of competitors in what has become a surprisingly sophisticated and global competitive scene. This story offers a window into the surprisingly rigorous world of speedcubing, where competitors race not just against each other but against the limits of human reaction time and spatial reasoning. Cautious farmers embrace renewable energy to boost income streams

Bridget Johnson's family, cattle breeders in South Australia's Upper South East, exemplifies this evolving mindset. For the Johnsons, the decision hinged on facts rather than perception, and they found the project compatible with their beef operation. Transparency and clear communication emerge as crucial: farmers want evidence-based information about roads, power lines, and how projects will affect their work. This story matters because it captures a quiet but significant transformation in rural communities. What makes this shift remarkable is its grounded nature—farmers weighing turbines against rain gauges, seeking stability in an uncertain climate, both meteorological and economic. It's a reminder that major energy transitions happen not through grand pronouncements but through individual families making careful, consequential decisions about their land and livelihood.

California Tribes receive beach and ancestral village site back from transportation authority

In a quiet but significant act of restoration, California's Department of Transportation has returned 136 acres of Northern California coastline to three Indigenous tribes—the first time the agency has ever transferred land back to Native ownership. The property includes Blues Beach and K'etim, an ancestral village site sacred to the Coastal Yuki and Pomo peoples, where creation stories still echo and ancestors rest beneath the bluffs where tall trees meet the Pacific. The journey to reclaim this land began in 2005, when tribal members learned that looters were disturbing archaeological sites and unearthing human remains at K'etim. Eddie Knight and others from the Coyote Valley Band of Pomo Indians, Round Valley Indian Tribes, and Sherwood Valley Band of Pomo Indians immediately asked for stewardship. What followed was nearly two decades of persistence, culminating in a 2021 state law specifically authorizing the transfer to Kai Poma, a nonprofit formed by the three tribes to receive and manage the site. The land includes half a mile of beach, terraced land holding archaeological treasures, and former grazing slopes descending from Highway 1 to the sea. This story matters not for its drama but for its patient resolve and what it represents: a recognition that some places belong to those whose stories are woven into the land itself. The beach will remain open to the public, and the tribes are considering building a traditional roundhouse and holding ceremonies on the buildable portions. It's a reminder that restoration—of land, of relationships, of memory—happens not in grand gestures but in the steady insistence that what was taken can, sometimes, find its way home.

World Rhino Day: What does it take to save a species?

World Rhino Day offers a moment to reflect on the painstaking work of bringing rhino populations back from the brink. Across multiple continents, conservationists are reintroducing these massive animals to landscapes where they once thrived, and the efforts are showing cautious signs of success. In Uganda, southern white rhinos have returned for the first time since the last wild northern white rhino was killed in 1983. Mozambique's Zinave National Park, once silenced by civil war and wildlife loss, now hosts 64 reintroduced black and white rhinos, with at least five black rhino calves born in what was once a battlefield. The rhinos serve an ecological role beyond their own survival: as grazers, they keep grasslands in check, reducing wildfire risk and creating habitat for other species. Meanwhile, in India's Manas National Park, 42 greater one-horned rhinos brought in over fifteen years have produced 40 calves, a hopeful indicator that the animals are adapting to their new home. Nepal's rhino numbers have grown by nearly 17% in recent years, though researchers caution that population size alone doesn't tell the whole story. What makes these efforts meaningful is the attention to detail. Conservationists are tracking reintroduced rhinos with GPS collars, studying their movements, and learning that habitat quality matters as much as numbers. When rhinos in Nepal's Bardiya National Park traveled unexpectedly long distances in search of water and better grazing, it became clear that true conservation success requires resilient ecosystems, not just rising counts. These stories remind us that saving a species is a quiet, deliberate act—one that demands patience, collaboration, and a willingness to learn as we go.

El Niño: Pacific Ocean reaches record daily warming mark even months before peak

El Niño: Oceano Pacífico atinge marca recorde de aquecimento diário mesmo meses antes do pico

The equatorial Pacific Ocean is experiencing unprecedented daily temperature spikes months before El Niño is expected to reach its peak, raising concerns that the 2026 event could become the strongest on record. Climate monitoring from the University of Maine's Climate Reanalyzer shows daily temperature anomalies in the critical Niño 3.4 region have already exceeded 3°C—surpassing even the peak readings from the powerful 2015 El Niño event. While daily spikes are dramatic, scientists emphasize that monthly averages determine the official intensity classification. September's average stood at 2.1°C above normal, placing the phenomenon in the "very strong" category according to NOAA's scale. Climatologist José Marengo from Brazil's Cemaden research center notes that the peak warming is still expected between November 2026 and January 2027, with monthly averages potentially reaching 2.6°C to 2.8°C. El Niño occurs when Pacific equatorial waters warm by at least 0.5°C, disrupting atmospheric circulation and weather patterns across the globe. This story matters because it offers a real-time glimpse into how climate patterns unfold and intensify over months, revealing both the precision of modern ocean monitoring and the fundamental unpredictability of ocean-atmosphere interactions. The early intensity suggests significant weather impacts ahead for regions worldwide, yet scientists maintain humility about forecasting—a reminder that even with advanced tools, Earth's climate system retains an element of mystery.

Her efforts focus on the Dilley facility in southern Texas, the only family detention center in the country, which reopened under the Trump administration after being closed during the Biden years. Luis Sánchez, a Nicaraguan father detained with his four children for over three weeks, was initially skeptical when another detainee told him about Revkin. After his lawyer confirmed it was real, he emailed asking for help. What makes this story quietly remarkable is its reminder that amid large policy debates and headlines about immigration enforcement, there are concrete human needs—and individuals willing to meet them. Revkin's work doesn't solve systemic issues, but it offers something tangible: a way for parents to care for their children in circumstances designed to strip away that dignity. It's a story about what one person, motivated by empathy and a mother's imagination, can do when she simply decides to help.
