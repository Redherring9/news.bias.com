This story offers a quiet testament to patience and partnership. The colony is co-managed by the Pukekura Co-management Trust, blending the efforts of Te Rūnanga o Ōtākou, local councils, and conservation groups. New museum's garden artwork recovered four lost First Nations words

A new art installation at Sydney's Powerhouse Parramatta is quietly correcting a historical injustice that erased an Indigenous man's contributions to early Australian botany. Daniel Moowattin, a Burramattagal man who worked with British botanist George Caley in the early 1800s, was the person actually collecting plant specimens—climbing trees and gathering clippings—yet his name was systematically removed from official records. While Caley's personal herbarium notes acknowledged Moowattin's work with handwritten annotations like "got by Dan," these attributions vanished when the records were formalized, leaving Moowattin largely forgotten. Artist Keg de Souza and Dharug elder Uncle Chris Tobin traced Moowattin's footsteps, collecting eucalyptus clippings from the same country where he once worked. Curator Emerson Radisich spent months searching archives worldwide, eventually finding about 100 herbarium sheets at London's Natural History Museum. Among these records, the team discovered handwritten notations in the Dharug language—including four words related to eucalyptus species that had been lost to time. One word, Dhuramba (the name for Eucalyptus Paniculata), has been confirmed, with three others awaiting formal publication. The bronze plaques now embedded in the museum's rooftop garden do more than commemorate one man's overlooked work. They represent a broader reckoning with how Indigenous knowledge has been appropriated and erased. As Uncle Chris observed seeing the installation for the first time, it helps people understand that Aboriginal history isn't just part of Parramatta's story—it's foundational to it. This quiet act of recovery reminds us that behind many scientific achievements are untold collaborators whose contributions deserve recognition.

Pediatric palliative care addresses the unique needs of seriously ill children and their families

Pediatric palliative care is a specialized field that addresses a reality many find difficult to contemplate: children facing serious, life-limiting illnesses. This approach emerged in the 1990s and early 2000s, adapting the adult hospice model to meet the distinct needs of young patients and their families. Rather than simply managing end-of-life care, it focuses on making every day as meaningful as possible, whether a child has months or years remaining. The care model brings together physicians, nurses, social workers, chaplains, and other specialists who work as a team to address physical, emotional, social, and spiritual needs. Research over the past two decades shows tangible benefits: children experience less pain and fewer distressing symptoms, and families receive crucial support in navigating complex medical decisions. For instance, when a palliative care team is involved, families are better equipped to choose treatments that genuinely improve quality of life rather than prolonging suffering. This holistic approach also helps ensure that intensive care interventions align with a family's actual goals, rather than defaulting to every possible medical intervention regardless of outcome. While nearly 80% of U.S. children's hospitals now have formal palliative care programs—making America a leader in this field—access remains inconsistent, particularly outside hospital settings. Globally, with an estimated 7 million children dying annually, the need far exceeds availability. This story matters because it illuminates a compassionate, evidence-based approach to one of life's most difficult experiences, reminding us that even in the face of profound loss, care can focus on comfort, dignity, and moments of joy.

Qué es el "zeptoverso", el mundo más pequeño jamás detectado en la naturaleza y por qué es clave para entender el universo

Scientists have detected signals from the smallest scale of nature ever explored — a realm 100 billion times smaller than an atom that some physicists are calling the "zeptoverse." The discovery, made by Cambridge-based researchers using the Large Hadron Collider at CERN, represents the culmination of more than 40 years of searching and could open new pathways to understanding dark matter and the fundamental workings of the universe. The breakthrough centers on observing an extremely rare decay of the bottom quark, a subatomic particle that exists for only 1.5 trillionths of a second before disintegrating. Researchers managed to detect its interactions with other tiny particles at a scale of one billionth of a meter. At these minuscule dimensions, particles may be more sensitive to dark matter, the mysterious substance that makes up much of our cosmos but remains invisible except through its gravitational effects. Dr. Harry Cliff, who led the data analysis, described the work as "knocking on the door of the zeptoverse," explaining that this subatomic dance gives researchers a privileged view of how potential new particles behave. This story offers a glimpse into the patient, decades-long work of pushing the boundaries of human knowledge into realms almost impossible to imagine. It reminds us that understanding the universe sometimes means looking not outward into the vastness of space, but inward into the inconceivably small — where the answers to cosmic mysteries may quietly wait to be discovered, one billionth of a trillionth of a meter at a time.

Molecular traces from 'blood cells' found in 115-million-year-old dinosaur bones

Scientists studying the fossilized bones of a 115-million-year-old Brazilian dinosaur have reported finding molecular traces of heme—a key component of hemoglobin that carries oxygen in red blood cells. The discovery, made by a team led by Curtin University's Kliti Grice, was found within ellipsoid structures in the vascular canals of Aratasaurus museunacionali, a feathered dinosaur estimated to have been about four years old when it died. According to Professor Grice, this marks the first time anyone has detected heme inside a fossil blood cell, opening new avenues for understanding dinosaur metabolism and physiology. The remarkable preservation may have been aided by microbial mats—colonies of bacteria and archaea common in coastal environments—that engulfed the dinosaur's remains. The researchers believe that conditions created by these microorganisms, including oxidation and simultaneous mineralization, helped preserve soft tissue over millions of years. The bones, discovered in a Brazilian mine in 2008, were analyzed using multiple techniques including electron microscopy and mass spectrometry. Surprisingly, the samples were found to be nearly 80 percent heme, though tests for ancient DNA came back negative. While the findings add to growing evidence that soft tissue can survive in dinosaur fossils longer than once thought possible, some experts remain cautious. Not all scientists are convinced the structures definitively represent red blood cells, noting that interpretation is still required when examining chemical remnants and mineralized shapes. Still, even skeptics acknowledge the presence of heme and collagen is remarkable. The study offers a glimpse into how ancient life might be preserved in unexpected ways, and researchers are now testing whether similar fossilization could occur with human blood in microbial mats from Western Australia.
