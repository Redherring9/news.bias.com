Should you take the plunge? How to tell how clean the sea is

As concerns about water quality make headlines across the UK, beachgoers are increasingly seeking reliable ways to assess whether it's safe to swim. The question of clean water has become more pressing, with sewage spills, agricultural runoff, and other pollutants potentially affecting coastal waters—though the picture is more nuanced than it might first appear. Several systems now exist to help swimmers make informed decisions. The Environment Agency rates designated bathing waters from "excellent" to "poor" based on bacteria samples collected over four years, testing for E. coli and intestinal enterococci that can cause illness. However, this official monitoring only runs from mid-May through September and doesn't reflect day-to-day conditions. Water companies provide their own alert systems, like South West Water's WaterFit Live, which notifies users of potential sewage impacts for specific periods after spills. Meanwhile, the campaign group Surfers Against Sewage has developed an app that aggregates data from multiple sources and maintains pollution alerts for 48 hours as a precautionary measure. The Blue Flag scheme offers another benchmark, assessing beaches against strict international criteria. This story matters because it highlights both progress and limitations in public information about water safety. While more tools exist than ever before, gaps remain—official testing is seasonal, alerts vary between systems, and a sewage spill doesn't automatically mean unsafe conditions. For anyone planning a coastal swim, understanding these different resources and their constraints offers a clearer path to making thoughtful choices about when and where to take the plunge.

Dry July for Western Australia as stunning pictures tell story

The unusual dry spell was caused by stubborn high-pressure systems that settled over southern Australia, blocking the cold fronts that normally sweep in from the Southern Ocean to deliver winter rain. The clear skies brought frosty mornings to inland areas, with some locations dropping below zero multiple times. Scientist's lifelong obsession with mystery of elusive 'pink zebra' seaweed

Off the coast of Cornwall, beneath waters most associate with the Great Barrier Reef's fame, lies a quieter marvel: maerl beds, ancient carpets of calcified seaweed that host some of the ocean's richest life. Forty years ago, marine scientist Christine Maggs discovered a tiny fragment of pink-striped seaweed living on maerl during her PhD research. It was unlike anything in the scientific literature, and she suspected it might be new to science. But proving that required a larger sample—something that would elude her for decades. Throughout her career, Maggs caught only fleeting glimpses of the elusive "pink zebra" seaweed, never enough to confirm its identity. Then, two years ago, specialist divers surveying the health of Cornish maerl beds brought up samples that independent marine biologist Francis Bunker thought might be what Maggs had been seeking. He sent the specimens to her in London, where she nurtured them in a wine fridge—the perfect cool, dark environment—until they grew large enough for DNA sequencing. The molecular analysis, conducted with colleague Beatriz de Barros Barreto, confirmed what Maggs had hoped: the seaweed was indeed a species new to science. A sample now resides in the Natural History Museum's official collection, and the findings will soon be published in the journal Phycologia. This story reminds us that discovery doesn't always require distant expeditions or high-tech laboratories. Sometimes it asks for patience, a magnifying glass, and four decades of quiet determination. In an age of rapid information, Maggs' lifelong pursuit of one small seaweed offers a different rhythm—one where curiosity, persistence, and wonder reveal that even well-studied waters still hold secrets waiting to be named.

Drifting tuna fishing gear enter half of marine protected areas: study

Industrial tuna fishing gear is drifting into marine protected areas across the globe, carrying consequences that ripple far beyond the fishing grounds where the devices were first deployed. A recent study estimates that drifting fish aggregating devices—floating rafts equipped with satellite trackers that help vessels locate tuna—have affected more than half of the world's protected marine areas. Over a 16-year period, researchers documented at least 6,300 strandings of these devices across 174 protected sites in 53 countries, some traveling thousands of kilometers before washing ashore or sinking. The devices, known as dFADs, are used by purse-seine vessels to attract skipjack, yellowfin, and bigeye tuna. While they're designed to be tracked and retrieved, many are abandoned, deactivated, or simply lost, continuing to drift on ocean currents long after they've served their purpose. When they strand in protected areas, they can damage coral reefs, entangle marine wildlife including sharks and turtles, and leave small island nations—which recorded roughly 30 percent of the documented strandings—with the difficult and costly task of removal. Hotspots were identified in the western and central Pacific, the western Indian Ocean, and the Caribbean, with French Polynesia, Seychelles, and the Maldives bearing the heaviest burden. This story offers a window into an often-invisible problem: the way fishing practices in one part of the ocean can affect ecosystems and communities thousands of miles away. It's a reminder that conservation boundaries drawn on maps can't stop the movement of debris on currents, and that protecting marine life may require rethinking how fishing gear is tracked, retrieved, and regulated across international waters.

England and Wales on course for driest July on record

England and Wales are experiencing their driest July since meteorological records began nearly two centuries ago. Wales has seen similarly stark conditions, recording 7.9mm of rain, also 8% of its average and far beneath its 1911 benchmark. The extreme dryness has prompted official drought declarations across more than half of England and all of Wales, with water being consumed faster than natural systems can replenish it. The consequences are tangible: rivers run low, harvests have come early, and more than 20 million people face hosepipe bans. The parched landscape has also heightened wildfire risk, with crews battling a blaze the size of 200 football pitches in Suffolk and a two-week fire in Scotland's Cairngorms that consumed 10 square miles of moorland. One location in Surrey recorded 44 consecutive rainless days. Beyond the lack of precipitation, July has also brought notable warmth. Wales is on track for its warmest July by mean temperature, while England appears headed for a joint record. The UK has already seen seven days above 35°C this year, surpassing previous records, and preliminary estimates suggest heat-related deaths may reach their highest levels since tracking began. This combination of drought and heat offers a sobering glimpse into how shifting weather patterns affect daily life, from water use to public health—a story worth watching as it quietly reshapes the rhythms of an entire region.

Smallpox, which plagued humanity for at least 1,500 years, was eradicated through global vaccination efforts in 1980. This research reminds us of both the devastating power of infectious disease and the scientific achievement of overcoming one of history's deadliest scourges.

Three-way deathmatch unearthed at 100-million-year-old 'crime scene'

A remarkable fossil discovery in outback Queensland has preserved a prehistoric drama that played out 100 million years ago in what was once the Eromanga Sea. The find captures a rare "three-tier predator interaction": an ichthyosaur (a dolphin-like marine reptile) that had eaten a pterosaur (a flying reptile) was itself attacked and killed by a massive kronosaurus, an ocean predator up to 14 metres long with more than 100 razor-sharp teeth. Travellers first stumbled upon the ichthyosaur remains in 2019, affectionately nicknamed "BOB" for "bag of bones." Three years later, closer examination revealed the extraordinary nature of the find. The ichthyosaur's midsection bore more than 30 bite marks—possibly the most ever recorded in a marine fossil—and its body had been violently shattered by the kronosaurus. Even more remarkable, preserved stomach contents showed pterosaur remains, marking the first documented case of an ichthyosaur having consumed one of these flying reptiles. The combination of predator, prey, and scavenger preserved together is exceptionally rare in the fossil record. While the precise sequence of events remains a mystery—did the kronosaurus leap from the water to attack, or was the ichthyosaur breaching to catch the pterosaur mid-flight?—the fossil offers an unprecedented window into ancient behaviour. As one palaeontologist noted, it's one of the clearest examples of three-tier predator interaction ever discovered. This "crime scene" from deep time reminds us that the drama of life and death has always been part of nature's story, and that sometimes, against all odds, those moments get preserved for us to puzzle over millions of years later.

‘They’re part of folklore’: storks return to Derbyshire after 600-year absence

Three juvenile white storks have arrived at Willington Wetlands in Derbyshire, marking the species' return to the region after more than 600 years. The birds—one male, one female, and one of undetermined sex—emerged from their transport crates to explore a specially prepared enclosure at the rewilded site, watched by an enthusiastic crowd of conservationists, volunteers, and birdwatchers. Their arrival represents years of careful habitat restoration and a hopeful step toward establishing a breeding population in the Midlands. White storks vanished from the area around the 14th century due to habitat loss and hunting. While recent sightings have increased as wild birds migrate from Africa through the Trent Valley, none have settled to breed. Conservationists hope this trio will serve as a "magnet flock," attracting passing storks and encouraging them to stay. The £309,000 project includes predator-proof enclosures, artificial nesting platforms in trees, and careful monitoring as the birds acclimate. The 40-hectare site, transformed from former gravel pits into thriving wetlands with pools and reed beds, also hosts Derbyshire's first beavers in over 800 years. This story offers a quiet reminder that landscapes can heal and species can return when given the chance. Beyond the storks themselves, the project reflects a broader vision: creating dynamic ecosystems that support countless species while rekindling human connection to the natural world. It's a patient, hopeful effort to restore not just a bird, but a piece of regional heritage and ecological richness that disappeared centuries ago.

Mining, drilling, and fire are fragmenting the world’s largest forests

The world's largest unbroken forests are disappearing at an accelerating pace, and the causes are shifting in unexpected ways. According to new research from the World Resources Institute, these "intact forest landscapes"—vast areas showing no signs of industrial activity—have shrunk by more than 15% since 2000, losing an area roughly the size of Mexico. Canada, Russia, and Brazil hold nearly two-thirds of what remains, and they've also seen the steepest declines. What's particularly striking is how fire has surged as a driver of loss, now accounting for nearly 40% of the reduction since 2020, up dramatically from 21% in earlier years. Meanwhile, logging has dropped to about one-fifth of recent losses. But perhaps most revealing is the rising impact of mining, drilling, and exploration, now the third-largest cause at nearly 19%. This figure doesn't primarily reflect direct land clearing—mines and oil wells themselves occupy relatively small footprints. Instead, it captures fragmentation: a single road, pipeline, or exploration corridor can slice a massive forest into smaller pieces, disqualifying them as "intact" even though much of the canopy remains. This story matters because it reveals how industrial infrastructure quietly unravels wilderness in ways satellite images of clear-cut land don't always capture. Once roads penetrate remote forests, they often invite logging, settlement, and additional development—cascading effects that extend far beyond the original project. The challenge isn't just protecting trees, but preserving the scale and continuity that make these forests ecologically irreplaceable. It's a reminder that intactness, once lost to fragmentation, is nearly impossible to restore.

Zoo staff rescue near-extinct snails from remote rock

In a dramatic conservation effort, specialists from Chester Zoo have successfully bred and returned 300 critically endangered Madeiran land snails to their native habitat on Portugal's remote Bugio Island. The rescue began when just 21 of these tiny snails—each no larger than 8 millimeters—were discovered clinging to a rock, representing what experts believed were the last surviving members of their species. The Madeira government reached out to Chester Zoo for help, setting in motion an urgent breeding program to pull the species back from the brink of extinction. The reintroduction proved to be one of the most challenging the zoo has ever attempted. Conservationists faced treacherous weather conditions and had to work within a narrow time window. With no jetty available on the isolated island, team members jumped directly into the Atlantic waters, then scaled cliff faces in darkness while wearing helmets and harnesses. Their mission: to find secure crevices in the rocks where the delicate snails could be safely placed to begin their new lives in the wild. Despite their diminutive size, these snails play an important ecological role, consuming vegetation and enriching the soil of their island ecosystem. This story offers a glimpse into the often-overlooked world of invertebrate conservation, where dedicated scientists risk physical danger to save species most people have never heard of. It's a reminder that biodiversity exists at every scale, and that even the smallest creatures contribute to the intricate web of life on our planet. The team will continue monitoring the snails as they adapt to their ancestral home once again.

Make the kids’ summer holidays go with a bang – play together like orangutans | Helen Pilcher

As parents face the annual challenge of filling six weeks of summer holidays with engaging activities, new research offers an unexpected source of inspiration: orangutans. Scientists studying wild Bornean orangutans have discovered that mothers deliberately arrange playdates for their young—a remarkable finding given that orangutans are among the most solitary of great apes, typically preferring their own territories and personal space. Researchers analyzed 30,000 hours of observations from 31 mother-offspring pairs and found that mothers made specific trips into neighboring territories to visit other mothers with similarly aged youngsters. These visits come at a real cost—time spent traveling means less time for feeding—suggesting the playdates serve an important purpose. This behavior is particularly notable because, unlike other social species where juvenile play happens naturally when adults gather, orangutan mothers actively orchestrate these encounters. The research hints at something profound about play itself: it appears across the animal kingdom from bear cubs to octopuses, caimans to corvids, and isn't merely frivolous fun but a crucial rehearsal for life, teaching communication, cooperation, and conflict resolution. What makes this story quietly remarkable is its dual revelation. First, it shows that even in solitary species, mothers recognize the developmental value of social play enough to invest precious energy in facilitating it. Second, research on human adults during the pandemic found that playful people coped better with stress—not by perceiving less risk, but by approaching challenges more creatively. The orangutan mothers arranging playdates despite the costs might be offering harried human parents not just solidarity, but permission: play isn't a luxury or childhood indulgence, but an essential tool for navigating life's challenges at any age.
