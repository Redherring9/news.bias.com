The Indian chef who brought curry to America - then vanished

In 1901, a charismatic Indian chef named Ranji Smile opened what was almost certainly America's first Indian restaurant—then disappeared from history almost as quickly as he arrived. His story offers a fascinating glimpse into both culinary innovation and the precarious nature of immigrant success in Gilded Age New York. Smile—likely a pseudonym combining the name of cricket star KS Ranjitsinhji and an anglicized "Ismail"—sailed to New York in 1899 after working as a curry cook at London's prestigious Cecil and Savoy hotels. Hired by Louis Sherry for his glittering Fifth Avenue restaurant, Smile became an immediate sensation. Dressed in a satin coat and blue silk turban, he turned curry service into theater, greeting diners with a salaam and asking whether they preferred their curry hot or mild. New York society, already curious about Indian culture through figures like Swami Vivekananda, was captivated. Headlines proclaimed that "New York women go wild over him." Riding this wave of celebrity, Smile opened Omar Khayyam in 1901, complete with Indian musicians, turbaned waiters, and ornate décor. Yet the restaurant failed within a year, dismissed by newspapers as a passing fad. Smile's fame faded, and the article hints at a darker turn involving deceit and criminal activity, though his ultimate fate remains unknown. His story is a reminder that culinary pioneers don't always find lasting success, even when they possess genuine talent and flair. It's a quietly poignant tale of ambition, reinvention, and the often-overlooked figures who helped shape America's diverse food landscape more than a century ago.

Finding megafauna footprints holds special meaning for these students

When ten-year-old Reece spotted a faint indentation in rocky beach crust near Fowlers Bay, South Australia, he had discovered something extraordinary: a fossilized footprint from a Procoptodon, a giant short-faced kangaroo that stood nearly three meters tall and vanished at least 15,000 years ago. Students from Koonibba Aboriginal School have been venturing far beyond their remote classroom, traveling 720 kilometers to the Flinders Ranges and their own coastal backyard to trace the ancient steps of Australia's megafauna—massive kangaroos, duck-like birds, and wombat-sized creatures that once roamed the continent. The most meaningful discovery for these students hasn't been the footprints themselves, but the realization that their ancestors walked alongside these giants tens of thousands of years ago. At the Yura Mulka petroglyph site, Adnyamathanha tour operator Kristian Coulthard showed them rock carvings made by his ancestors depicting megafauna, explaining how the depicted animals help date the artwork. Palaeontologist Aaron Camens guided the students in identifying different traces—hopping marks from giant kangaroos, round impressions from diprotodons, and three-toed prints from enormous birds—preserved when ancient sand layers hardened over roughly 120,000 years. These footprints appear and disappear with storms and erosion, making each discovery fleeting and precious. This story matters because it weaves together science, history, and cultural connection in a way that brings learning to life. These students aren't just studying paleontology—they're standing on one of Earth's oldest landscapes, engaging with one of its oldest living cultures, and discovering their own ancestors' intimate knowledge of a lost world. It's education that honors both scientific inquiry and Indigenous heritage, showing how the past remains present beneath our feet.

À la COP17, des textes clés adoptés pour protéger la biodiversité

Nearly 200 countries gathered in Yerevan, Armenia, for two weeks of intense negotiations at COP17, the global biodiversity summit, ultimately adopting key agreements aimed at protecting nature worldwide. The talks stretched to the final day, with plenary sessions repeatedly delayed as nations worked to bridge their differences. When the session finally opened, texts were swiftly approved to applause, though their full details weren't immediately released. The agreements focus on reviewing and accelerating progress toward the 2022 Kunming-Montreal biodiversity accord, often called the Paris Agreement equivalent for nature. That landmark deal set 23 targets for 2030, including protecting 30 percent of land and oceans, combating pollution and invasive species, and restoring degraded ecosystems. Nearly all these goals are currently behind schedule. One contentious point involved subsidies for activities harmful to nature, such as oil extraction. A second major text addressed financial mobilization, including debate over expanding the list of donor countries beyond a roster unchanged since 2006, with the EU pressing emerging economies like China to contribute. The summit also saw tensions over the scientific foundations guiding global biodiversity policy. While observers noted the agreements weren't particularly detailed or inspiring, simply reaching consensus represented progress at a moment when public finances are strained globally and responsibility for funding conservation remains hotly contested. This story matters because it captures the quiet, grinding work of international cooperation on an issue as critical as climate change, yet far less visible: the accelerating loss of the natural world that sustains us all.

Aztec methods and crocodiles: How biologists are restoring mangroves in Mexico

In the wetlands of Veracruz, Mexico, a conservation group called REVIVE is taking an unusual approach to mangrove restoration: combining ancient Aztec farming techniques with the reintroduction of crocodiles. The project addresses a critical challenge—Mexico has experienced the highest rate of mangrove loss in the Americas, and many restoration efforts fail because seedlings don't survive poor planting methods or unsuitable sites. The breakthrough came when biologist Aníbal Ramírez Soto observed that healthy mangroves grew on elevated clumps of land. Drawing inspiration from Aztec chinampas—small artificial islands used for farming—he began planting mangrove seedlings on raised mounds built from wetland soil. This technique reversed damage caused by cattle ranching, which had caused the ground to collapse and sink. The results were remarkable: not only did the planted seedlings thrive, but additional mangroves naturally took root on the mounds, essentially allowing the forest to heal itself. After just eight years, canopy cover reached 80%, and the restored areas now blend seamlessly with natural forest. The project has since scaled to 15,000 chinampas across 99 acres, with seventeen years of data supporting its success. REVIVE has also reintroduced crocodiles to help maintain the ecosystem, as the reptiles create channels and move sediment that keeps water flowing—though this aspect remains scientifically debated. This story offers a thoughtful example of how looking to traditional knowledge, combined with careful observation of nature, can solve modern environmental challenges. It's a reminder that successful restoration isn't just about planting trees, but about rebuilding entire ecosystems with patience and cultural wisdom.

A historic land restitution process offers new hope for Botswana’s Indigenous San

After nearly two decades of legal limbo, Indigenous San communities in Botswana may finally be on the path to reclaiming rights to their ancestral lands in the Central Kalahari Game Reserve. The story begins with a reserve established in 1961 both to protect wildlife and to provide sanctuary for the San people's traditional way of life. Despite a landmark 2006 court victory that found the evictions unlawful, broader questions about Indigenous land rights remained unresolved, leaving communities in economic and social hardship. The proposed framework would maintain the reserve's protected status while granting Indigenous communities legally recognized land rights and roles in conservation management. The plan envisions a four-phase process covering community representation, land rights negotiations, legal reform, and conservation co-management. This story matters because it represents a rare opportunity for genuine restitution after decades of displacement. Yet crucial details remain unsettled—whether rights will be individual or collective, how tourism revenues will be shared, and what meaningful participation will look like. It's a quiet but potentially transformative moment in the long struggle to balance conservation with Indigenous rights and dignity.

Bat Week: Celebrating ecologically important, poorly understood mammals

Bat Week, celebrated annually from October 24-31, shines a spotlight on a group of mammals that accounts for one-fifth of all mammal species yet remains surprisingly misunderstood. Found on every continent except Antarctica, bats provide vital ecosystem services including mosquito control, pollination, and seed dispersal. Despite their ecological importance and widespread presence, significant gaps remain in our understanding of these nocturnal creatures. Recent reporting reveals the scope of this knowledge gap across different regions. In India, home to 135 known bat species, fewer than 50 dedicated researchers study these animals, leaving seven species listed as threatened and 35 with unknown conservation status. Meanwhile, in New Zealand—where only two native land mammal species survive, both of them bats—a community-led initiative called Finding Franklin Bats has mobilized over 180 volunteers to survey populations and raise awareness among rural communities. In Kenya, researchers set up delicate nets at cave entrances at dusk, carefully measuring bats to understand their role in local ecosystems and how human activity affects their habitats. Pennsylvania Bat Rescue offers another perspective, rehabilitating injured bats while educating the public about their importance. This story matters because it reveals a quiet paradox: creatures so common and ecologically essential remain largely invisible to science and the public alike. Bat Week reminds us that understanding and protecting biodiversity begins with curiosity about the species living quietly alongside us, performing essential work in the shadows.
