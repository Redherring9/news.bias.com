When Charlie isn't at school, he's welding thousand dollar scrap sculptures

In a workshop in Dunsborough, Australia, 14-year-old Charlie Guiver transforms salvage yard scraps into striking metal sculptures, recently selling a large wolf piece for $8,000. Long before he could walk, Charlie was learning to use power tools under his parents' guidance, and he's since developed a distinctive sculptural practice that he describes as puzzle-solving with welded metal. His favorite creation is a kinetic eagle designed to move with the wind, and he dreams of traveling to Europe to study traditional blacksmithing. Charlie has become the youngest artist participating in the Margaret River Region Open Studios, the largest event of its kind in Australia, where 155 artists open their private workspaces to the public. For two weeks each September, the event draws over 100,000 visits and generates millions in sales, offering both artists and audiences a rare behind-the-scenes look at creative processes. This year marks a bittersweet transition: while Charlie opens his shed studio for the first time, veteran glass artist Gerry Reilly will close his doors after operating an open studio since 1991 and participating in the regional event since its beginning 13 years ago. This story captures a quiet changing of the guard in Australia's arts community—a teenager just beginning to share his work with the world as a master craftsman steps back after five decades. It's a reminder that creative passion can flourish at any age, and that opening studio doors to curious visitors helps bridge the gap between finished artwork and the skilled hands that make it possible.

Five night sky events to look out for this autumn

As autumn settles over the northern hemisphere, longer nights create ideal conditions for stargazing, and the coming months promise a series of celestial events worth venturing outside to witness. From ancient star clusters to speeding meteors, the sky offers a gentle reminder of our place in a dynamic cosmos. The season opens with the Pleiades, a cluster of young stars also known as the Seven Sisters, becoming increasingly visible in the constellation Taurus. Though named for seven, most observers see about six stars unaided, with binoculars revealing many more. Two meteor showers follow: the Orionids, peaking around October 21st as Earth passes through debris from Halley's comet, and the Leonids in mid-November, notable for being among the fastest meteors visible from Earth, traveling at roughly 70 kilometers per second. Between these showers, early risers on November 14th might catch a rare alignment of Mercury, Venus, Mars, and Jupiter above the pre-dawn horizon, though Mercury will prove elusive near the sun's glare. The season concludes with the Beaver supermoon on November 24th, named for the time when beavers busily prepare for winter, appearing slightly larger and brighter as it orbits closer to Earth. This story offers something quietly valuable: a calendar of wonder that requires no special equipment, just curiosity and a willingness to look up. In an age of endless digital distractions, these celestial events invite us to step outside, let our eyes adjust to darkness, and reconnect with rhythms that have marked human experience for millennia.

Fossilized 10,000-year-old mastodon tooth found by California scientist

A geomorphologist working at a preserve in San Mateo County, California, recently discovered what she initially thought was an unusual rock. The object turned out to be something far more remarkable: a 10,000-year-old molar from a Pacific mastodon, and according to paleontologists, it's in exceptionally pristine condition. Wayne Thompson, a paleontologist examining the find, called it the most complete and spectacular mastodon molar from the region he has ever encountered, describing the moment as giving him chills. Pacific mastodons, relatives of modern elephants, roamed the American West for millions of years before vanishing over 10,000 years ago. Their extinction resulted from a combination of climate change affecting their habitat and overhunting by humans. These herbivorous giants once wandered across what is now California, Oregon, the Rocky Mountains, and parts of Mexico. The tooth has been donated to Stanford University, where researchers plan to use carbon dating to pinpoint its exact age and learn more about the ecosystem these creatures inhabited. This discovery joins a growing collection of mastodon finds that continue to emerge across North America, including a jaw found beneath a New York lawn in 2024 and another tooth discovered on a California beach the year before. As researchers note, fossils like this tooth offer an irreplaceable window into ancient biodiversity, helping scientists understand past ecosystems and make more informed decisions about protecting wildlife today. It's a quiet reminder that traces of vanished worlds still rest beneath our feet, waiting to be found.

Auckland's City Rail Link stations are a feast for the eyes

After a decade of planning and construction, Auckland's new City Rail Link stations have opened as unexpected galleries of Māori art and storytelling. Three underground stations—Karanga-a-Hape, Maungawhau, and Te Waihorotiu—weave together contemporary design and traditional narratives, transforming daily commutes into encounters with the cultural and natural history of Tāmaki Makaurau (Auckland). Each station tells distinct stories through carefully chosen materials and symbols. Hidden within this metal forest is a kauri carving of Horotiu, a serpent-like guardian spirit. At Maungawhau Station, Tessa Harris designed a dramatic waterwall combining basalt, concrete, and glass to tell the story of freshwater and volcanic deities, with 53 glass triangles mapping Auckland's volcanic cones in colors that evoke molten lava. What makes this project quietly remarkable is its commitment to making infrastructure meaningful. Rather than treating public transit stations as purely functional spaces, Auckland has created places where thousands of daily travelers can connect with ancestral stories, geological time, and the living landscape beneath their feet—a reminder that even underground, we move through layers of history and belonging.

Why we have warm blood and what advantages it has over cold blood

Por qué tenemos sangre caliente y qué ventajas tiene sobre la sangre fría

Why do humans maintain a steady body temperature of around 37 degrees Celsius, whether shivering in Antarctica or sweating in the Sahara? This question leads to a fascinating exploration of warm-blooded versus cold-blooded animals, and the evolutionary journey that separates them. The terms themselves—"cold-blooded" and "warm-blooded"—are somewhat misleading. Cold-blooded animals, or ectotherms, regulate their temperature externally, relying on their environment. Warm-blooded creatures, or endotherms, generate heat internally. A desert lizard basking in the sun can actually be warmer than many mammals, making the labels relative rather than absolute. The language of "cold blood" has curious roots in ancient Greek medicine, particularly the Theory of Humors proposed by Hipocrates and Galen. They believed bodily fluids controlled temperament, and that blood literally heated up during strong emotions—hence expressions like "blood boiling." Writers in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries adopted this medical metaphor to describe human behavior, coining phrases like acting "in cold blood" long before scientists formally classified reptiles and amphibians as cold-blooded in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. All animals originally descended from ectothermic fish, but some evolved fur or feathers that enabled them to retain body heat, giving rise to endotherms—still a minority among Earth's creatures. This story offers a window into how evolution shaped our biology and how ancient medical theories continue to color our everyday language. It's a quiet reminder that understanding our own bodies connects us to both natural history and cultural heritage, revealing the remarkable systems that keep us functioning optimally at a precise temperature.

The push for a sustainable artisanal mining in the Peruvian Amazon

The slow, muddy work of saving ancient bogs

On the Isle of Man, a small team is undertaking the painstaking work of restoring ancient peatlands that have been damaged over centuries. Sarah Hickey, the island's Peatland Officer, leads efforts to repair bogs across 8,000 hectares of upland terrain, armed with metal probes, diggers, and an intimate knowledge of how these ecosystems function. Peat—partially decomposed vegetation accumulated over millennia in waterlogged conditions—stores more carbon than any other soil type on Earth. But the island's peatlands bear scars from generations past. By the 17th century, deforestation had forced islanders to harvest peat itself as fuel, cutting it into blocks for cooking and heating. Though the practice was banned in 2020, the exposed cuts continue to erode, washing sediment into reservoirs and releasing stored carbon. Restoration involves using lightweight diggers to reshape slopes, covering bare peat with vegetation, and installing bio-logs and netting to create conditions where new growth can establish. The true hero of bog recovery, Hickey explains, is sphagnum moss—a plant that doesn't just inhabit peatlands but actively creates them by holding vast amounts of water, slowing runoff, filtering sediment, and making the landscape more fire-resistant. This story is worth attention because it reveals how patient, unglamorous work can yield swift rewards. After creating new pools at one site, Hickey's team watched dragonflies arrive almost immediately to lay eggs. It's a quiet reminder that ecological repair, though measured in decades for full recovery, can spark visible life nearly overnight—and that some of our most valuable landscapes require defenders willing to get muddy.

The vanishing pharmacy of the Sundarbans

In the tidal mangroves of the Sundarbans, traditional healer Suranjan Raptan navigates mudbanks and twisted roots in search of medicinal plants his grandfather taught him to recognize. For twenty-one years, the kabiraj has practiced knowledge passed down through generations—never written, only shown and remembered. He walks barefoot through the forest, identifying leaves for stomach pain and bark for persistent coughs, carrying forward a pharmacy built entirely from the mangrove ecosystem that gave his community both its name and its remedies. But the forest Suranjan knew is thinning. Cyclone Aila in 2009 marked a turning point; since then, sunlight has begun piercing canopies that were once impenetrably dense. By 2015, familiar medicinal plants were becoming harder to locate, and the rare Sundari tree—the forest's namesake—now appears only occasionally. Rising salinity and increasingly frequent storms are reshaping the ecosystem, and with it, the availability of the natural ingredients traditional healers depend upon. At the same time, Bangladesh's expanded community clinic program has offered villagers new healthcare options, and the crowds that once filled Suranjan's yard have dwindled to a handful of patients. This quiet story captures a double vanishing: both an ecological transformation and the slow fading of generations-old medical knowledge. It's a reminder that climate change doesn't only alter landscapes—it erodes cultural practices rooted in those places, and the living libraries of knowledge held by people like Suranjan, who still rise before dawn to read the forest like an ancient text.
