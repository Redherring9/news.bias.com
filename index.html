Nickel expansion puts Indonesia’s biodiversity goals under pressure, study finds

Indonesia finds itself at a crossroads between two global imperatives: supplying nickel for the green energy transition and protecting some of Earth's most biodiverse ecosystems. A new study published in Nature Ecology & Evolution reveals that the country's nickel production has grown roughly tenfold over the past decade, positioning Indonesia to potentially supply nearly three-quarters of the world's nickel by 2040. This metal is essential not only for electric vehicle batteries but also for stainless steel, renewable energy infrastructure, and everyday items from kitchen cookware to construction materials. The environmental stakes are significant. Researchers found that nearly half of projected global nickel demand through 2050 could be met by mines located in areas ranked among the world's top 10% for biodiversity and carbon conservation. For Indonesia, this creates a direct tension with its ambitious conservation commitments, including a pledge to protect 30% of its territory by 2030 and a goal to make its forestry and land-use sector carbon-neutral by the same year. Much of Indonesia's nickel comes from laterite deposits formed in tropical regions, where mining requires extensive land clearing that can fragment critical wildlife habitat across Sulawesi and the Maluku islands. This story matters because it illustrates a paradox at the heart of the clean energy transition: the materials needed to build a sustainable future may come at a steep cost to the natural systems we're trying to protect. Indonesia's challenge is emblematic of a global dilemma, reminding us that environmental solutions often require difficult choices rather than easy answers.

Solar eclipse and the island of Ireland: The weather forecast and all you need to know

Skywatchers across Ireland are preparing for a rare celestial treat: a deep partial solar eclipse on Wednesday that will obscure more than 90% of the sun—the most dramatic eclipse visible from the island until 2090. The timing is remarkable, as the eclipse will be followed just hours later by the peak of the Perseid meteor shower, creating what one astronomer describes as "celestial fireworks." For those traveling to Spain, a total eclipse lasting nearly two minutes awaits. The eclipse will begin around 6:10 PM BST, with coverage varying by location. Belfast will see 93% of the sun covered, while Skellig Michael off the Kerry coast will experience nearly complete obscuration at 98%. Astronomers emphasize that even with such extensive coverage, the remaining sunlight remains dangerously bright—looking directly at it can damage the retina without any pain warning. Viewers are urged to use certified eclipse glasses or creative alternatives like pinhole viewers or even kitchen colanders to safely project the sun's image. The weather forecast offers mixed prospects, with cloud cover likely in parts of Northern Ireland while southern and eastern counties may enjoy clearer skies. What makes this event quietly extraordinary is its rarity and the convergence of cosmic events in a single evening. For researchers like Ryan Milligan, who has traveled to Spain for his thirteenth eclipse, the experience transcends data and screens—it's described as visceral and emotional, a moment when day turns to night and the familiar world briefly transforms. For those staying home, even a partial eclipse offers a reminder of our place in a dynamic solar system, a spectacle that won't return in this form for seven decades.

Rare piglet the size of 'a grapefruit with legs' born at zoo

A tiny babirusa piglet named Rana has made her public debut at Chester Zoo, offering a glimmer of hope for a species teetering on the brink of extinction. When she was born, keepers described her as roughly the size of a grapefruit with legs—a fitting image for one of nature's more unusual creatures. Babirusas, known as "pig-deer" in Malay due to their distinctive appearance, are found on only four Indonesian islands and face catastrophic decline in the wild, primarily due to African swine fever that reached their habitat five years ago. Unlike most pigs, babirusas typically give birth to just one or two offspring at a time rather than large litters, making population recovery painfully slow. New mothers are also highly sensitive to disturbance, so keepers monitored Rana and her mother Matano remotely until they were ready for visitors. Matano herself was born at Chester Zoo eleven years ago and now has descendants across Europe, representing an important genetic safety net. The species, long classified as vulnerable, is being upgraded to critically endangered—a sobering reflection of how quickly their situation has deteriorated. Beyond their quirky looks—males sport upper tusks that curve dramatically back toward their faces—babirusas play a crucial ecological role. As they forage, they disperse seeds that aid forest regeneration, making them vital to the health of island ecosystems. Conservationists describe Rana as a lifeline for her species and emphasize that her birth represents a race against time to prevent the loss of one of the oldest pig species still living. It's a quiet reminder that even the most charismatic and culturally significant creatures can vanish if we're not paying attention.

Hiroshi Okuda, Toyota chair who pushed for the Prius, dies at 93

Hiroshi Okuda, the visionary leader who guided Toyota Motor Corporation through a transformative period in the late 1990s and early 2000s, has passed away at 93. His tenure marked a significant shift for the Japanese automaker, as he became the first president from outside the founding family in a generation when he assumed the role in 1995. Okuda's leadership style set him apart in Japan's often consensus-driven corporate culture. Tall, outspoken, and willing to take calculated risks, he pushed Toyota to embrace innovation at a time when the automotive industry stood on the cusp of major change. Under his guidance, the company launched the Prius, the pioneering gas-electric hybrid that would reshape global attitudes toward fuel efficiency and environmental responsibility in transportation. He also championed the RAV4 SUV, which became another commercial success. Beyond product development, Okuda earned recognition internally for his cost-cutting initiatives and decisive leadership, which he saw as essential antidotes to the bureaucratic inertia that can slow large corporations. Okuda's career spanned the arc of Toyota's rise from a post-World War II manufacturer to a global automotive giant. His willingness to bet on unconventional technology like hybrid powertrains—when such vehicles were unproven in the marketplace—demonstrates a forward-thinking approach that helped position Toyota as an industry leader in sustainable transportation. This story reminds us how individual leadership and the courage to pursue untested ideas can shape entire industries and influence how millions of people move through the world.

UK to get best eclipse since 1999 as Moon blocks out Sun across Europe

On Wednesday evening, the UK experienced its most significant solar eclipse since 1999—and the best the country will see for another half-century. Up to 96% of the Sun was covered by the Moon, with the peak occurring between 18:58 and 19:15 BST. Devon, Cornwall, and parts of Wales enjoyed the fullest views, while much of the country saw coverage exceeding 90%. Demand for eclipse glasses surged, with one London bakery selling around 100 pairs in a single morning. Meanwhile, northern Spain, along with parts of Russia, Greenland, Iceland, and Portugal, witnessed a total eclipse. An estimated half a million visitors traveled to Spain for the event, gathering in cities like Bilbao, Valencia, and Palma to experience the minute and forty seconds of totality—when the Moon completely obscures the Sun and daylight briefly fades. Observers described the phenomenon as a chance to witness "the grandeur of nature," with families investing in the experience rather than "trivial things." Weather conditions were largely favorable across Spain, though some parts of Scotland and Northern Ireland faced cloudy skies. Solar eclipses occur roughly every 18 months somewhere on Earth, but being in the path of totality—where the Moon's shadow falls—is rare for any given location. The UK won't see this level of coverage again until 2081, and a total eclipse won't grace British skies until 2090. For those fortunate enough to witness it, the event served as a humbling reminder of the celestial mechanics quietly unfolding above us, connecting strangers across continents in shared wonder.

Lebanon parliament votes to end death penalty, 1st in Middle East to do so

Lebanon has become the first country in the Middle East to formally abolish the death penalty, with parliament voting to replace capital punishment with life imprisonment and aggravated hard labor. The decision transforms what had been an unofficial moratorium on executions—in place since 2004—into permanent legal protection. At the time of the vote, 85 people were facing death sentences in Lebanon. The legislation passed with majority support in the 128-member assembly, though Hezbollah's parliamentary bloc opposed the measure. Justice Minister Adel Nassar called it a "historic step," and human rights organizations praised the move as a significant milestone for the region. Amnesty International noted that Lebanon is now aligned with a global trend toward abolition, turning a temporary pause in executions into lasting human rights progress. However, practical questions remain about how the new punishment of aggravated hard labor will actually be enforced, given Lebanon's resource constraints and overcrowded prisons. The timing is complex: parliament is simultaneously debating a broad amnesty law that could reduce sentences or release individuals convicted of violent offenses—the largest such amnesty since Lebanon's civil war ended in 1990. This has drawn protests from families of victims, some of whom had hoped to see perpetrators executed rather than granted reduced sentences. The story offers a window into how societies navigate competing values around justice, mercy, and human rights, even amid practical challenges and deep disagreements about what accountability should look like.

Lunar calendar: Mayan book predicted eclipses a thousand years ago

Calendário lunar: livro maia previu eclipses há mil anos

A thousand years ago, Maya astronomers created something that continues to astonish modern scientists: a lunar calendar so precise it could predict solar eclipses across centuries. The Dresden Codex, one of the oldest surviving books in the Americas, contains an intricate system that tracked the moon's cycles and anticipated eclipses with remarkable accuracy—all without telescopes, computers, or modern physics. What makes this achievement particularly impressive is how the Maya solved a problem that plagues all lunar calendars: the moon's cycles don't align perfectly with solar years, causing errors to accumulate over time. The Maya developed what researchers now recognize as a kind of ancestral "algorithmic thinking," creating a system that combined different lunar cycles and included periodic corrections to keep the calendar synchronized. Through patient observation spanning generations and ingenious calculations, they built a tool that could forecast virtually every solar eclipse visible from Maya territories between 350 and 1150 CE, with an error margin of only about one hour. This story offers a quiet reminder of the sophistication of ancient scientific thinking. The Dresden Codex reveals a culture deeply invested in understanding celestial patterns, not through sudden flashes of genius, but through methodical observation, careful record-keeping, and mathematical innovation passed down across generations. It's a testament to what human curiosity and persistence can achieve, even without the technology we now take for granted.

Perseid meteor shower reaches peak: When and where to catch a glimpse

The Perseids meteor shower, widely considered the best annual celestial display, reaches its peak this week, offering skywatchers a chance to see up to 100 shooting stars per hour. This year brings an unusual bonus: a deep partial solar eclipse visible in the UK will occur just hours before the meteor shower begins, creating what astronomer Dr. Becky Smethurst calls "the perfect cosmic coincidence of events." The Perseids are caused by debris from the Swift-Tuttle comet, a massive object with a 16-mile solid core that takes 133 years to orbit the Sun, meaning some of the burning fragments may have been left behind more than a century ago. While the Perseids are active from mid-July through late August, the prime viewing window falls in the early morning hours of August 12-13, between midnight and dawn. This year offers especially favorable conditions due to the new moon, which means no competing moonlight to wash out fainter meteors. For the best experience, observers should seek locations away from city lights, allow their eyes 20-30 minutes to adjust to darkness, and look toward the widest possible expanse of sky. England and Wales are expected to have clear skies, while Scotland and Northern Ireland may contend with more cloud cover. This story offers a gentle reminder to look up and reconnect with the cosmos. The Perseids provide a rare chance to witness ancient debris burning up in real time, connecting us to celestial events that unfold on timescales far beyond human lifespans—what Smethurst aptly describes as "the Universe's own cosmic clock."

Where in the UK will get the best view of the solar eclipse?

For the first time since 1999, skywatchers across the UK will witness an almost total solar eclipse on a Wednesday evening in August, with the moon blocking between 88% and 96% of the sun's light. The western regions of the UK will enjoy the most dramatic views, with Plymouth seeing 95% coverage, followed by Cardiff and Belfast at just over 93%. Experts urge using certified eclipse glasses that meet international safety standards, or indirect viewing methods like pinhole projectors.
