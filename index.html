Ant 'clones' males of another species to produce its own workers

Formiga ‘clona’ machos de outra espécie para conseguir produzir suas próprias operárias

Scientists have discovered a remarkable reproductive strategy in Iberian harvester ants (Messor ibericus) that challenges fundamental assumptions about species boundaries. Queens of this species don't just produce their own offspring—they also generate genetic clones of males from an entirely different species, Messor structor, which split from them evolutionarily over 5 million years ago. Researchers analyzing 390 individuals across five European Messor species found that all 164 worker ants examined were first-generation hybrids, with roughly equal genetic contributions from both species. The puzzle deepened when these hybrid workers were discovered in regions where M. structor doesn't even exist—some more than a thousand kilometers away from its known range. The answer lay within the colonies themselves: queens were producing two distinct types of males, one group genetically belonging to their own species and another that was pure M. structor, despite having no M. structor fathers in the environment. Through a process called androgenesis, the queens use their eggs to propagate the nuclear genome of M. structor males, essentially maintaining a captive clonal line within their own nests. This discovery, published in Nature, reveals what researchers describe as a form of "sexual domestication"—a species breeding and maintaining males of another species solely to produce the hybrid workers essential for colony function. It's a quietly astonishing example of how evolution can blur the lines we draw between species, showing nature's capacity for strategies far stranger than we might imagine.

Solar-Powered School Boat Inaugurated in Cubatão to Promote Environmental Education

Barco-Escola movido a energia solar é inaugurado em Cubatão para promover educação ambiental

In the coastal city of Cubatão, Brazil, a solar-powered educational boat has begun offering a quietly innovative approach to environmental learning. The Barco-Escola Neosão glides through local rivers on weekdays carrying students from public and private schools, and welcomes the general public on weekends and holidays. What makes this floating classroom particularly thoughtful is how it integrates technology with environmental sensitivity. Developed by the Meu Oceano Institute in partnership with the municipal government and Usiminas, the vessel features a bifacial solar panel that captures both direct sunlight and reflected light from the water's surface, providing up to five hours of autonomous operation. During the 90-minute journey along the Casqueiro and Santana rivers, educators share insights about the mangrove ecosystem, local biodiversity, and the city's history. The initiative makes environmental education accessible through a sliding fee scale, with reduced rates for residents, students, teachers, seniors, and people with disabilities, and free admission for children under three. Carbon credit project prevents deforestation and protects jaguars, anteaters and other Amazon animals

Projeto de créditos de carbono evita desmatamento e protege onças, tamanduás e outros animais da Amazônia

In the southern reaches of Roraima, Brazil, camera traps have captured striking images of jaguars, giant anteaters carrying their young, and tapirs roaming freely across 14,000 hectares of Amazon rainforest—land that could have legally been cleared for agriculture. Instead, the property owner chose a different path, partnering with a São Paulo-based company to keep the forest standing through carbon credit sales, transforming conservation into a viable income source. The initiative, called Vale do Rio Branco, seeks out Amazonian properties with legal deforestation rights and offers an alternative to clearing land for crops like soy. By preserving the forest, the project calculates how much greenhouse gas emission is avoided—each ton of carbon kept stored in trees generates one carbon credit that companies can purchase to offset their own emissions. Nine cameras scattered across the property monitor wildlife biodiversity, while a tower equipped with 360-degree cameras and artificial intelligence scans up to 30 kilometers for fire outbreaks, enabling rapid response to threats. The project has secured a 40-year authorization to sell credits, with an anticipated investment of 20 million reais. What makes this story quietly remarkable is its practical reimagining of conservation. Local residents, including Brazil nut collectors, remain part of the forest economy, harvesting and selling their products while the ecosystem stays intact. It's a model that doesn't pit livelihoods against nature but instead finds a way to value both—offering a glimpse of how financial mechanisms might support the Amazon and its creatures, from the smallest anteater clinging to its mother's back to the elusive jaguar padding through the understory.

Bhutan’s pursuit of happiness takes root on its farms

In the Himalayan kingdom of Bhutan, where Gross National Happiness guides national policy, an ambitious agricultural transformation is underway. The country has set a goal to achieve food self-sufficiency by 2029, a remarkable aspiration for a mountainous nation where only a small fraction of land is suitable for farming. Though agriculture employs more than 40 percent of Bhutan's 800,000 citizens, the path from farm to table is fraught with obstacles that test both crops and farmers alike. The challenges are distinctly Bhutanese: steep terrain makes transportation difficult, with every extra hour in transit diminishing the quality of fresh produce. Remote farms lack cold storage and proper roads. Meanwhile, young people are leaving agriculture behind, viewing it as a last resort rather than a viable profession. To address these issues, the government is investing $180 million, with most funds directed toward smallholder farmers—predominantly women. The initiatives include introducing smaller farm machinery that women can operate, a practical shift from the heavy equipment traditionally used only by men. Yet another complication arises from Bhutan's commitment to conservation: strict wildlife protection laws mean farmers face crop damage from animals, prompting a controversial fencing project spanning thousands of kilometers. This story offers a window into how a small nation attempts to balance tradition with modernization, conservation with cultivation, and happiness with practical survival. As chef Bleu Tshering Dorji observes, Bhutan's food system isn't merely about production—it's about sustaining landscapes, communities, and culture itself. It's a quiet reminder that food security, particularly in challenging terrain, requires more than agricultural policy; it demands reimagining the relationship between people, land, and the wild creatures they share it with.

Backyard astronomer contributes to world-first Saturn discovery

In the backyard of his homemade observatory in Broken Hill, outback New South Wales, former miner Trevor Barry has made a contribution to planetary science that has earned him co-authorship in a peer-reviewed journal. Since the early 2000s, Barry has been capturing detailed observations of Saturn from his small-town setup, and in mid-2024, he noticed something unusual: a ripple of bright spots forming around the planet's south pole. He sent his findings to a professor in Spain with whom he'd been collaborating for years, setting in motion the announcement of a world-first discovery. The feature turned out to be a decagon—a ten-sided atmospheric wave spinning around Saturn's southern hemisphere. It's the first large jet pattern ever observed in that region and echoes the famous hexagonal storm that has swirled around Saturn's north pole since the 1980s. But while the hexagon has been studied for decades, this southern decagon is brand new, and much about it remains unknown: how it formed, whether it's permanent, and what it might reveal about Saturn's complex atmosphere. Barry's meticulous data—captured during moments of exceptionally stable air—proved crucial in identifying and confirming the structure, filling gaps that even NASA's Hubble Space Telescope couldn't cover. This story is a quiet reminder that major scientific discoveries don't always require billion-dollar missions. Sometimes they happen in a backyard, driven by patience, precision, and a deep curiosity about the night sky. Barry's work shows how amateur astronomers continue to play a vital role in planetary science, and that a town like Broken Hill can now claim a place in the record books of the solar system.

The successes and failures of 3 maps we use today (and why the one now approved by the UN also doesn't correctly represent the world)

Los aciertos y errores de 3 mapas que usamos hoy (y por qué el aprobado ahora por la ONU tampoco representa correctamente al mundo)

The United Nations has called for replacing the widely used Mercator map with a new projection that more accurately represents the true size of continents like Africa. The resolution, titled "Correct the Map," addresses how the Mercator projection makes Africa appear similar in size to Greenland, though Africa is actually about 14 times larger. The UN has adopted the Equal Earth projection, created by cartographers in 2018, which better reflects the actual sizes of landmasses. The Mercator map, created in 1569 by Flemish cartographer Gerardus Mercator, was designed to help European sailors navigate the oceans by ensuring that straight lines on the map corresponded to straight compass bearings in reality. To achieve this on a flat surface, Mercator had to stretch the grid lines as they moved away from the equator toward the poles, making countries near the poles appear much larger than they actually are. While perfectly suited for navigation, critics argue this distortion has psychological effects, making some regions seem less important than they truly are. Kenyan geographer Kioko Muendo notes this has implicitly diminished perceptions of Africa's vast resources, population, and investment opportunities. This story offers a fascinating glimpse into how the tools we use to understand our world carry hidden assumptions and trade-offs. No map can perfectly represent a sphere on a flat surface—each must sacrifice either accurate area, shape, distance, or direction. The conversation about which map to use is ultimately about which truths we prioritize and how our tools shape our perceptions of global importance and interconnection.
