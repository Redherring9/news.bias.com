Birdwatch: the paradoxical puffin – a comical bird with a fighting spirit

The Atlantic puffin is a study in contradictions: adored across Britain yet rarely encountered, comically charming yet surprisingly scrappy. These small seabirds spend spring and summer in bustling coastal colonies before disappearing into the open North Atlantic for half the year. Despite their clownish appearance and diminutive size, puffins fiercely defend their catches of sand eels from much larger predators like gulls and skuas, revealing a tenacity that belies their gentle image. Their appeal is partly rooted in their oddly human quality. Naturalist Bill Oddie once compared them to "old men in dinner jackets, waiting to go into supper at the club," and their scientific name translates to "little brother of the Arctic." Historically, their oversized, vividly colored beaks earned them the nickname "sea parrot," while the name "puffin" itself originally belonged to another seabird entirely—the Manx shearwater, which confusingly still carries the scientific name *Puffinus puffinus*. Yet these beloved birds now face mounting threats. Climate change is pushing sand eels northward, disrupting the food supply puffins depend on to raise their single chick each season. Avian flu, pollution, and human disturbance add further pressure, with well-meaning visitors sometimes causing harm in their eagerness to photograph the birds up close. This story matters because it highlights how even our most cherished wildlife faces precarious futures, and how the traits that endear animals to us—accessibility, charisma—can ironically contribute to their vulnerability.

Israel returns 22 stolen ancient artefacts to Greece

Israel has returned 22 ancient artefacts to Greece in what marks the first repatriation of cultural objects between the two nations. The collection, which includes 15 Attic vases, six Corinthian vases, and a bronze female statuette dating from 700 BC to 320 BC, was located by Israel's Antiquities Authority Theft Prevention Unit. The handover ceremony took place at Athens' National Archaeological Museum, though officials from both countries did not disclose how the items were discovered in Israel or the circumstances of their original theft from Greece. Greek Culture Minister Lina Mendoni emphasized the significance of the gesture, describing it as "the starting point of closer cooperation between the two countries for the protection of cultural heritage." She noted that returning cultural objects to their place of origin carries profound symbolic and moral weight beyond the physical items themselves. Israel's ambassador to Greece, Pninat Yanay, framed the repatriation as strengthening bilateral ties between the nations. This cultural exchange reflects broader warming relations between Greece and Israel, which have expanded cooperation in energy and defense sectors in recent years. The story offers a quiet reminder of how cultural heritage can serve as a bridge between nations, and how the journey of ancient objects—stolen, recovered, and finally returned—reflects both our failures to protect history and our capacity to make amends. For anyone interested in how countries navigate the complex terrain of repatriation, this marks a meaningful precedent in Mediterranean cooperation.

Students risk 'cognitive outsourcing' if AI overused, schools warned

Australia's national education research body has issued a caution about artificial intelligence in classrooms, warning that overreliance on the technology could undermine students' ability to think independently. The Australian Education Research Organisation reviewed studies showing that students who lean too heavily on AI struggle to perform when the technology isn't available—a phenomenon researchers call "cognitive outsourcing." When learners depend on tools to do their thinking, they may never build the foundational knowledge needed to become creative, critical thinkers later in life. The organisation recommends avoiding AI entirely in the first three years of primary school and using it sparingly afterward, always in combination with proven teaching methods. By high school, some exposure becomes important since students will encounter AI in higher education and beyond. However, the guidance emphasizes that AI should guide reasoning rather than simply provide answers. Some Australian states have developed customized versions of tools like ChatGPT that require students to engage more actively with problems rather than outsourcing their homework entirely. Parents are urged to limit unfettered access to AI at home as well. This story matters because it addresses a tension at the heart of modern education: how to prepare students for a technology-saturated future without sacrificing the deep learning that builds genuine understanding. The research suggests that the path forward isn't rejecting AI outright, but using it thoughtfully—ensuring that digital tools remain servants of learning rather than substitutes for it. It's a reminder that even as technology evolves, the fundamentals of how humans learn remain remarkably constant.

Inside the ‘unique’ tail of Iran’s spider-tailed horned viper

Deep in the Zagros Mountains of western Iran lives a snake with one of nature's most elaborate deceptions. The spider-tailed horned viper remains motionless among the rocks, indistinguishable from its surroundings—except for its tail, which it twitches like bait. That tail, complete with bulbous tip and leg-like scales, looks uncannily like a plump spider, luring hungry birds and mammals into striking range. Scientists have known about this remarkable snake since 2006, but only recently have they understood what makes the lure work. Using high-resolution 3D scans of a specimen collected in 1968, researchers discovered that the spider mimicry comes from enlarged, elongated scales unlike anything else in the living reptile world. The structure loosely resembles appendages found in ancient creatures from the Triassic and Jurassic periods—not because they're related, but likely because evolution independently arrived at similar solutions to similar problems. Herpetologists studying related viper species have observed simpler tail modifications in the wild, suggesting a gradual evolutionary pathway: first a change in color or movement, then a few thread-like scales, eventually building toward the spider-tail's stunning complexity. This story matters because it reveals how adaptable reptilian skin and scales can be, with evolutionary potential researchers are only beginning to appreciate. The spider-tailed viper represents what one scientist calls "the peak of morphological complexity" in tail-luring behavior among vipers. It's a quiet reminder that even in well-studied animal groups, nature still holds surprises—and that patient observation of rare creatures can reshape our understanding of what's possible in the living world.

A study unravels the genetic mystery of a cobra divided by sea

A new genetic study has reopened a decades-old question about whether Sri Lanka's cobras are truly distinct from their mainland cousins. Researchers analyzing 67 spectacled cobras using nine genetic markers found that the island's population forms a genetically unique lineage that likely diverged from Indian cobras around two million years ago—possibly when changing sea levels separated Sri Lanka from the subcontinent. The cobras themselves are thought to have descended from African ancestors that migrated through the Middle East. The debate has deep roots. In 1939, Sri Lankan zoologist P. E. P. Deraniyagala noticed distinctive eye-like markings on the island's cobras and gave them a special designation. For decades, this observation faded into taxonomic footnotes, with the forms being lumped together. Then in 2022, researchers proposed recognizing Sri Lankan cobras as a separate species based on genetic differences. But that claim was challenged in 2023 when other scientists argued the evidence was insufficient and revealed that the original cobra specimen used to define the species actually came from Sri Lanka, complicating the naming conventions. This latest research confirms the genetic distinctiveness without rushing to declare a new species, leaving that formal decision to taxonomists who will need to weigh whether the differences warrant species-level recognition. Beyond the scientific intrigue, the cobra holds special meaning in Sri Lanka, woven deeply into the island's folklore, traditions, and cultural identity—making this not just a question of genetics, but of heritage. The story reminds us that understanding where species boundaries lie can be as complex and layered as the evolutionary histories that shaped them.

Baseboard, Chair Rail, and Crown Molding: Do You Know the Difference?

Rodapé, rodameio e rodateto: você sabe a diferença?

In Brazilian Portuguese, there are three distinct terms for architectural trim that runs along different parts of a wall, each serving both practical and aesthetic purposes. The story explores rodapé, rodameio, and rodateto—elements of interior design that often go unnoticed yet play crucial roles in protecting and defining living spaces. Rodapé, the baseboard, protects walls from brooms, furniture, and vacuum cleaners while concealing the gap between floor and wall. Its height can dramatically alter a room's character: lower profiles around 7 cm offer a classic, understated look, while taller baseboards of 10 cm or more suit rooms with high ceilings. Rodameio, a mid-wall trim installed at chair-back height, guards walls from scratches and scuffs in dining rooms and hallways. Rodateto, or crown molding, bridges the junction between wall and ceiling, hiding imperfections while adding architectural definition. This piece offers a gentle reminder of how thoughtful details shape our everyday environments. The materials matter too—polystyrene has largely replaced traditional wood for these trims due to its light weight, easy installation, and moisture resistance. What makes this story quietly worthwhile is its focus on the overlooked: those finishing touches that create visual harmony without demanding attention, yet whose absence would be immediately felt. It's a celebration of the modest craft that transforms construction into comfort.

Scientists deploy a 24-armed secret weapon to save California’s kelp forests

Off the California coast, scientists are deploying an unusual ally in the fight to restore devastated kelp forests: the sunflower sea star, a critically endangered predator with up to 24 arms that looks like something from another world. Divers are carefully releasing these alien-like creatures into specialized cages on the ocean floor, hoping they'll venture out and reclaim their role as ecosystem architects. The sunflower stars were once abundant—6 billion strong from Alaska to Baja California—but a marine heatwave beginning in 2013 triggered a devastating disease that dissolved their bodies, wiping out more than 90% of the Pacific population. With the stars gone, the underwater world shifted dramatically. Purple sea urchins, no longer kept in check by their primary predator, exploded in number and devoured the kelp forests from below. Over the past decade, 95% of California's kelp forests—towering underwater sanctuaries that grow as tall as ten-story buildings and sequester roughly 200 million tons of carbon dioxide annually—have disappeared, transforming lush marine habitats into barren deserts. Now researchers are working to restore what they call the "halo of fear," the 16-foot radius around each sunflower star where urchins hide and eat less, simply from detecting the predator's scent. A single star can consume three to four urchins daily, but its true power lies in deterrence. This story offers a glimpse into the intricate connections that hold ecosystems together and the creative, determined efforts to mend them when those threads break. It's a reminder that sometimes saving a forest means first saving a star.

Photos: Taiwan’s tea revival blends ancient terraces with new climate adaptations

In the misty hills just outside Taipei, a quiet agricultural revival is underway. Taiwan's Shenkeng region, once a powerhouse of tea production that accounted for a significant portion of the island's export earnings in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, saw its terraced slopes return to jungle after industrialization drew workers away in the 1970s. Now, researchers and local growers are working to restore both the tea industry and the cultural heritage woven into these historic stone terraces—but they're doing so under increasingly unpredictable conditions. Climate change is reshaping the very environment that once made Shenkeng ideal for tea cultivation. The morning mists that used to keep the slopes damp now dissipate earlier, stressing the shallow-rooted tea plants. Rainfall arrives in intense bursts rather than gentle drizzles, and typhoons have grown notably more severe, with category 4 and 5 storms becoming two to three times more common this century. A single typhoon can devastate an entire plantation, creating landslides on the loosely terraced hillsides. The challenge has prompted a creative response: blending traditional farming wisdom with modern technology like drip irrigation, soil sensors, and more resilient tea varieties. This story offers a window into how communities preserve cultural practices in a changing world. It's not simply about nostalgia or economic revival—it's about adapting cherished traditions to new realities while honoring the landscape and knowledge passed down through generations. Sampling wizards The Avalanches mine magic from old ads and obsolete tech

The Avalanches, the Australian sampling pioneers who built their reputation on turning forgotten vinyl into sonic gold, have returned with a new kind of archaeology. It's a project that transforms the detritus of consumer technology into something surprisingly emotional, tapping into the peculiar power of these sounds to transport listeners back in time. Now a trio after years of lineup changes, the group has shifted from crate-digging in op-shops to spelunking through YouTube archives, hunting for obsolete media that carries unexpected emotional weight. The 31-track album weaves together contributions from an eclectic roster—Jamie xx, Karen O, Japanese legend Haruomi Hosono, and rising stars like Tunisia's Khadija Al Hanafi—alongside samples from 1970s children's television and Nokia ringtones. The album's very title comes from a 1980s floppy disk advertisement, a phrase that began as a joke about data storage but evolved into something more resonant. What makes this work compelling is its ambivalence. The Avalanches aren't simply celebrating or condemning the way capitalism packages emotion and sells it back to us—they're sitting with the contradiction. These jingles and tech sounds are manipulative marketing tools, yes, but they're also genuinely beautiful, saturated with color and craft, and undeniably lodged in our collective memory. It's a bittersweet meditation on how the sounds we didn't choose to absorb have nonetheless become part of who we are.

Auckland Council using discounts to separate trash and treasure at transfer station

Auckland Council has introduced a simple but effective program at the Waitakere Resource Recovery Park: offer people a discount for sorting their own waste. What began as a 10 percent price reduction last year has grown to 15 percent this July, and the response has been warmly positive. Customers aren't just motivated by saving money—many have told staff they'd be happy to sort without any discount at all, simply because it feels good to contribute to something meaningful. The initiative addresses a practical challenge: Auckland has set an ambitious zero-waste goal for 2040, but improving waste diversion without hiring more staff requires creative thinking. By redesigning the unloading area to include clearly marked bins for wood, steel, cardboard, plastics, and hazardous materials, the council has made it easier for residents to participate. Reusable items are sold in an on-site recycle shop run by a local community trust, giving discarded goods a second life. Council refuse manager Dick Fong notes that the program consistently diverts about two-thirds of incoming materials from landfill—a significant achievement. This story is worth attention because it quietly demonstrates how behavioral change doesn't always require heavy-handed regulation or expensive infrastructure. Sometimes a modest financial incentive, combined with clear systems and a sense of shared purpose, is enough to turn a mundane chore into an act of environmental stewardship. It's a reminder that people often want to do the right thing—they just need an accessible way to do it.

Hiroo Kanamori, the Japanese scientist who created the scale that replaced Richter's (and why it's better for measuring large earthquakes)

Hiroo Kanamori, el científico japonés que creó la escala que reemplazó a la de Richter (y por qué es mejor para medir terremotos de gran magnitud)

When Hiroo Kanamori was a child in wartime Japan, he saw snow-covered mountains in Nagano for the first time and wondered how such natural grandeur came to be. Growing up during World War II without school supplies or formal classes, he learned to entertain himself by building small motors from salvaged materials—a practice that taught him to think independently. That curiosity about the natural world eventually led him to geophysics and to one of seismology's most important innovations: the moment magnitude scale, which now measures earthquakes worldwide. Kanamori developed his scale in 1977, later expanding it in collaboration with American seismologist Thomas Hanks. It replaced the famous Richter scale, particularly for larger earthquakes, though Richter's method remains in use for smaller tremors. The challenge scientists faced for decades was distilling the complexity of an earthquake into a single, quickly calculable number. Before Richter's 1935 breakthrough, researchers relied on "intensity" measurements—how strongly a quake was felt in different locations—which generated many numbers for a single event. Richter focused on the maximum amplitude of seismic waves recorded by identical instruments in Southern California, creating the first unified magnitude system. Now 90 and professor emeritus at Caltech, Kanamori has spent over half a century advancing our understanding of earthquakes. His story reminds us that scientific breakthroughs often begin with childhood wonder and the habit of asking questions. The tools he developed help communities around the world understand and prepare for seismic events, turning a boy's curiosity about mountains into knowledge that saves lives.

High silver prices threaten Lucknow’s famed bridal nagra craft

In Lucknow's historic Chowk Bazaar, a centuries-old craft is quietly fading. Azhar Hussain has spent three decades creating silver nagras—ornate slip-on shoes decorated with hand-engraved floral motifs. The process is painstaking: he sketches designs onto thin silver sheets, hammers them into heated resin, shapes them over wooden moulds, and assembles them with velvet and cardboard bases. This technique has remained unchanged since the 1600s, when these shimmering shoes were made exclusively for Nawabs, the Muslim rulers of Awadh, symbolizing wealth and status. Over the past century, silver nagras evolved from royal footwear into a beloved wedding tradition across Lucknow's social classes. Brides, in particular, adopted them as an essential part of wedding attire. Hussain's family has crafted nagras since the early 1900s, and the trade once provided a comfortable living—he typically made about ten pairs monthly, priced between $105 and $230, enough to support his family with dignity. But over the past year, orders have disappeared. Silver prices, already climbing due to tight supplies and investor demand, surged further following the U.S.-Iran conflict in February as global uncertainty drove investors toward precious metals. Prices that fluctuated around $830 per kilogram in 2023 have now soared beyond reach for many Lucknow shoppers. This story offers a window into how global events ripple through the smallest workshops, threatening crafts that have survived centuries. It's a reminder that heritage isn't just preserved in museums—it lives in the hands of artisans whose livelihoods depend on customers who can still afford beauty.

These women are bringing Darwin’s rheas back to Chilean Patagonia

In the windswept expanses of Chile's Patagonia National Park, a team of women rangers is working to restore a missing piece of the ecosystem: Darwin's rheas, large flightless birds that once roamed freely across the steppe. For over a century, sheep and cattle ranching dominated the Chacabuco Valley, fragmenting the landscape with fencing and causing rhea populations to plummet to as few as 11 to 15 individuals. Now, through one of South America's most ambitious rewilding projects, these distinctive birds are making a comeback. The rangers, working with Rewilding Chile Foundation, spend nearly a year preparing each group of rheas for release. The birds undergo two months of acclimatization while the team manages threats from pumas, monitors body condition, and removes the thousands of kilometers of fencing that once carved up their habitat. The work is painstaking and around-the-clock, requiring vigilance against predators, illegal grazing, and the region's famously harsh weather. The rheas themselves play a vital ecological role as "gardeners of the steppe," dispersing seeds across the landscape and helping to restore native plant communities alongside guanacos and other wildlife. This story offers a quiet glimpse into conservation work that unfolds across years, not headlines—a reminder that healing landscapes requires patience, dedication, and people willing to face Patagonian winds for the chance to see an ecosystem become whole again. It's a testament to what's possible when former ranchlands are given back to the wild, and to the women whose daily care is making that transformation real.

Massacred in 1940 because they were Black, two Senegalese riflemen finally honored in Yonne

Massacrés en 1940 parce que noirs, deux tirailleurs sénégalais enfin honorés dans l’Yonne

In the small village of Gigny in France's Yonne department, a community of just 92 residents is preparing to honor two Senegalese riflemen executed by German forces 86 years ago. Yali Fal and Souleyemane Ly, soldiers serving in France's colonial infantry, were shot on June 18, 1940—the eve of the armistice and General de Gaulle's famous appeal. A commemorative plaque will mark the spots where they fell, and bullet holes in a farmhouse wall still bear witness to the violence that claimed Fal's life. The story of their deaths has been preserved through local memory and a handwritten note left by refugees from Châlons-sur-Marne who witnessed the executions. When most of Gigny's residents fled westward during the exodus, these witnesses saw German soldiers separate the captured colonial troops, pull Fal from the ranks during a moment of weakness, and execute him against a wall. Two days later, returning villagers discovered Ly's body and buried both men themselves. Historians note that these killings were part of a broader pattern of racist violence by the Wehrmacht, which systematically separated Black soldiers from white ones and executed them in violation of the rules of war—hatred rooted in resentment dating back to World War I. This quiet commemoration matters because it brings long-overlooked violence into the light and acknowledges the sacrifices of African soldiers who fought for France. In a village where memory has been carefully tended across generations, the ceremony represents both a reckoning with a painful past and recognition of contributions that have too often gone unremembered.

Country diary: The house martins are gone, but not forgotten | Ed Douglas

A nature observer in England reflects on the sudden disappearance of house martins from the skies above Kinder Scout, capturing what the Japanese call mono no aware—the poignant awareness of impermanence. Just a week earlier, the same valley had been alive with dozens of these small birds swooping and feeding on insects in preparation for their long migration south. Now the skies are empty, leaving only a hunting kestrel and questions about where these travelers have gone. House martins, now on the red list of threatened species due to declining numbers, undertake remarkable journeys to sub-Saharan Africa each winter. Despite modern research efforts, including isotope analysis of feathers by Dutch scientists, their exact wintering locations remain largely mysterious, narrowed down only to somewhere in west Africa. The discovery of microplastics trapped in their feathers adds a sobering dimension to their story, revealing how human impact reaches even into the atmospheric layers where these birds hunt insects. This quiet meditation on seasonal absence offers something deeper than a simple nature note. Champion golfer reflects on lasting legacy in outback WA

In the red earth of outback Western Australia, a world-class golf course stands as an unlikely testament to ambition and perseverance. Graham Marsh, an Australian golfing champion with 70 tournament wins and 65 golf course design projects to his name, recently returned to his birthplace of Kalgoorlie for the WA PGA Championship. At 82, he reflected on creating what he considers Australia's first true desert-style golf course—a project that opened in 2010 after decades of planning and an $18.5 million investment. The course has faced considerable headwinds since its creation. Built on the site of a former rubbish tip in a city dependent on a 560-kilometer pipeline for water, it has drawn criticism for its cost burden on ratepayers. Promises of a permanent clubhouse and resort remain unfulfilled, caught in legal disputes. Tourism efforts have been hampered by limited accommodation for visiting golfers. Yet the project has also catalyzed community development: the land from three former golf clubs made way for the new course, a housing estate with plans for over 2,000 lots, a retirement village, and short-stay facilities. Local membership has grown to 620 players, with renewed emphasis on junior development. This story offers a quiet lesson in the complicated math of legacy projects. What Marsh envisioned as a world-class course to bring recognition to the region has indeed delivered something distinctive—each hole dramatically different from the next, a serene "core golf" experience unburdened by surrounding housing. Whether the community's investment will fully pay off remains uncertain, but for now, the course hosts national tournaments and continues to grow its local game, a patch of green ambition in the red outback.
