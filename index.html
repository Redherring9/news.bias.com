‘Legally, they don’t exist’: the hybrid crabs puzzling biologists and fishers

In the waters off Alaska, something unexpected is happening beneath the waves. Researchers aboard fishing vessels have discovered that hybrid crabs—crosses between snow crabs and tanner crabs—now outnumber their pure-bred snow crab parents in commercially sized catches. These unusual creatures sport a blend of features from both species: squashed M-shaped mouths and eyes that are part red, part green. What was once a rarity has surged to more than thirteen times historical levels since 2025, and the trend appears to be accelerating. The hybrid boom traces back to an extreme marine heatwave in 2018 and 2019 that left the Bering Sea nearly ice-free and triggered mass starvation among snow crabs. Roughly 10 billion crabs died in what became one of the largest marine mass mortality events on record. With snow crabs largely absent, their hybrid offspring found an ecological opportunity. Without competition from their parent species—which would have competed for food and potentially preyed on younger crabs—the hybrids thrived in ways they never had before. This surge presents fisheries managers with a peculiar challenge: legally, hybrid crabs don't exist. Current regulations classify them as either snow crabs or tanner crabs based on physical features, meaning hybrids are sold under snow crab quotas that remain conservative after the population collapse. Fisheries are now advocating for separate quotas to account for this abundant new catch. The story offers a window into how rapidly ecosystems can shift under climate pressure, and how our management systems sometimes struggle to keep pace with nature's unexpected adaptations.

The British island that made a deal with nature

Lundy Island, a windswept outcrop in the Bristol Channel between England and Wales, has become one of the United Kingdom's most remarkable conservation success stories. Two decades ago, the island's seabird population had plummeted to around 6,000 birds, devastated by accidentally introduced rats that preyed on eggs and chicks in their burrows. Today, following a successful rat eradication program launched in the early 2000s, the seabird population has soared to approximately 48,000, including puffins, razorbills, guillemots, and Manx shearwaters. What makes Lundy's conservation approach particularly noteworthy is its collaborative spirit. Rather than simply excluding human activity, the island's marine protected area was designed with input from local fishermen, conservationists, and business owners who all depend on these waters. In 2003, Lundy became home to the UK's first no-take zone—a carefully designated area where all fishing is prohibited—while surrounding waters allow sustainable activities like recreational fishing and lobster potting. The restrictions on destructive practices like trawling and dredging have helped restore the island's diverse underwater habitats, from kelp forests and rocky reefs to sandy bottoms and caves. This story matters because it offers a blueprint for conservation that works with communities rather than against them. At a time when many British seabird populations continue to decline, Lundy demonstrates that recovery is possible when local stakeholders come together with a shared commitment to protecting their environment. The island now thrives as a place where wildlife, fishing traditions, and tourism coexist—proof that nature and human livelihoods need not be at odds.

Hatching a plan to ensure Australia's native turtles survive and thrive

Australia's 1 Million Turtles campaign is making quiet but remarkable progress in protecting the country's native freshwater turtles. Launched in 2021 with the ambitious goal of helping a million turtles survive over 30 years, the citizen science project has already logged nearly 40,000 turtle sightings and helped rescue more than 3,000 individual turtles from roads and other dangers. Volunteers have also discovered and protected nearly 2,500 nests, potentially safeguarding around 50,000 eggs. The program focuses on three species—the broad-shelled turtle, eastern long-necked turtle, and Murray River short-necked turtle—and relies on everyday Australians to report sightings through a mobile app called TurtleSAT. The data helps researchers understand turtle ecology and address critical knowledge gaps, particularly during the vulnerable period after hatching. By radio-tracking 50 hatchlings, scientists discovered that most young turtles are eaten by native birds during what researchers call "the missing years" before they re-emerge at around three years old. This finding reveals both the dangers hatchlings face and their important role as a food source in the broader ecosystem. This story offers a heartening example of how community science can make a tangible difference for wildlife. The wet spring currently underway in southeastern Australia is especially promising, as more water means more habitat and nesting opportunities. What makes this project quietly remarkable is its dual nature: while helping individual turtles survive today, it's simultaneously building the scientific knowledge needed to protect these ancient reptiles for generations to come.

In a carefully orchestrated conservation effort, 62 San Francisco garter snakes—born and raised at the San Francisco Zoo—have been released into the wild for the first time this autumn. Each young snake underwent genetic testing to ensure it was returned to the habitat best suited to its lineage—a detail that underscores the precision and collaboration behind the project, which involved state and federal agencies, nonprofits, and zoo scientists.
