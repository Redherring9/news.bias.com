First-time farmer solves mulberry packing problem with dental drill

When retired dentist Peter Lynch and his wife Karen noticed supermarkets didn't stock mulberries, they decided to try their hand at farming despite having no horticultural background. Starting with cuttings from a single suburban plant, the couple built Kureelpa Mulberry Company on Queensland's Sunshine Coast, gradually expanding to 1,500 trees over a decade. Their venture quickly revealed why few Australian farmers grow mulberries commercially: the delicate fruit bruises easily during transport. Initially, workers picked berries into buckets, then repacked them into punnets at the shed—a double-handling process that caused significant bruising and drove up labor costs. Drawing on his dental background, Peter devised an ingenious solution using a $4 plastic basket and his old dental drill. He reshaped the containers to hold two stacked piles of punnets that strap to pickers' waists, allowing them to pack fruit directly while keeping their hands free. This simple modification reduced fruit damage by 80 percent and helped the business invest in proper cold storage. The nostalgic fruit, which once thrived in larger Australian backyards, evokes memories of purple-stained fingers and silkworm-raising for many customers. Now the Lynchs face a different challenge: proposed cuts to working holiday maker visas threaten their ability to staff the intense four-week harvest each September and October. It's a small story about resourcefulness meeting regulatory reality—a reminder that innovation sometimes comes from unexpected places, and that even the humblest solution can transform a business built on patience and a willingness to experiment.

Jean Phillips-Martinsson obituary

When Jean Phillips-Martinsson stumbled upon a 1984 article about Prader-Willi syndrome in the Lancet, fourteen years of confusion suddenly made sense. Her son Anders, who had been floppy as a baby, struggled with behavioral issues, and was constantly hungry, finally had a diagnosis for the rare genetic condition that affects roughly one in 15,000 to 25,000 people. What began with a mother's determination to understand her son's condition grew into a global movement that would transform care for PWS families worldwide. Phillips-Martinsson's first instinct was to connect with other Swedish families facing the same isolation. After an emotional first meeting at her Stockholm home in 1986, she quickly recognized that the condition's rarity demanded international collaboration. Drawing on her skills in public relations, translation, and multiple languages, she networked tirelessly with emerging PWS organizations and researchers. In 1989, she partnered with American geneticist Dr. Suzanne Cassidy to organize a landmark 1991 conference in the Netherlands, securing support from organizations including the WHO and NATO. The gathering of 200 delegates from 22 countries voted to establish the International Prader-Willi Syndrome Organisation, with Phillips-Martinsson as its founding president. Under her leadership through 1998 and beyond as honorary president, IPWSO became a vital bridge between scientific research and the families who needed it most. She championed parent voices at medical conferences, established regular newsletters, and worked to reduce global inequality by lobbying for free genetic testing in underserved countries. This story reminds us how one person's search for answers can build networks of hope and practical support that reach around the world, turning isolation into community.

What photonics is and how it can make AI data centers consume less water and energy

Qué es la fotónica y cómo puede lograr que los centros de datos de la IA consuman menos agua y energía

As artificial intelligence drives unprecedented growth in data centers, industry leaders are looking to replace copper wiring with light-based photonics technology. Chris Sharp of Digital Realty suggests we're reaching "the end of the copper era" — not because supplies are running low, but because a better alternative is emerging. A typical 100-megawatt data center uses around 400 tons of copper, with roughly 20 tons dedicated solely to network cabling that connects servers and processing units. The solution lies in photonics: transmitting data as photons of light through fiber optic cables rather than as electrons through copper wire. While fiber optics have carried long-distance communications for decades, researchers are now working to bring this technology inside data centers themselves, sometimes integrating optical components directly onto computer chips. The key advantage is thermal: light generates far less heat than electricity, meaning data centers would require significantly less energy for cooling — a major contributor to their substantial environmental footprint. Photonics also allows multiple data streams to travel through a single channel, increasing capacity. After years of laboratory development, the technology appears ready for widespread adoption, bolstered by support from AI chip giant Nvidia. Yet challenges remain in manufacturing and cost reduction, as photonics brings together different engineering traditions and supply chains scattered across the globe. For readers concerned about technology's environmental impact, this story offers a glimpse of how innovation might quietly reduce the resource intensity of our digital infrastructure — making the invisible machinery of the internet age just a bit lighter on the planet.

AI models cost more when you don’t use English

A new study has uncovered a surprising economic disparity hiding in plain sight within artificial intelligence systems: people using less common languages may pay significantly more to access AI services than English speakers. The research, published by the Coalition for Language Equity in AI Research, reveals that AI models require vastly different numbers of "tokens"—the basic units these systems process—depending on the language used. Even among the internet's 20 most popular languages, some require up to five times more tokens than others. When the analysis expanded to 200 languages, one model showed a 17-fold gap in token costs. This isn't just about money. Higher token counts mean slower response times and reduced capacity to retain conversation history or context—imagine a tutor who can't remember your earlier questions as clearly. The disadvantage hits hardest in regions where computing resources and internet connectivity are already limited, potentially determining who can participate meaningfully in the AI economy. A startup in Bengaluru might face surcharges that its Austin-based competitor doesn't encounter. The script a language uses matters as much as its prevalence: Asturian, with few speakers, benefits from using the Latin alphabet and resembling Italian, while languages written in Burmese, Tibetan, or Khmer scripts face consistent disadvantages across nearly all models. This story matters because it reveals how technical design choices can create invisible barriers in ostensibly borderless technology. While token prices are falling and researchers are collecting better multilingual data, experts say truly fixing the problem will require deliberate effort to design tokenizers that treat all languages and scripts more equitably—turning language efficiency into a core requirement alongside speed and accuracy.

Lights out: the island that goes dark to save young seabirds – in pictures

On a tiny island in the Azores, an entire community chooses darkness to save lives. Every autumn for five nights, Corvo—home to just 450 people and the smallest island in Portugal's Atlantic archipelago—switches off all public lights. The reason is both simple and profound: to help young Cory's shearwaters find their way safely to the ocean for the first time. The story began in 1991 when locals noticed alarming numbers of juvenile seabirds dying in their streets. These fledglings spend three months in their nests before taking their maiden flight, navigating by starlight toward the sea. But artificial streetlights fatally confuse them, causing crashes in the narrow lanes of Vila do Corvo. Residents began venturing out in darkness to rescue disoriented birds, an effort that inspired a regional campaign now spanning all nine Azorean islands. Yet Corvo remains unique—the only island committed to a complete blackout. During these nights, volunteers walk through the darkened village collecting fallen birds, placing them in cardboard boxes to recover, then releasing them from oceanside cliffs the following day. Since 2015, more than 30,000 shearwater fledglings have been rescued across the Azores, with 6,000 saved on Corvo alone. This story matters because it demonstrates something quietly radical: that a small community can choose collective inconvenience for the sake of another species. It's a reminder that conservation doesn't always require grand policies or cutting-edge technology—sometimes it simply asks us to turn off the lights and let the stars guide those who depend on them.

This transformation decimates the diverse ecosystems that depend on healthy reefs, from tiny invertebrates to commercially important fish species. The world's most celebrated reef systems—including Australia's Great Barrier Reef, the Coral Triangle, and the Red Sea reefs—are all classified as critically endangered. The timing is especially precarious, as the emergence of a strong El Niño pattern compounds the climate-related threats these ecosystems already face. Yet amid the stark warnings, scientists emphasize that coral reefs have demonstrated surprising resilience, with the ability to recover from severe bleaching when conditions allow. The assessment reminds us that these "rainforests of the sea" support a quarter of all marine species and provide essential resources—food, tourism revenue, and coastal protection—to communities worldwide. This story matters because it frames a critical moment: coral reefs are not yet lost, but the window for meaningful action is rapidly closing.

The immortal jellyfish that may inspire treatments for age-related diseases

A tiny, translucent jellyfish called Turritopsis dohrnii has captured scientists' attention for its remarkable ability to cheat death. Marine biologist Maria Miglietta has tried nearly everything to kill these creatures—cutting, boiling, starving, exposing them to extreme salinity—yet they persist. When stressed, the jellyfish sink to the bottom of their tanks and transform into balls of cells, completely losing their characteristic umbrella shape. Within days, these cell clusters develop into polyps, an early life stage that grows into a colony capable of producing hundreds of new jellyfish, each potentially able to repeat the cycle indefinitely. This regenerative phenomenon was first documented in 1992 when an Italian scientist accidentally left his jellyfish unfed over a weekend and returned to find new polyps instead of dead specimens. Researchers discovered that during rejuvenation, certain tissues undergo "transdifferentiation," reverting to stem cell states before specializing into different cell types needed to form the polyp. This ability has earned Turritopsis dohrnii the nickname "immortal jellyfish" and represents what scientists believe is a unique capability in the animal kingdom. While transforming elderly humans into babies remains science fiction, researchers hope understanding these molecular and genetic processes could revolutionize regenerative medicine. The goal is to help cells and tissues return to younger states to heal wounds and treat age-related diseases. Though practical human applications remain distant, studying organisms that evolved these processes millions of years ago offers a window into possibilities that might one day help address degeneration and aging in meaningful ways.
