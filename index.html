‘You’d want one as a pet’: the irresistibly cute solar-powered sea slug

A sea slug the size of a grain of rice has captured hearts and imaginations worldwide, not just for its adorable appearance but for its remarkable ability to harness solar power. The leaf sheep, or Costasiella kuroshimae, grazes on algae in tropical waters around Japan, Indonesia, and the Philippines, but it does something extraordinary with its food: it steals the chloroplasts from the algae and stores them in leaf-like branches on its back called cerata. Through a process called kleptoplasty, these stolen chloroplasts continue to perform photosynthesis inside the slug's own cells, essentially turning the tiny creature into a solar-powered animal. Its vivid green cerata, often tipped with white, pink, or purple, serve double duty as both solar panels and chemical defenses, while also providing perfect camouflage against the velvety algae where it lives and feeds. The leaf sheep navigates its world using rhinophores—tentacle-like sense organs above its eyes that help it locate food and interpret its surroundings. Only identified by scientists in 1993, the leaf sheep has become an internet sensation thanks to advances in underwater macro photography that reveal its irresistibly cute features. Readers comparing it to everything from Shaun the Sheep to Pokémon characters have nominated it for Invertebrate of the Year 2026. Beyond its charm, the leaf sheep offers an inspiring example of nature's ingenuity—a tiny creature that figured out how to power itself with sunlight, a lesson that resonates powerfully in our era of seeking sustainable energy solutions.

How a 'fecal explosion' transformed Earth 540 million years ago

A new scientific review suggests that a dramatic shift in how early animals ate and digested food may have helped transform Earth's oceans around 540 million years ago. This timing coincides with the Cambrian explosion, when many major animal groups first appeared and marine life underwent rapid diversification. As animals developed more sophisticated digestive systems and consumed different types of food, they began producing larger and more varied waste. These fecal pellets acted like small packages of nutrients, sinking through the water column and delivering organic matter from surface waters to deeper regions of the ocean. The review draws on evidence from more than 35 fossil deposits worldwide, including coprolites—fossilized feces—ranging from microscopic particles to specimens several centimeters long. Some preserved shells and animal fragments, revealing increasingly complex feeding relationships. Researchers also found signs of predation and prey remains within the digestive systems of fossils. The scientists emphasize that feces aren't the sole explanation for the Cambrian explosion; factors like rising oxygen levels and environmental changes also played crucial roles. But this study adds a compelling dimension to our understanding of that ancient transformation. The efficient transport of carbon and nutrients by animal waste may have accelerated the diversification of ocean life, establishing patterns that persist in modern marine ecosystems. It's a reminder that even the most humble biological processes can have profound consequences for life on Earth.
