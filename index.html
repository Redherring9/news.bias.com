PearlBone gets FDA clearance for world's largest orthopaedic market

A Western Australian biotech company has achieved a quietly remarkable milestone: receiving US Food and Drug Administration clearance for a bone graft substitute made from mother-of-pearl harvested in the Kimberley region. PearlBone, developed by Marine Biomedical, is the first product of its kind to use nacre—the iridescent material typically associated with jewelry and ornamental items—for repairing bone fractures. The material's composition shares building blocks with human bone, making it effective for bone healing and regeneration. The company emerged from a 2021 partnership between Broome pearling business Willie Creek Pearls and the University of Western Australia. Pearl shells, a byproduct of the farm's pearl production, undergo cleaning and preparation before being manufactured into the medical product. The FDA clearance demonstrates that PearlBone is as safe and effective as existing bone grafts in the US market, which is valued at $1.5 billion. While regulatory approval is an important first step, the company still faces hurdles before the product can reach patients and plans to eventually bring it to Australian customers and expand into dental applications. This story offers a fresh perspective on how traditional industries can intersect with cutting-edge medical innovation. It highlights the potential hidden in materials long valued for their beauty, now finding purpose in healing. For the pearling industry, it opens doors to entirely new applications, and for medical researchers in Western Australia, it represents an inspiring example of local innovation making its way onto the global stage.

Jojo the gorilla has spent his life alone. Can he finally find a companion?

Jojo, a western lowland gorilla now in his twenties, has lived almost entirely without the company of other gorillas. Captured from the wild as an infant after poachers killed his family, he spent over a decade confined alone in a tiny cage at a private zoo in Gabon before being moved to the Lekedi Foundation sanctuary in 2015. His caregivers now hope to find him a companion, but the challenge is complex given his long isolation and the trauma he has endured. Experts studying Jojo describe his case as one of the most extreme examples of social deprivation they have encountered in great apes. Most gorillas spend their first 14 to 19 years embedded in family groups, learning essential social behaviors. Jojo, by contrast, craves human interaction and makes calls typically used by silverbacks to summon females, signs his keepers interpret as profound loneliness. Researcher Gillian Forrester has used thermal imaging to reveal what appears to be chronic stress: Jojo's nose remains persistently cold, a sign his stress response may be malfunctioning after years of isolation. Jojo is one of thousands of African primates caught up in legal and illegal trade for exotic pets and other purposes between 2000 and 2023. This story matters because it illuminates both the devastating psychological toll of the primate trade and the difficult, compassionate work of helping traumatized animals recover. While other sanctuaries successfully rehabilitate young gorillas by quickly introducing them to groups, Jojo's maturity and deep-rooted stress make his path to companionship uncertain. His case is a quiet reminder of what social connection means to great apes—and what happens when it is taken away.

Tracking down Tasmania's rare, flightless pandani moth

In the remote alpine regions of Tasmania, a peculiar bronze-brown moth hops among the fronds of pandani plants, having long ago traded flight for a more grounded existence. The pandani moth is one of Tasmania's many "living relics," ancient species that have vanished elsewhere but persist on this island ark. First spotted in the 1980s and formally named in the early 2000s, the moth remained elusive until a dedicated group of volunteers—calling themselves the Friends of the Pandani Moth—set out to find it. Their search required patience and creativity. Unlike most moths, this species isn't drawn to light, so the team visited Mount Field National Park armed with white sheets and a plan to physically shake the pandani plants after dark. After hours of effort in the snow, a single moth tumbled onto their sheet—a moment ecologist Jarrah Dale described as "something out of a fairy tale." The discovery sparked a follow-up expedition to gather genetic samples and population data, helping researchers piece together the moth's mysterious life cycle and ecology. This story offers a quiet reminder of how much remains unknown even in well-studied places. Tasmania hosts roughly 2,000 native moth species, yet hundreds remain unnamed—a biodiversity rivaling that of native plants. With climate change and bushfires accelerating, scientists face a race to document these species before they vanish. The pandani moth, found nowhere else on Earth, represents both the wonder of Tasmania's unique evolutionary path and the fragility of ecosystems we're only beginning to understand. It's a tale of curiosity rewarded and the enduring appeal of seeking out life's smaller, stranger mysteries.

Fruit bats emerge as unlikely allies in the Atlantic Forest restoration

In Brazil's Atlantic Forest, fruit-eating bats are proving to be powerful partners in restoring degraded landscapes. These often-misunderstood mammals disperse seeds from more than 140 plant species, potentially depositing up to 25,000 seeds per hectare daily in areas needing regeneration. Unlike most birds, bats scatter seeds while flying, crossing agricultural land for several kilometers and bridging fragmented forest patches. For over two decades, researchers have been studying how to harness this natural behavior through an elegant strategy: scent. Scientists discovered that bats rely on their acute sense of smell to locate ripe fruit, leading to experiments using essential oils to attract them to restoration sites. Early tests in Paraná State Park successfully lured bats 50 meters into open agricultural fields using pariparoba fruit scent. Researchers then analyzed the chemical composition of favored fruits, finding that individual species like pariparoba release 95 different compounds that vary with ripeness and harvest time. The team is now working to identify synthetic alternatives that could be produced sustainably, avoiding the ecological harm of harvesting essential oils from wild plants. This research reflects a broader shift in how ecologists view bats—not as creatures of horror, but as essential ecosystem engineers. Professor Lays Parolin, who became captivated by these animals early in her career and now sports a tattoo of the great fruit-eating bat, notes that people tend to overlook bats' role as seed dispersers. The emerging scent-based strategy offers a promising complement to conventional restoration techniques, turning these nocturnal fliers into active participants in healing fragmented forests.

What the timber industry considers a flaw—a sign of compromised, rotting wood—craftspeople treasure for its beauty. The unique variations transform ordinary lumber into art, as seen in wooden bowls and furniture. This quiet observation offers a reminder that appearances can deceive in nature. Investigation: Fungus may have killed Atlantic Forest amphibians in the 1970s and ’80s

Scientists believe they've solved a decades-old mystery behind the dramatic die-off of frogs in Brazil's Atlantic Forest during the 1970s and '80s. For years, researchers debated what caused the disappearance, with theories ranging from extreme weather events to acid rain. Now, a team analyzing preserved museum specimens has found compelling evidence pointing to the chytrid fungus—a deadly pathogen that attacks amphibian skin and has devastated frog populations worldwide. The breakthrough came from examining 370 frog specimens collected at Brazil's Boracéia research station before and after the decline. Using DNA testing, researchers discovered no trace of the Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis fungus in samples from 1978, but found it widespread in specimens from 1983 onward—suggesting a rapid invasion during the gap years when no frogs were collected. The pathogen, which wasn't scientifically described until 1999, is now known to have emerged in Asia in the 1970s and has since contributed to declines in more than 500 amphibian species globally. The fungus disrupts critical skin functions, affecting oxygen intake and mineral regulation, often proving fatal. This investigation offers more than just answers to a historical puzzle. It demonstrates how carefully preserved museum collections serve as biological time capsules, enabling scientists to investigate disease outbreaks long after they occur. The methods developed could become a toolkit for solving similar wildlife mysteries and help researchers anticipate future disease patterns. It's a reminder that patience, preservation, and scientific curiosity can illuminate the past in ways that protect the future—turning dusty specimens into keys for understanding emerging threats to biodiversity.

Dozens of jobs saved after local investors buy Candida's NZ manufacturing plant

A New Zealand envelope manufacturer that faced closure has found a lifeline through local investment, preserving dozens of skilled jobs in Auckland's North Shore. Candida, an Australasian stationery company founded in Auckland in 1976, had announced earlier this year it would shutter its New Zealand plant and consolidate operations in Australia, putting approximately 57 positions at risk. Now, New Zealand-owned private equity firm Muri Capital has stepped in to purchase the local assets and continue envelope production under the Candida name. The buyout means manufacturing will continue without interruption, with director Guy Phillips confirming that "dozens" of the threatened jobs will be reinstated. Workers will receive permanent contracts, and the company plans to honor employees' original start dates—a gesture that recognizes their years of service. Muri Capital, which already owns printing company Touchprint, has committed to maintaining competitive manufacturing standards and becoming a Living Wage employer, ensuring staff earn the same or better wages than before. The timing proves particularly fortuitous, with the Christmas season—a peak period for envelope demand—just around the corner. This story offers a quietly hopeful counterpoint to familiar narratives of manufacturing decline. It demonstrates that local investment and confidence in skilled workers can create viable alternatives to offshoring. In an era when envelope usage might seem diminished by digital communication, the survival of this nearly 50-year-old New Zealand operation suggests there remains real value—and real demand—for domestic manufacturing capabilities and the specialized expertise required to keep those machines running.

What does a biologist find around Brazil? Travels reveal surprising landscapes and stories

O que uma bióloga encontra pelo Brasil? Viagens revelam paisagens e histórias surpreendentes

A Brazilian biologist's journey through her country's diverse ecosystems reveals both the wonder of nature and the power of accessible education. Thamires Freitas Campos, originally from Pernambuco, has spent her career traveling across northeastern Brazil for environmental research and conservation projects, camera in hand to document the landscapes and creatures she encounters along the way. From childhood curiosity about plants and animals to earning degrees in biological sciences and biodiversity conservation, Thamires credits public education policies for making her academic path possible. Her work has taken her across six northeastern states, studying everything from microplastics in amphibians to coordinating environmental licensing programs that involve flora rescue and wildlife management. The dynamic nature of her work means each project brings new challenges and requires adaptation to different environmental and administrative realities. She holds particular affection for the Caatinga, a semi-arid biome often overlooked despite its remarkable biodiversity and specially adapted species. Among her most memorable experiences was participating in the reintroduction project for the Lear's macaw, an emblematic species with an extremely restricted range in the Bahian sertão. Working alongside conservation coordinator Érica Pacífico, Thamires helped with bird releases and population censuses, then tracked the animals after their return to the wild. Her story quietly illustrates how scientific careers can open doors to extraordinary encounters with nature while connecting professionals to local communities across varied landscapes—a reminder that conservation work is as much about people and place as it is about protecting species.

Latin America's first quantum computer to be installed in Paraíba; learn the details

Primeiro computador quântico da América Latina vai ser instalado na Paraíba; conheça os detalhes

A replica of Latin America's first quantum computers has been installed at a technology park in João Pessoa, Brazil, offering the public a glimpse of cutting-edge technology soon to arrive in the northeastern state of Paraíba. The actual quantum computers, currently being shipped from China, are expected to be operational by December at the new Quantum Computing Center of Paraíba, backed by an initial investment of approximately 150 million reais. Unlike classical computers, quantum computers don't simply work faster—they solve problems differently. By harnessing the principles of quantum physics applied to extremely small particles, these machines can tackle complex calculations involving millions of variables that would stump traditional systems. The technology holds promise for advances in artificial intelligence, cybersecurity, banking, logistics, and drug development. One of the most striking features visitors notice is the cooling system: the quantum chip must be kept at an astonishingly cold temperature of 10 millikelvin—just a fraction of a degree above absolute zero—maintained by a sophisticated dilution refrigerator visible in the replica. This story matters because it marks a significant technological milestone for Latin America and challenges assumptions about where advanced scientific infrastructure takes root. That a state in northeastern Brazil is becoming home to this rare technology speaks to the global spread of quantum research and the potential for emerging regions to leap forward in fields once dominated by a handful of wealthy nations. It's a quiet reminder that the future of computing is being built in unexpected places.

French wartime resistance heroine Solange Alexandre dies aged 104

France has lost one of its last living World War Two resistance heroes. Solange Alexandre, who died at 104, was a rare woman who not only fought against Nazi occupation but commanded her own network of fighters. Her story offers a window into the courage and resourcefulness that defined the French Resistance during one of history's darkest chapters. Born in 1920s France to a family marked by World War One, Alexandre was just eighteen when she heard Charles de Gaulle's radio appeal from London in 1940. She soon joined the Resistance as a courier, bicycling across the Le Mans region to pass messages and gather intelligence. By 1944, she and her first husband—a gendarme who had deserted rather than arrest Jewish citizens—founded the Pas-de-Boeuf network. Leading some thirty men, she organized sabotage operations, sheltered Allied airmen, and fought German forces after D-Day. She narrowly escaped the Gestapo twice and ended the war as a sub-lieutenant. What makes Alexandre's story quietly remarkable is not just her bravery, but her humility. She twice refused France's highest honor, the Légion d'Honneur, insisting she had "only done her duty." She finally accepted in 2024, just months before her death. In an era when women's contributions to wartime resistance are often overlooked, her leadership and moral clarity stand as a testament to the power of ordinary people who choose extraordinary paths. Her life reminds us that history's turning points depend not only on famous generals, but on those who, armed with little more than conviction and a bicycle, refuse to surrender to tyranny.

Eleven unknown Australian soldiers killed in WWI formally identified

More than a century after they fell in World War I, eleven Australian soldiers once commemorated as unknown have been formally identified, giving names and stories back to men who made the ultimate sacrifice. The identifications were made possible through a combination of historical research, archaeological investigation, and DNA analysis, bringing closure to families who have carried their relatives' stories for generations. The soldiers, who served in various battalions and came from towns across Australia, include men ranging from privates to decorated officers. Among them is Lieutenant Andrew Anderson, killed at age 23 during the Battle of Menin Road in Belgium in 1917. His case illustrates the painstaking detective work involved: buried by fellow soldiers near a roadside and marked with a wooden cross, his remains were recovered in 1919 with the notation "Anderson on shirt" on the grave registration record, but his identity wasn't confirmed at the time. An independent researcher flagged the possible connection in 2020, eventually leading to verification. In a remarkable personal connection, Defence Minister Richard Marles revealed Anderson was his great-uncle, saying the identification was "profoundly meaningful" for his family. The announcement, timed for Remembrance Day 108 years after the Armistice, represents what officials called "the ultimate expression of lest we forget." These identifications matter not just as historical corrections but as acts of remembrance that restore individual humanity to the collective memory of war. For the descendants who assisted researchers and the communities these soldiers came from, seeing names replace "unknown" on headstones ensures that specific lives, specific sacrifices, are honored by future generations—a quiet but powerful form of homecoming.

Inside the Wellington Town Hall after 13 years shut

After being shuttered for 13 years due to earthquake safety concerns, Wellington's historic Town Hall is nearing completion of what has become one of New Zealand's most complex building restoration projects. The venue, which first closed in 2013 after being deemed earthquake-prone on its original unreinforced concrete piles and reclaimed coastal land, has undergone a dramatic transformation—both structurally and in budget. The project ballooned from an initial $43 million estimate to nearly $330 million, though the final phase appears likely to come in $20 million under the latest budget. The engineering challenges were formidable: crews had to lift the entire building, install base isolation to protect against earthquakes, and navigate a stream running underneath—all while preserving the 121-year-old Adam Auditorium's world-renowned acoustics and the original 1906 organ. Modern touches complement the heritage restoration, including light installations honoring indigenous design and coastal themes. Beyond renovation, the Town Hall will house the new National Music Centre, a collaboration between Victoria University's music school and the New Zealand Symphony Orchestra. Students will share practice and performance spaces with professional musicians, filling the building with sound 135 days a year. What makes this story quietly remarkable is the vision of culture as civic infrastructure. Young trombonist Jakob Elijas captures it simply: inspiring environments shape the art people create. For a city that invested hundreds of millions in restoration, the hope is that filling a grand old building with hundreds of young musicians daily might give them reason to build careers at home rather than looking overseas—a modest but meaningful bet on culture keeping communities alive.

The swan sanctuary wetland fighting climate change

As the Wildfowl and Wetlands Trust celebrates eight decades since its founding, scientists are highlighting an unexpected climate ally: wetlands can absorb carbon four times faster than rainforests. The charity, which now operates ten reserves across the UK, is calling for the restoration of one million hectares of wetlands worldwide—a crucial goal given that 85% of the world's wetlands have already been lost. The story begins in 1946, when naturalist Peter Scott arrived at marshy land near Slimbridge, Gloucestershire, captivated by thousands of migrating geese and swans. He built a home with a panoramic window overlooking the wetlands, dug lakes and channels, and created observation towers. What started as one man's passion grew into a pioneering conservation movement. Scott went on to campaign against commercial whaling, present early BBC natural history programs, and help establish the World Wildlife Fund. His work earned him a knighthood in 1973—the first ever awarded for conservation. One of his boldest projects involved rescuing the Hawaiian Goose, or Nēnē, from near-extinction. When only 24 birds remained in 1949, Scott hand-reared them at Slimbridge, eventually helping the population recover to over 3,000. This story quietly reminds us that individual vision can spark systemic change. Beyond providing sanctuary for endangered species, these wetlands offer flood protection, water purification, and mood-boosting spaces for visitors—all while silently fighting climate change beneath the surface. It's a testament to how protecting nature's most overlooked places might be among our most powerful tools for the future.

Alien menace or victim of xenophobia? The battle over Britain’s parakeets

Every evening at dusk, thousands of ring-necked parakeets descend on Hither Green cemetery in south-east London, creating a spectacle that feels oddly out of place in the English suburbs. These bright-green birds with scarlet beaks aren't native to Britain—they hail from the Himalayan foothills of Asia—but over the past fifty years, they've established thriving populations across the country, even reaching as far north as Glasgow. The sight of their nightly gathering is described as both awesome and glitch-like: a metropolitan wildlife experience as impressive as any salmon run or butterfly migration. Yet the parakeets have become controversial. In their native range, they're considered pests, and European studies suggest they may negatively impact local bird and bat populations. They're also carriers of Newcastle disease and Cryptosporidium, raising health concerns for poultry and humans alike. Despite alleged government plans for a cull in 2021, the idea was abandoned due to cost and logistical challenges. Meanwhile, the population continues to grow—one researcher estimated in 2003 that it would need to be reduced by 30% annually just to stabilize numbers in Greater London. This story captures a tension at the heart of modern urban wildlife: are these parakeets an invasive menace or simply adaptable newcomers making a home in a changing world? It's a quietly remarkable reminder that nature doesn't always follow our expectations, and that the question of belonging—whether for birds or people—is rarely simple. The parakeets offer city dwellers a rare brush with wild abundance, even as they challenge our ideas about what belongs where.
