Beavers filmed bonding after release in the wild

Two beavers released into the wild on England's Holnicote Estate have been caught on trail cameras grooming each other, a promising sign they may be forming a lifelong pair bond. The semi-aquatic rodents were released in February 2026 as part of the National Trust's effort to restore rivers and wetlands across the 5,000-hectare Somerset estate, and conservationists are hopeful their pairing indicates they've found suitable habitat and resources to establish a territory together. Beavers typically live in small family groups centered on an adult pair who mate for life. Once established, their engineering activities—building dams, digging canals, and creating wetlands—generate new habitats for other wildlife and help retain water in the landscape for longer periods. These two animals were among the first groups released freely in Somerset following the UK government's 2025 approval of beaver reintroduction in England. The species had been reintroduced to Holnicote previously, but only within enclosed areas. This story offers a quiet glimpse into rewilding in action. Beavers were hunted to extinction in Britain during the 16th century for their fur, meat, and scent glands, making their return a meaningful ecological restoration. The trail camera footage—capturing behavior that happens largely at night, beyond human observation—reveals not just the animals' presence but their social lives taking root. If this pair stays together and eventually raises young, they could help establish a self-sustaining beaver population that reshapes the Somerset landscape for generations of wildlife to come.

UN votes to adopt new world map that reflects Africa’s true size

The United Nations General Assembly has voted overwhelmingly to adopt a new world map that more accurately represents the true sizes of continents and countries. The resolution, sponsored by African Union member states and the Bahamas, passed 164-1 with six abstentions, with only the United States voting against it. The initiative seeks to address a centuries-old cartographic distortion that has visually diminished Africa's size and prominence for 450 years. The widely-used Mercator projection, created by Flemish cartographer Gerardus Mercator in the 16th century, expands land masses closer to the poles while shrinking those near the equator. This makes Greenland appear roughly the same size as Africa, even though Africa is actually about 14 times larger. Critics argue this distortion has shaped global perceptions, perpetuating ideas of European superiority while marginalizing the Global South in education, media, and policy. The resolution promotes the Equal Earth projection as a fairer alternative, though it remains non-binding and doesn't affect maritime or aerial navigation, where Mercator remains useful. This quiet cartographic revolution reflects a broader reckoning with colonial legacies. As Togo's Foreign Minister Robert Dussey put it, "A fair world begins with a fair map." France has already announced plans to abandon the Mercator projection in official materials. The campaign's real work now begins: encouraging schools, newsrooms, tech companies, and international organizations to adopt more accurate representations. The story reminds us how easily visual distortions can shape our understanding of the world—and how correcting something as fundamental as a map might help us question other unexamined assumptions about entire continents and cultures.

What is terva, the 'tar' that Finns can't stop eating

Qué es el tarvu, el "alquitrán" que los finlandeses no pueden dejar de comer

In the northern Finnish city of Oulu, visitors ordering a simple pancake might be surprised to find tar among the flavor options. This isn't a mistake—terva, or pine tar, has become an unlikely culinary ingredient, flavoring everything from beer to ice cream with its intense, smoky character. The thick, dark substance is produced by heating pinewood in the absence of oxygen, and while it might sound unappetizing to outsiders, it carries deep cultural significance for Finns who associate its aroma with wooden boats, summer cottages, and saunas. Oulu's relationship with pine tar runs centuries deep. By the 1860s, this picturesque Baltic port city had become the world's largest tar exporter, thanks to surrounding pine forests and the winding channels of the Oulujoki River delta. Tar was essential for waterproofing wooden sailing ships, produced through slow combustion in forest pits before being transported downriver for global export. Beyond shipbuilding, Finns valued tar for its antibacterial properties, using it as a natural remedy—captured in the saying, "If sauna, alcohol, and tar don't help, the illness is fatal." The leap to food began in pharmacies, where medicinal tar lozenges appeared in the early 1900s to treat coughs and respiratory ailments. The iconic Terva Leijona (Tar Lion) lozenge, created in 1933 by Oulu pharmacist Y.W. Jalander after observing locals inhaling tar smoke for health benefits, remains popular today. This story offers a fascinating glimpse into how practical necessity can evolve into cultural identity, transforming an industrial material into a beloved flavor that connects modern Finns to their maritime and folk medicine heritage—a reminder that tradition often tastes nothing like we'd expect.
