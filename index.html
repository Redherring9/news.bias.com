SpaceX has achieved a milestone in reusable rocket technology by successfully catching the upper stage of its Starship system as it returned to Earth. SpaceX founder Elon Musk envisions Starship operating with the frequency and reliability of airliners, supporting everything from satellite deployment to NASA's planned moon missions and eventual Mars exploration. While challenges remain, including perfecting the heat shield for complete reusability, the successful catch demonstrates that catching giant rockets from space may become routine rather than remarkable.

Return of the wild oyster: conservationists bring shellfish back from local extinction off Scottish coast

Off the coast of Scotland, something remarkable is happening beneath the waves. For the first time in the UK, scientists have confirmed that wild European flat oysters are reproducing naturally at a site where they had vanished more than a century ago. In the Dornoch Firth north of Inverness, researchers discovered baby oysters — each about the size of a 50-pence coin — proving that a twelve-year restoration effort is finally bearing fruit. The story of Britain's native oysters is one of dramatic boom and bust. Victorian-era maps reveal that billions once carpeted the seabed, forming vast reefs that supported thriving marine ecosystems. At their peak, 30 million oysters a year were harvested from the Firth of Forth alone, shipped by railway to feed growing cities. But within decades, these populations were fished to extinction across much of the UK. Today, European flat oysters are critically threatened worldwide. The Dornoch Environmental Enhancement Project has spent over a decade carefully relocating roughly 140,000 adult oysters from one of Scotland's few surviving populations, cleaned by volunteers to protect biosecurity. The breakthrough came when researchers found juveniles thriving on their own — helped, unexpectedly, by warmer seas in recent years. This quiet success matters because oysters do far more than feed us. They filter water, stabilize sediments, store carbon, and create complex habitats that can double seabed biodiversity within a decade. The Dornoch project has inspired similar efforts across the UK, offering hope that patient, science-led restoration can reverse what seemed like permanent loss. It's a story about second chances — for ecosystems, for species, and for the possibility of repair.

'Eu não pinto gordas': o que está por trás do estilo único de Botero

Fernando Botero, the celebrated Colombian artist who passed away in 2023, created one of the most instantly recognizable styles in modern art. His figures—whether reimagining the Mona Lisa or depicting everyday scenes—are marked by rounded, voluminous forms with large heads and delicate features. This distinctive aesthetic, known as "Boterismo," has made him one of Latin America's most renowned artists, with works displayed everywhere from New York's Park Avenue to Paris's Champs-Élysées. The style was born almost by accident in 1956 when Botero, living in Mexico City, drew an unusually small sound hole on a mandolin in a still-life painting. The instrument suddenly appeared disproportionately large and full, sparking a lifelong artistic approach. For Botero, exaggerating forms was "a way to celebrate the presence of things"—a celebration of life, sensuality, and nature itself. His son recalls that the artist believed art's purpose throughout history was to provide pleasure, something he felt shape and color could uniquely achieve. The Museo Botero in Bogotá houses not only his own paintings and sculptures but also his personal collection, including works by Picasso, Monet, and Dalí, deliberately positioning himself within the European artistic tradition he deeply admired. This story offers a gentle look at how a singular vision can redefine artistic expression—and how one artist's joyful celebration of volume and form became a universal visual language that continues to lift spirits worldwide.

In the waterways of the Brazilian Amazon, a century-old tradition brings color and personality to the region's boats: skilled artisans known as "abridores de letras" hand-paint the names and decorative flourishes on vessels that travel the rivers. This November, researchers from the Instituto Letras que Flutuam are expanding their fieldwork to Santarém and Alter do Chão in western Pará state, mapping these traditional sign painters and documenting their distinctive techniques. Each abridor de letras develops a signature style—unique combinations of colors, letterforms, and ornamental designs that become part of the visual landscape of Amazonian rivers. Beyond preservation, the mapping addresses questions of authorship and recognition, as the distinctive lettering styles increasingly appear on clothing, brands, and commercial products, often without crediting the original creators. This story offers a window into how cultural traditions adapt and endure in riverine communities, and how documentation efforts can support both preservation and fair recognition. The project invites local participation—residents can share information about artisans they know—and includes workshops for students, a public mural, and a free screening of a documentary about the letter-painters of Marajó. Ocean that once covered Flinders Ranges preserved Earth's earliest life

Deep beneath the rugged peaks of South Australia's Flinders Ranges lies evidence of an ancient ocean that cradled some of Earth's very first complex organisms. Researchers from the University of Adelaide have mapped unusual rock formations across the region, revealing a network of massive submarine canyons—some reaching 280 metres deep and 6 kilometres wide—that formed roughly 550 million years ago during the Ediacaran period. The discovery offers fresh insight into how rising sea levels and flooding seawater carved these underwater valleys, creating ideal conditions for early life to flourish. As sediment filled the canyons and shallow shelf environments developed, microbial mats took hold, supporting the diverse community of soft-bodied organisms known as Ediacaran biota. These strange, alien-looking creatures represent a pivotal moment in Earth's history: the dawn of complex life, appearing just before the explosion of animal diversity that would follow. The Flinders Ranges became famous for these fossils when they were first discovered in 1946, and the site remains one of the world's most important windows into this distant era. This story matters because it helps us understand not just what early life looked like, but where and why it emerged. The research contributes to an ongoing effort to secure UNESCO World Heritage status for the Flinders Ranges, recognizing the area's unique geological and biological significance. For anyone curious about our planet's deep past, this work offers a rare glimpse into a world so ancient it predates nearly every form of life we recognize today—a landscape that is, in the words of the researchers, perhaps the most alien environment we can still touch.

This dictionary update is more than a catalog of vocabulary — it's a quiet acknowledgment that language belongs to everyone who speaks it. How to spot November's planetary parade in the pre-dawn sky

November offers early risers a rare celestial treat: four planets lining up in a diagonal parade across the pre-dawn sky. Mercury, Venus, Mars, and Jupiter will all be visible to the naked eye in the southeastern to eastern horizon throughout the month, with no telescope required. The show becomes particularly special on November 15-16, when Mars and Jupiter appear to nearly touch, separated by just the width of a finger held at arm's length—close enough that they might resemble a triple star alongside the bright star Regulus. Each planet brings its own character to the display. Venus, the brightest object in the night sky after the Moon, will be easiest to spot thanks to its high reflectivity and proximity to Earth. Mercury, perpetually locked in solar glare as the Sun's closest companion, will be the trickiest to find—hovering dim and low on the horizon. Mars and Jupiter dominate higher in the sky, with Jupiter outshining its reddish neighbor. For those with binoculars, bonus sightings of Uranus, Saturn, and Neptune are also possible. Starting November 24, the first of four supermoons joins the planetary lineup, adding the Moon's glow to the morning spectacle. This story reminds us that some of nature's most stunning performances require nothing more than an alarm clock and clear skies. It's a gentle invitation to step outside in the quiet hours before sunrise, let your eyes adjust to the darkness, and witness worlds aligning in the vastness above—a moment of cosmic choreography that asks only for our attention.
