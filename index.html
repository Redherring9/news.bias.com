Uncovering the secrets of New Zealand’s elusive ‘jungle penguins’

In the remote fjords of New Zealand's South Island, researchers are unraveling the mysteries of one of the world's most peculiar penguins. The Fiordland crested penguin, known by its Māori name tawaki, has proven so elusive that scientists describe it as a "ghost." These half-meter-tall birds with striking yellow eyebrows dig elaborate tunnel systems to escape capture, sometimes taking researchers days to retrieve tracking devices. Until 2014, almost nothing was known about them. The Tawaki Project, founded by Dr. Thomas Mattern and his wife Ursula Ellenberg, has mapped over 1,200 nests across nine fjords in twelve years, revealing a population roughly three times larger than previously thought. What makes these penguins truly unusual is their complete rejection of typical penguin behavior. While most penguins nest in coastal colonies, tawaki build solitary burrows deep in dense rainforest, sometimes at high elevations, earning them the nickname "jungle penguins." Their hunting habits vary unpredictably between fjords, with some groups fishing locally while others venture to the open ocean. Perhaps most baffling is their pre-molting migration. Each December, after breeding season ends and despite abundant local food, these exhausted penguins embark on a 5,000 to 7,000 kilometer journey to sub-Antarctic waters to feed before their annual feather replacement. Mattern likens it to "running a marathon while trying to gain weight." He suspects they're following ancient instincts shared with other crested penguin species. This story reminds us how much remains unknown even about creatures living in well-studied regions, and how patient observation can reveal nature's delightful refusal to follow our expectations.

Two sisters and one piano working wonders at dementia village

Researchers have found that the part of the brain responsible for musical memory is unusually resilient to dementia's progression, allowing people to recognize and respond to music even when other cognitive abilities fade. Music also releases dopamine, which can lift mood and ease anxiety. This story is a gentle reminder of how small acts of generosity can have outsized impact. Two children, armed only with their instruments and curiosity, stumbled upon a way to reach people that medicine alone cannot. In a world where dementia is now Australia's leading cause of death, their concerts offer something beyond research or treatment—they offer connection, joy, and a language everyone can understand.

Astronomers discover first green star ever identified; understand what explains the rare color

Astrônomos descobrem primeira estrela verde já identificada; entenda o que explica a cor rara

Astronomers have identified the first green star ever recorded — a white dwarf named WD J1330+6435 located about 280 light-years from Earth in the constellation Draco. This ghostly yellow-green hue represents a rare departure from the usual rules that govern stellar color, where temperature typically determines whether a star appears orange, white, or blue. The unusual color emerged from a cosmic recycling process. This white dwarf, the dense remnant core of a star that exhausted its nuclear fuel, absorbed rocky debris from planets that once orbited it. That debris enriched the star's outer layers with elements including calcium, lithium, and especially sodium. Under the extreme density of the white dwarf — roughly Earth-sized but containing half the Sun's mass — sodium atoms behave differently than they would in normal stars. Constantly bombarded by surrounding helium atoms, the sodium no longer absorbs just a narrow band of orange wavelengths as it does in ordinary stars or streetlights. Instead, it absorbs yellows, oranges, and some reds, leaving behind a distinctive greenish-yellow glow. This discovery emerged from a broader project examining how white dwarfs would actually appear to human eyes. The researchers liken the phenomenon to a painter's palette: remove certain colors from the mix, and the final result transforms. For those drawn to the unexpected corners of the cosmos, this green star offers a reminder that the universe still holds visual surprises — not from exotic distant galaxies, but from the quiet fate awaiting ordinary stars, transformed by physics into something entirely unfamiliar.

The wound that suggests Neandertals were more violent than previously believed

La herida que sugiere que los neandertales eran más violentos de lo que se creía

A 75,000-year-old Neandertal skeleton discovered in a cave in Iraqi Kurdistan bears a remarkably circular hole in its shoulder blade that researchers believe was caused by a wooden spear. The 15-millimeter wound appears to have been inflicted from the front, piercing through the chest, likely damaging a lung and major blood vessels before exiting through the left shoulder blade. This finding may represent the earliest evidence of lethal interpersonal violence using such a weapon in the prehistoric Middle East. Dr. Emma Pomeroy from Cambridge University notes that the wound's clean, circular shape doesn't occur naturally and closely matches damage patterns from experimental reconstructions using wooden spears. While the injury could theoretically result from a hunting accident or friendly fire, experts like Professor Chris Stringer from London's Natural History Museum consider an attack by a rival the most plausible explanation, given the wound's location and severity. Intriguingly, another Neandertal from the same cave system, dating to 50,000 years ago, also showed evidence of a partially healed spear wound. This discovery adds to a growing body of evidence suggesting that violence among early humans was more common than previously thought. Rather than depicting Neandertals as peaceful, these findings hint at a more complex social reality that included conflict alongside the cooperation and care for which they're also known. It's a quiet reminder that certain human behaviors may have deeper roots in our shared prehistoric past than we'd perhaps like to acknowledge.

VIDEO shows bleached coral recovering by touching a healthy one

VÍDEO mostra coral branqueado se recuperando ao tocar um saudável

Scientists at Stanford University have discovered that bleached corals can recover their color and health in just a few weeks through simple physical contact with healthy corals. The breakthrough came from an accidental observation in a laboratory aquarium, where two coral fragments from the same colony had toppled over and touched—one bleached white, the other vibrant and healthy. Within weeks, color began returning to the bleached coral at the point of contact. The mechanism is remarkably straightforward: when the tissues of the two fragments fuse at the contact point, microalgae from the healthy coral migrate into the bleached one. These algae, which give corals their color and provide energy through photosynthesis, then multiply up to ten times faster than rates observed in natural reef environments. Coral bleaching occurs when ocean temperatures rise too high, causing corals to expel their algae partners, lose their color, and begin to starve. The phenomenon has accelerated globally, with researchers warning that mass bleaching events could become annual by 2080. Brazil experienced its first major bleaching in 2019, with subsequent events in 2024 and ongoing risks. This discovery offers a glimmer of hope for reef restoration efforts worldwide. Coral reefs support a quarter of all marine life, protect coastlines from storms, and sustain fishing industries and tourism. The finding that "bleached corals may be dying, but they're not dead yet," as lead researcher Danny Collins noted, suggests new possibilities for intervention. What makes this story quietly remarkable is its simplicity—sometimes nature's solutions require nothing more than bringing the right neighbors together.
