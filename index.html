Two damselfly species return to restored woodland

A three-year woodland restoration project in Hertfordshire has brought unexpected visitors back to ancient forests: two species of damselfly not previously recorded at the site, along with a notable increase in butterfly populations. The Ancient Astonbury and Wilder Woodlands initiative, supported by a £162,698 National Lottery Heritage Fund grant, focused on reviving traditional land management practices across seven nature reserves in the region. The project centered on coppicing—an ancient tree management technique where trees are cut at the base to encourage new growth—and clearing overgrown woodland glades that hadn't been managed in roughly two decades. By opening up the forest canopy and allowing more sunlight to reach the woodland floor, conservationists created conditions for wildflowers to flourish. This, in turn, attracted pollinators and other insects. Beautiful Demoiselle and Willow Emerald damselflies were spotted at Astonbury Wood near Stevenage, while Silver-washed Fritillary butterflies appeared in much higher numbers than in previous years. Even during a dry summer in 2025, hornbeam trees showed strong regrowth after coppicing, demonstrating the resilience of well-managed woodlands. This story offers a quiet reminder that sometimes looking backward—to traditional practices and patient stewardship—can move ecosystems forward. As climate change brings hotter, drier summers to the region, the trust hopes to continue restoration work, particularly around ponds that risk drying out. It's a testament to how thoughtful intervention, rather than simply leaving nature alone, can help wildlife not just survive but return and thrive.

Scientists unearth remarkable volcanic origins of Lord Howe Island

Lord Howe Island, a World Heritage site rising dramatically from the Tasman Sea 600 kilometers off Australia's east coast, holds a geological secret beneath its surface. The island's towering basalt peaks are the visible remains of a 7-million-year-old shield volcano, but it's merely the tip of a vast underwater mountain range. Two parallel chains of mostly submerged extinct volcanoes stretch for 1,500 kilometers along Australia's eastern seaboard, their unusual proximity puzzling scientists for years. New research published in Gondwana Research offers an elegant explanation for this geological oddity. As the Australian tectonic plate has drifted northward over tens of millions of years, a deep Earth hotspot—essentially a blowtorch of super-heated rock rising from the planet's interior—has punched through the crust, creating volcanoes. Each volcano is eventually carried away by the plate's movement, forming chains like breadcrumbs marking the continent's journey. Lead author Ben Mather and his team used 3D numerical modeling to demonstrate that the mantle plume likely encountered an obstacle deep underground—a slab of ancient seafloor that had sunk hundreds of kilometers into the Earth. Rather than stopping, the plume split into two branches, creating both the Lord Howe and Tasmantid seamount chains from a single source. This story quietly rewrites our understanding of how the Earth's interior shapes the surface we see. It reminds us that even familiar landmarks like Lord Howe Island are chapter markers in a planetary story written over millions of years, and that the ground beneath our feet—or beneath the waves—remains full of surprises waiting to be decoded.

Brazil's Amazon rainforest has reached a remarkable environmental milestone, with deforestation dropping to its lowest level since monitoring began in 1988. According to data from Brazil's space research agency Inpe, just 3,335 square kilometers of forest were cleared in the twelve months through July—a 42 percent decrease from the previous year. President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva, who took office in 2023 with a pledge to end illegal deforestation by 2030, called the results proof that political commitment can curb forest loss and greenhouse gas emissions. This story matters because it offers a rare piece of encouraging news in the often-discouraging realm of global environmental challenges. The Amazon's role as a critical carbon sink and biodiversity reservoir makes every square kilometer preserved a quiet victory for the planet's ecological future.

Uncovering the secrets of New Zealand’s elusive ‘jungle penguins’

In the remote fjords of New Zealand's South Island, researchers are unraveling the mysteries of one of the world's most peculiar penguins. The Fiordland crested penguin, known by its Māori name tawaki, has proven so elusive that scientists describe it as a "ghost." These half-meter-tall birds with striking yellow eyebrows dig elaborate tunnel systems to escape capture, sometimes taking researchers days to retrieve tracking devices. Until 2014, almost nothing was known about them. The Tawaki Project, founded by Dr. Thomas Mattern and his wife Ursula Ellenberg, has mapped over 1,200 nests across nine fjords in twelve years, revealing a population roughly three times larger than previously thought. What makes these penguins truly unusual is their complete rejection of typical penguin behavior. While most penguins nest in coastal colonies, tawaki build solitary burrows deep in dense rainforest, sometimes at high elevations, earning them the nickname "jungle penguins." Their hunting habits vary unpredictably between fjords, with some groups fishing locally while others venture to the open ocean. Perhaps most baffling is their pre-molting migration. Each December, after breeding season ends and despite abundant local food, these exhausted penguins embark on a 5,000 to 7,000 kilometer journey to sub-Antarctic waters to feed before their annual feather replacement. Mattern likens it to "running a marathon while trying to gain weight." He suspects they're following ancient instincts shared with other crested penguin species. This story reminds us how much remains unknown even about creatures living in well-studied regions, and how patient observation can reveal nature's delightful refusal to follow our expectations.
