Ancient tree with strong smell wins Tree of the Year

A ginkgo biloba tree standing in the Geelong Botanic Gardens has been crowned Victorian Tree of the Year, capturing nearly a third of more than 4,400 public votes. Planted in 1859 from a seed reportedly delivered by a ship's captain, the tree predates iconic Australian events like the first Melbourne Cup and Ned Kelly's bushranger days. At 20 meters tall, it's believed to be the largest ginkgo in Victoria, possibly in all of Australia. Ginkgo biloba trees are often called living fossils, descendants of plants that flourished alongside dinosaurs during the Middle Jurassic period, some 160 million years ago. Once thought extinct in the wild, small populations endured in China, and ancient specimens still stand at temples across East Asia. The species gained symbolic meaning after surviving the atomic bombing of Hiroshima in 1945. The Geelong tree displays rare stalactite-like growths usually seen only on much older specimens, adding to its botanical intrigue. As a female tree, it produces fruit with a pungent aroma that divides opinion—some find it unremarkable, others find it off-putting. The National Trust of Australia has maintained a register of significant trees for over four decades, advocating for statutory protections similar to those afforded historic buildings. This story offers a quiet reminder of resilience and continuity: a single seed planted during the gold rush era has grown into a living connection between deep geological time, human history, and community memory, all while serving as a beloved backdrop for weddings and celebrations.

RN reconhece experiência de Paulo Freire em Angicos como patrimônio cultural imaterial

The Brazilian state of Rio Grande do Norte has officially recognized the "40 Hours of Angicos" as intangible cultural heritage, honoring a groundbreaking 1963 literacy experiment led by educator Paulo Freire. In the small town of Angicos, which had fewer than 10,000 residents at the time, Freire set out to accomplish something audacious: teach adults to read and write in just 40 hours. Of the 380 participants who began the course in January, around 300 completed it by early April. What made Freire's approach revolutionary was its foundation in the lived experiences of learners. Rather than relying on traditional primers with disconnected phrases, he used "generative words" drawn from students' daily realities—factory workers learned through words like "brick" and "cement," while farmers engaged with "sugarcane," "hoe," and "harvest." This method encouraged participants to decode and reconstruct language while simultaneously reflecting on their own lives and communities. The recognition now preserves the memories, oral histories, and pedagogical practices of those who took part, aiming to pass this legacy to future generations without mandating any specific teaching method in state schools. This story matters because it celebrates an educational innovation rooted in dignity and respect for learners' knowledge. Freire's work in Angicos was meant to expand statewide, addressing widespread illiteracy, but his exile following the 1964 military coup cut those plans short. Decades later, this formal recognition serves as a quiet acknowledgment of how teaching can honor people's experiences and how a single experiment in a small town can ripple through history, reshaping our understanding of what education can be.

Five new mussel species reveal how much biodiversity US rivers still conceal

For two decades, researchers waded through rivers across the southeastern United States, collecting what appeared to be familiar freshwater mussels. Visual inspection suggested nothing unusual, but DNA analysis revealed a hidden truth: five species that science had never formally recognized. The discovery, published in Zootaxa by teams from Shedd Aquarium, Arkansas State University, and the U.S. Geological Survey, underscores how much biodiversity remains undocumented even in well-studied waterways. The challenge lies in the mussels themselves. Shell shape varies dramatically depending on habitat—headwater individuals grow flat, while downstream relatives grow round—and males and females look different too. So the researchers relied on genetic sequencing of three genes and detailed analysis of more than 440 shell outlines. Each major river basin turned out to harbor its own distinct lineage. The newly described species carry names rooted in place and Indigenous language: the Mobile hickorynut from Alabama's Sipsey River (a Choctaw word meaning "swamp"), the Acolapissa hickorynut honoring the people who lived along the Pearl River, and the Big Thicket hickorynut clinging to scattered spots in Louisiana and Texas pine forests. Several species are rare, confined to single river systems, with one known only from a ten-mile stretch of Mississippi's Big Black River. This story matters because freshwater mussels are both essential and imperiled—they filter and clean river water, serving as quiet sentinels of ecosystem health, yet remain the most endangered group of animals in North America. The discovery reminds us that even familiar landscapes hold secrets, and that understanding what lives in our rivers is the first step toward protecting it.

237-million-year-old amphibian may have been the first to live only in the sea

Anfíbio de 237 milhões de anos pode ter sido o primeiro a viver só no mar

Scientists in China have discovered what may be the oldest amphibian to spend its entire life in the ocean. Two nearly complete skeletons of Gerrothorax maritimus, dating back 237 million years, were found in Guizhou Province in southern China. The find is remarkable because none of today's 8,000-plus amphibian species are fully marine—frogs, toads, and salamanders all struggle with saltwater due to their highly permeable skin, which causes them to lose body water in ocean environments. G. maritimus appears to have solved this problem through some clever adaptations. The creature had a flattened body covered in heavy bony armor, which researchers believe acted as a physical barrier against water loss. It also possessed gills and a specialized digestive system that may have helped expel excess salt and absorb seawater, much like modern marine fish. Chemical analysis of the bones revealed a signature matching ancient seawater from that period, and the fossils were found in rocks formed at least 30 kilometers from the nearest islands of the time. The animal likely spent much of its time on the ocean floor, using its wide mouth and strong throat structure to capture prey by suction—a strategy similar to some modern bottom-dwelling sharks. This story offers a fascinating glimpse into how ancient life experimented with different solutions to environmental challenges. While soft tissues weren't preserved—leaving questions about reproduction and detailed physiology unanswered—the discovery reminds us that evolution has explored pathways that no longer exist today, and that the boundaries we observe in modern nature aren't necessarily fixed across deep time.

Country diary: Our tiny island is awash with waxcaps | Tim Earl

A small island off the coast of the Isle of Man has revealed an unexpectedly rich treasure of rare fungi. When mycologist Nev Kilkenny and two companions made an impromptu autumn visit to the Calf of Man—a bird observatory island typically difficult to reach due to gales—they weren't watching for migrating birds. Instead, they were scanning the sheep-cropped fields for waxcaps, a family of colorful mushrooms with names as delightful as their appearance: parrot, butterscotch, snowy, and golden. The expedition proved remarkably fruitful. Over two days of careful surveying, the team identified eighteen waxcap species, effectively doubling the number previously recorded on the island. This work contributes to a growing map of Manx fungi that will inform government conservation decisions, a project that has gained momentum as the Isle of Man's fungal significance becomes clearer. The island now boasts four sites qualifying for top European conservation status for fungi—more than the entire Netherlands—and several areas are expected to receive special scientific protection specifically for their mushroom populations. Scientists video REM-like sleep in Australian jumping spider species

A tiny Australian jumping spider is offering scientists a remarkable window into the evolution of sleep across the animal kingdom. Researchers have documented that Portia fimbriata, a species found in northern Australia, experiences at least two distinct sleep stages—including one that closely resembles REM sleep in humans, complete with leg twitches, eye movements, and little arm jerks as the spider dozes suspended upside down in its web. The research team, led by behavioral ecologists from the University of Bonn, used careful video analysis to identify the sleep patterns. They observed a quiescent stage where the spiders groomed themselves and slowly drooped down, followed by the more active REM-like phase. To confirm the spiders were truly sleeping, the researchers tested their responsiveness to loud noises at different times. Just like humans roused from deep sleep, the spiders were slow to respond—or didn't respond at all—when disturbed during their REM-like state. One Australian expert described the study as "the most complete behavioral case for sleep in any arachnid." While it might seem surprising to compare spider sleep to our own, scientists note that similar behaviors have been observed across vastly different species, from dogs to reptiles. The fact that a creature separated from humans by 500 million years of evolution exhibits such familiar sleep patterns raises intriguing questions about how fundamental rest may be to life itself. This story is worth your time because it gently expands our understanding of what we share with even the smallest, most alien-seeming creatures—and reminds us that the need for a good night's sleep might be more universal than we ever imagined.

Jean Phillips-Martinsson obituary

When Jean Phillips-Martinsson stumbled upon a 1984 article about Prader-Willi syndrome in the Lancet, fourteen years of confusion suddenly made sense. Her son Anders, who had been floppy as a baby, struggled with behavioral issues, and was constantly hungry, finally had a diagnosis for the rare genetic condition that affects roughly one in 15,000 to 25,000 people. What began with a mother's determination to understand her son's condition grew into a global movement that would transform care for PWS families worldwide. Phillips-Martinsson's first instinct was to connect with other Swedish families facing the same isolation. After an emotional first meeting at her Stockholm home in 1986, she quickly recognized that the condition's rarity demanded international collaboration. Drawing on her skills in public relations, translation, and multiple languages, she networked tirelessly with emerging PWS organizations and researchers. In 1989, she partnered with American geneticist Dr. Suzanne Cassidy to organize a landmark 1991 conference in the Netherlands, securing support from organizations including the WHO and NATO. The gathering of 200 delegates from 22 countries voted to establish the International Prader-Willi Syndrome Organisation, with Phillips-Martinsson as its founding president. Under her leadership through 1998 and beyond as honorary president, IPWSO became a vital bridge between scientific research and the families who needed it most. She championed parent voices at medical conferences, established regular newsletters, and worked to reduce global inequality by lobbying for free genetic testing in underserved countries. This story reminds us how one person's search for answers can build networks of hope and practical support that reach around the world, turning isolation into community.

What photonics is and how it can make AI data centers consume less water and energy

Qué es la fotónica y cómo puede lograr que los centros de datos de la IA consuman menos agua y energía

As artificial intelligence drives unprecedented growth in data centers, industry leaders are looking to replace copper wiring with light-based photonics technology. Chris Sharp of Digital Realty suggests we're reaching "the end of the copper era" — not because supplies are running low, but because a better alternative is emerging. A typical 100-megawatt data center uses around 400 tons of copper, with roughly 20 tons dedicated solely to network cabling that connects servers and processing units. The solution lies in photonics: transmitting data as photons of light through fiber optic cables rather than as electrons through copper wire. While fiber optics have carried long-distance communications for decades, researchers are now working to bring this technology inside data centers themselves, sometimes integrating optical components directly onto computer chips. The key advantage is thermal: light generates far less heat than electricity, meaning data centers would require significantly less energy for cooling — a major contributor to their substantial environmental footprint. Photonics also allows multiple data streams to travel through a single channel, increasing capacity. After years of laboratory development, the technology appears ready for widespread adoption, bolstered by support from AI chip giant Nvidia. Yet challenges remain in manufacturing and cost reduction, as photonics brings together different engineering traditions and supply chains scattered across the globe.
