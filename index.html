The tilcayo, a new species of wild cat discovered in Bolivia

Le tilcayo, une nouvelle espèce de félin sauvage découverte en Bolivie

In the mountainous Los Yungas region of Bolivia, about 90 kilometers northeast of La Paz, scientists have identified a new species of wild cat—the first discovered anywhere in the world in over a century. The leopardus tilcayo, named after the local term "tilcayo" used by residents, is a small feline with spotted fur measuring roughly 45 centimeters in length. The last time a new wild cat species was described was in 1923, when the Uruguayan pampas cat was identified, making this discovery particularly significant for the scientific community. The tilcayo's journey to recognition began in an unexpected way. One male was adopted by a family who mistook him for an ordinary domestic cat, but his unusual behavior prompted them to surrender him to an animal conservation refuge. A female specimen was rescued after being attacked in a village and later released back into the wild. Both animals underwent extensive genetic analysis, and the research findings were published in the scientific journal Current Biology. According to Paola Nogales, a National Geographic explorer involved in the study, the tilcayo is slightly smaller than a house cat and has distinctive rounded ears along with its spotted coat. This discovery serves as a reminder that even in our well-mapped world, nature still holds surprises waiting to be found. Unfortunately, the species has so far been detected only in Bolivia and is already considered threatened with extinction, underscoring the urgency of conservation efforts for this newly recognized member of the cat family.

Whales in mourning: why a humpback mother stayed beside her stillborn calf

Baleias de luto: por que uma mãe jubarte permaneceu ao lado de seu filhote natimorto

Off the coast of Australia's Gold Coast, researchers documented a humpback whale mother spending hours beside her stillborn calf resting on the ocean floor. The mother repeatedly returned to touch the newborn gently with her head and pectoral fin, resting eye-to-eye in what scientists believe may be the first recorded case of a humpback caring for a stillborn calf. This rare observation opens a window into one of the least understood aspects of whale behavior: how these highly social creatures respond to death. Humpback whales are known for remarkable acts of care beyond their own species. They defend their young from orcas, stay beside injured or entangled companions, and have even intervened when orcas attack seals or gray whale calves. This protective behavior, called epimeletic behavior, is common among cetaceans and demonstrates powerful social bonds. While orcas have been observed carrying dead calves for days or even weeks—one mother famously carried her calf for seventeen days across 1,500 kilometers—such behavior has rarely been documented in baleen whales like humpbacks, partly because their stillborn young typically sink quickly. This poignant encounter raises profound questions about grief in highly social animals. Scientists cannot directly measure the emotional states of whales, but behaviors suggest that maternal bonds persist even after death. Whether whales truly experience grief as humans understand it remains unknown, yet their instincts and hormonal responses drive them to stay with lost offspring. The story is a quiet reminder of the depth of connection in the animal world—a glimpse of devotion that transcends species and invites us to reconsider what we think we know about consciousness, loss, and love in nature.

From wanting to be seen as white to finding his way back to te reo Māori

Nathan Grey grew up knowing he was Māori but spent his youth actively distancing himself from that identity. Raised in a generation removed from te reo Māori—the Māori language—due to historical suppression, he was taught that success meant assimilation. In high school, he surrounded himself with Pākehā (New Zealanders of European descent) and proudly accepted the nickname "Dalmatian," seeing it as confirmation that despite being brown, people perceived him as white. That carefully constructed identity would eventually give way to something deeper and more authentic. The turning point came when Grey's older brother completed an immersive te reo Māori course. Feeling a pull toward his ancestral language, Grey enrolled at Te Wānanga Takiura and began what he describes as one of the hardest journeys of his life. Learning the language meant confronting twenty years of suppressed feelings—what he calls mamae, or pain. The process was frightening, but the wānanga taught him to lean on others going through similar reckonings. He came to understand that the shame he carried wasn't his to bear, that not knowing his language and culture was the result of historical forces, not personal failing. Today, Grey teaches te reo Māori at Selwyn Community Education, where his students range from fifth-generation Pākehā to immigrants from Argentina, all seeking to respectfully engage with the language of the land they call home. He believes that sharing te reo widely—without making it intimidating—creates greater understanding across communities. For Grey, the journey back to language was also a journey back to himself, and he hopes his students leave knowing they're capable of the same transformation, supported every step of the way.

Annie Dillard brought poetry into prose, pioneering a mystical form of creative nonfiction that critics both admired and misunderstood

Annie Dillard, who died in September 2026 at age 81, is remembered as a pioneer of creative nonfiction—but the true distinctiveness of her work may lie in something critics often overlooked: her deep grounding in poetry. Dillard won the Pulitzer Prize at just 28 for "Pilgrim at Tinker Creek," a book that set the template for her career—close attention to nature, theological curiosity, and language that shimmered with poetic intensity. While she drew on transcendentalists like Thoreau and openly acknowledged fiction's influence, it was poetry that gave her prose its arresting, almost mystical quality. Dillard's writing blurred the line between genres in ways that feel both deliberate and organic. Her essay "The Death of a Moth" exemplifies this: a burning insect becomes a "flame-faced virgin gone to God" in prose dense with image and sound, while Dillard reads French poet Arthur Rimbaud by its light. She didn't just admire poetry—she used it as fuel. In "The Writing Life," she describes reading Conrad Aiken's verses aloud for their "pure sound," repeating Wallace Stevens' lines like a mantra, letting poetry's rhythms seep into her sentences. This wasn't ornament; it was method. Dillard's legacy matters because she showed that nonfiction need not choose between accuracy and beauty, between observation and transcendence. Her work reminds us that genre boundaries are permeable, that the tools of poetry—heightened language, arresting imagery, emotional intensity—can deepen our encounter with the real world. In an age of information overload, her approach feels quietly radical: slowing down, paying attention, and finding the sacred in a burning moth.

What the codex of pre-Hispanic history shows as it is exhibited for the first time in Mexico after nearly two centuries in France

Qué muestra el códice de la historia prehispánica que se exhibe por primera vez en México tras estar casi dos siglos en Francia

A precious illustrated manuscript chronicling the Mexica people's history has returned to Mexico for the first time in nearly two centuries. The Codex Azcatitlan, housed in France's National Library since the 1840s, is now on display at Mexico City's renowned National Museum of Anthropology following a cultural exchange agreement between presidents Claudia Sheinbaum and Emmanuel Macron. In return, Mexico sent the Codex Boturini to Paris. The codex tells a sweeping story through vivid pictographs: the Mexica journey from the mythical city of Aztlán, their decades-long migration seeking a promised land, and the founding of Tenochtitlan in 1325 after witnessing the divine sign of an eagle devouring a serpent on an island in Lake Texcoco—an image that adorns Mexico's flag today. Created on European paper by tlacuilos (indigenous painters) working under Spanish commission, the manuscript blends pre-Hispanic pictographic techniques with European artistic influences. Its 25 folios span roughly 500 years, documenting rulers, wars, celebrations, and the early colonial period, though six pages are mysteriously missing. This homecoming offers more than scholarly opportunity. While experts will study the codex for new insights into Mesoamerican history, the public can appreciate the artistic richness of these documents—part of a collection of 15 to 17 authentic pre-Hispanic codices and some 500 colonial-era manuscripts created with traditional techniques. The exhibition runs through year's end, offering a rare window into how indigenous peoples recorded their stories and a chance to see cultural treasures that bridge ancient tradition and colonial transformation.
