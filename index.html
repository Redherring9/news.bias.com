Ancient stories in the stars revealed at Indigenous-owned observatory

In the remote Indigenous community of Koonibba in South Australia's far west, ancient star stories passed down through generations are finding new expression. The community has opened the Koonibba Observatory, a stargazing facility built in partnership with rocket launch company Southern Launch, allowing visitors to learn about Indigenous astronomy while community members train to operate a sophisticated deep-sky telescope. For chairperson Geraldine Ware, who grew up hearing Elders share stories of the constellations, the observatory represents both cultural continuity and economic possibility for the roughly 140-person community. The facility, which cost over one million dollars to build, sits near the Koonibba Test Range where Southern Launch has conducted commercial rocket launches since 2020. More than a dozen community members have signed up for telescope operation training, with hopes of developing tourism around both rocket viewing and traditional astronomical knowledge. Yet the project has sparked thoughtful questions about authenticity. International Dark Sky Park status for Fiordland National Park

Fiordland National Park in New Zealand has earned recognition as the largest International Dark Sky Park in the Southern Hemisphere, spanning an impressive 12,607 square kilometers. The global accreditation from DarkSky International celebrates not only the park's exceptional natural darkness but also the region's commitment to preserving it, making Fiordland the world's third-largest international dark sky park and Southland's second such designation after Stewart Island received sanctuary status in 2019. The achievement reflects years of careful collaboration involving the Department of Conservation, regional partners, and local communities. The work has included monitoring sky quality, upgrading public lighting to reduce light pollution, and community education efforts. Beyond the technical aspects, the accreditation acknowledges the cultural significance of dark skies in Māori knowledge systems, including navigation, seasonal cycles, and observances like Matariki. Conservation officials note that protecting natural darkness also benefits native wildlife, as many species depend on undisturbed light cycles for their survival and behavior. This milestone represents a meaningful step toward New Zealand becoming a "Dark Sky Nation," a vision championed by DarkSky International president Nalayini Brito-Davies, who experienced Fiordland's night skies firsthand while hiking the Milford Track. The story matters because it illustrates how environmental protection extends beyond daytime landscapes to include something increasingly rare in our modern world: true darkness and the celestial wonder it reveals, inviting visitors and locals alike to rediscover the night sky while safeguarding ecosystems that have evolved under starlight for millennia.

Bug warfare at century-old Sydney rose garden to help deter pests

In Sydney's Centennial Park, a century-old rose garden is being protected by an unusual army: thousands of ladybirds and parasitic wasps deployed to combat aphid infestations. The historic rosarium, planted around 1908 and now home to 33 rose varieties and roughly 800 plants, faces an annual challenge when aphids swarm the new shoots after winter pruning. Left unchecked, these pests would distort the roses, spread viruses, and encourage unsightly black mould. The solution involves releasing five batches of predatory insects between September and early November. While ladybirds simply eat the aphids, the parasitic wasps employ a more dramatic strategy: laying eggs inside the aphids' bodies, which hatch and consume their hosts from within. The insects arrive by mail from South Australia in polystyrene coolers, packed with shredded newspaper like precious cargo. Senior horticulturalist Tristan Harman oversees the operation, which costs about $1,300 annually—comparable to chemical treatments but far gentler on the garden's ecosystem, particularly its bee population. What makes this story quietly remarkable is how rapidly biological pest control has shifted from fringe practice to mainstream gardening. Harman notes that when he studied horticulture three decades ago, using predatory insects seemed like "black magic." Today, it represents a thoughtful approach to stewardship—one that protects not just the roses but the entire community of creatures that share the garden. It's a reminder that sometimes the most effective solutions work with nature rather than against it, turning what once seemed mystical into simple common sense.

Genetic study describes two new species of Amazonian howler monkeys

A comprehensive genetic analysis of howler monkeys has unveiled two new species in the Amazon rainforest, reshaping scientists' understanding of primate diversity in the region. Researchers formally described the Pantepui red howler monkey in northwestern Amazonia and the Mato Grosso red howler monkey in the southern Brazilian Amazon, following more than a decade of fieldwork and the analysis of over 2 terabytes of genomic data. The discovery emerged when biologist Romina Batista noticed distinct genetic lineages on opposite sides of Brazil's Branco River, differences reinforced by physical characteristics like the more yellowish fur of the Pantepui species. Historical museum specimens proved invaluable, allowing researchers to revisit older samples with modern genomic tools and identify distinguishing features that had been overlooked. The study also recommends elevating the howler monkey population in Trinidad and Tobago to full species status, highlighting how genomic approaches can reveal evolutionary complexity that earlier methods missed. These findings carry urgent conservation implications. The Mato Grosso red howler monkey lives in Brazil's arc of deforestation, where agricultural expansion has severely fragmented its habitat, potentially qualifying it as critically endangered. Meanwhile, a proposed hydroelectric dam on the Branco River threatens the Pantepui species and its relatives. Conservation efforts are already underway, including canopy bridges that reconnect isolated forest patches—structures that researchers have documented the monkeys successfully using. This story matters because it demonstrates how modern science continues to reveal hidden diversity in well-studied regions, and how each discovery carries immediate real-world stakes for protecting species we're only just learning exist.

Funding shortages threaten search for critically endangered ‘Asian unicorn’

In the remote forests of Laos, a team of trackers and sniffer dogs is searching for one of the world's most elusive animals—the saola, a critically endangered bovid often called the "Asian unicorn." First described by scientists only in 1992, this reclusive creature hasn't been officially recorded since a single camera-trap photograph in 2013. Fewer than fifty individuals are believed to remain in the wild, and none exist in captivity. The species, which has remained largely unchanged for 15 million years, now faces an existential threat not just from wire snares set for other animals, but from something more mundane: a lack of funding. The Saola Foundation launched an ambitious search effort in 2025, deploying expert trackers and rapid DNA testing across 300 square kilometers of Bolikhamxay province. But severe funding shortfalls have forced the operation into a precarious month-to-month existence. Emergency donations from zoos secured fieldwork through October, yet long-term support remains uncertain. The conservation community itself is divided—some believe the saola is already extinct after years without detection, while others argue that the animal's extraordinarily low population density (potentially one per 300 square kilometers) makes it nearly impossible to find, not necessarily gone. This story matters because it captures a quiet race against time in one of Earth's biodiversity hotspots, where the Annamite Mountains harbor more than 3% of the world's land vertebrates on less than 0.2% of its surface. The search represents perhaps the last chance to locate saola for potential captive breeding—and without it, a species that survived 15 million years may vanish on our watch, not with drama, but in silence.

Investigation: Fungus may have killed Atlantic Forest amphibians in the 1970s and ’80s

Scientists believe they've solved a decades-old mystery behind the dramatic die-off of frogs in Brazil's Atlantic Forest during the 1970s and '80s. For years, researchers debated what caused the disappearance, with theories ranging from extreme weather events to acid rain. Now, a team analyzing preserved museum specimens has found compelling evidence pointing to the chytrid fungus—a deadly pathogen that attacks amphibian skin and has devastated frog populations worldwide. The breakthrough came from examining 370 frog specimens collected at Brazil's Boracéia research station before and after the decline. Using DNA testing, researchers discovered no trace of the Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis fungus in samples from 1978, but found it widespread in specimens from 1983 onward—suggesting a rapid invasion during the gap years when no frogs were collected. The pathogen, which wasn't scientifically described until 1999, is now known to have emerged in Asia in the 1970s and has since contributed to declines in more than 500 amphibian species globally. The fungus disrupts critical skin functions, affecting oxygen intake and mineral regulation, often proving fatal. This investigation offers more than just answers to a historical puzzle. It demonstrates how carefully preserved museum collections serve as biological time capsules, enabling scientists to investigate disease outbreaks long after they occur. The methods developed could become a toolkit for solving similar wildlife mysteries and help researchers anticipate future disease patterns. It's a reminder that patience, preservation, and scientific curiosity can illuminate the past in ways that protect the future—turning dusty specimens into keys for understanding emerging threats to biodiversity.
