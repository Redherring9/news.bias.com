VIDEO: Caracara caught in 'intimate moment' of feather care

A wildlife guide in Brazil's Pantanal wetlands captured an unexpectedly tender moment: a caracara bird taking a leisurely bath. Cibele Manfredini filmed the scene in slow motion, revealing details invisible to the naked eye as the raptor splashed, dunked its head, and shook droplets from its feathers. The footage offers a rare glimpse into the private routine of a bird more commonly associated with fierce hunting than self-care. The caracara is known throughout Brazil as an opportunistic predator, immortalized in song as a bird that "catches, kills, and eats" with courage surpassing humans. Yet this video shows a different side: the bird methodically spreading water through its plumage in the heat, seeking relief and hygiene. According to ornithologist Fernando Igor de Godoy, the bath serves multiple purposes—cleaning off bacteria from scavenged carcasses, preventing parasites, and regulating body temperature in the sweltering climate. After bathing, caracaras typically preen their feathers with an oily secretion from a gland near their tail, completing their grooming ritual. This story reminds us that even the most formidable creatures have quiet, intimate moments of care. The slow-motion perspective transforms an ordinary behavior into something contemplative, inviting viewers to notice the small rituals that sustain wild lives. It's a gentle counter-narrative to the caracara's fearsome reputation, and a testament to the value of patient observation in revealing the full complexity of the natural world.

Could humans ever communicate with whales? - podcast

Scientists have been captivated by whale vocalizations since the first recording of whale song in 1949, and now advancing technology is bringing researchers closer to understanding the complex communication systems of these marine mammals. A recent podcast explores how scientists are decoding the distinctive patterns in the wails, clicks, grunts, and squeals that different whale species use to communicate with one another. Journalist Emma Bryce, who spent months investigating human efforts to understand whale song, discusses how beluga whales' intricate vocalizations are being studied and interpreted. The research draws on decades of recordings and observations, combining traditional field work with modern computational tools to identify patterns and potential meanings in whale communication. Organizations like the Raincoast Conservation Foundation and Project CETI are at the forefront of this work, capturing footage and audio that reveal the richness of cetacean language. What makes this research particularly meaningful goes beyond satisfying human curiosity. Understanding how whales communicate could prove essential to protecting them from human-caused threats like ocean noise pollution, ship strikes, and habitat disruption. By learning to decode their language, scientists hope to better understand whale behavior, social structures, and needs—knowledge that could inform more effective conservation strategies. The effort also holds a mirror up to ourselves, offering insights into the nature of intelligence, communication, and our relationship with the natural world. It's a reminder that the ocean holds mysteries that remain largely undeciphered, and that listening carefully might be one of the most important things we can do.

'Without technique and persistence, I would never have gotten where I am': biologist transforms nature into art

A biologist in Brazil has found an unexpected way to celebrate the natural world: by transforming her scientific training into intricate works of art. Maíra Gonzales Mezzacappa, who graduated from the University of São Paulo in 2012, chose to pursue scientific illustration rather than traditional research, blending her deep knowledge of biodiversity with artistic expression. Her work spans multiple mediums—from pencil and watercolor on paper to painted wooden pieces and, most recently, miniature sculptures made from cold porcelain. What makes Mezzacappa's art distinctive is its scientific rigor. She maintains taxonomic accuracy in her depictions of birds and other wildlife, ensuring each piece is both beautiful and faithful to the species it represents. Her painted wooden magnets and panels have become particularly popular, produced during daylight hours so she can perceive colors with precision. The artist's latest venture into cold porcelain began playfully, when her nine-year-old niece invited her to play with modeling clay. The resulting miniature bird was so well-received on social media that Mezzacappa expanded into three-dimensional work, creating tiny avian sculptures protected in glass containers. This story quietly illustrates how passion can bridge seemingly separate worlds. Mezzacappa's journey reminds us that science and art need not be competing paths—they can inform and enrich each other. Her work offers viewers both aesthetic pleasure and an invitation to look more closely at the biodiversity around them, making scientific observation accessible through beauty. It's a gentle testament to persistence and technique, and to finding creative ways to share what we love with others.

Marajoara designs: handicraft from Pará becomes footwear collection and debuts at Milan Fashion Week

Ancient Amazonian artistry is making its way to one of fashion's most prestigious stages. A new footwear collection featuring traditional Marajoara designs—geometric patterns created by indigenous peoples who inhabited Brazil's Marajó Island before colonization—will debut at Milan Fashion Week in September 2026. The project brings together artisans from the Amazon, shoemakers, and social enterprises in a collaboration that bridges centuries of cultural heritage with contemporary design. The initiative connects diverse Brazilian communities through creative economy and social impact. Artisans from Marajó Island contribute ancestral knowledge of the intricate patterns originally found on archaeological ceramics. In São Paulo, the Costurando Sonhos Brasil Institute, led by Marajoara native Suéli Feio, involves women from peripheral communities in professional training and production. The G10 Favelas coalition supports the project's expansion, linking grassroots entrepreneurship with global markets. Beyond commerce, the choice of Milan carries poetic significance: Italian priest Giovanni Gallo founded the Marajó Museum and devoted his life to preserving the island's archaeological treasures, making the collection's Italian debut a symbolic homecoming. This story quietly demonstrates how cultural preservation and economic opportunity can reinforce rather than contradict each other. It's worth attention not for spectacle, but for its thoughtful model of collaboration—one that honors indigenous heritage while creating pathways for contemporary artisans, connects distant geographies through shared purpose, and positions traditional craft as innovation rather than nostalgia.

Kākāpō population soars to 325

New Zealand's kākāpō recovery program has reached a remarkable milestone, with the critically endangered night parrot's population jumping from 235 to 325 birds following the most successful breeding season on record. Ninety chicks survived to independence this year—a number that once took sixteen years to achieve in the program's early days. When conservation efforts began in 1995, only 51 kākāpō remained in the world. This season's success reflects both favorable natural conditions and an important philosophical shift in conservation strategy. On two Fiordland breeding islands, an abundant rimu fruit crop allowed mother kākāpō to raise their chicks without human intervention, while birds on Codfish Island still required supplementary feeding when their fruit crop failed to ripen. Notably, only two chicks needed hand-rearing this year, compared to extensive intervention in previous seasons. Conservation managers are deliberately stepping back from intensive management, relying instead on remote monitoring technology—each bird wears a smart transmitter—to determine when intervention is truly necessary. The team has even decided not to name all the new chicks, symbolizing a move toward allowing kākāpō to exist in a more natural state. This story offers a refreshing perspective on conservation in an age often dominated by extinction narratives. It demonstrates how patience, adaptive management, and technological innovation can work together to bring a species back from the brink, while also showing the wisdom in knowing when to let nature take the lead.

Country diary: A wasps’ nest in our shed is a work of art, a family saga | Mark Cocker

When a naturalist discovered a tiny paper lantern hanging in his garden shed in early June, he had no idea it would grow into something extraordinary. What began as a modest wasps' nest the size of a lampshade eventually expanded to the size of a football, forcing him and his family to abandon the shed for ten weeks while thousands of wasps worked tirelessly at their construction project. The finished nest reveals itself as an intricate work of natural architecture. Its exterior displays bands of paper pulp in shades ranging from off-white to cedar red, each color representing a different wood source—at least nine in total. The writer calculates that every five centimeters of wall contains thirty-six separate bands, each requiring countless individual journeys by worker wasps. Inside this outer shell lie multiple layers and suspended tiers of brood cells where as many as 25,000 wasps may have been raised over the colony's lifetime. The social structure itself is remarkable: a single queen chemically controlled her daughters, suppressing their own reproductive instincts so they would serve the colony. The workers fed larvae with insects from the garden, while the larvae reciprocated by feeding adults with sugary saliva. This story offers a quiet invitation to reconsider creatures often viewed as nuisances. The wasps' nest represents simultaneously a family history, an architectural marvel, and a microcosm of the garden's ecological economy—all built with patience and precision over a single summer. It's a reminder that extraordinary craftsmanship and complex societies exist right in our backyards, if we're willing to look closely and wait.

Kākāpō rising: 90 chicks swell population of the world’s heaviest parrot

New Zealand's kākāpō, the world's heaviest parrot, has reached a population milestone not seen in three-quarters of a century. Thanks to a bumper breeding season, ninety chicks have joined the ranks of these critically endangered birds, bringing the total to 325—the highest number since intensive recovery efforts began in 1995 with just 51 individuals. The breeding boom was triggered by a mass fruiting of rimu trees, whose berries provide essential nutrition for kākāpō reproduction. These flightless, nocturnal parrots breed only when rimu harvests are plentiful, which happens every two to four years, making population growth painstakingly slow. This year's success followed a four-year wait and produced as many chicks as it took sixteen years to hatch in the program's early days. All three breeding populations live on predator-free islands, safe from the cats and stoats that nearly drove the species to extinction by the 1900s. The recovery team now looks ahead to the next potential breeding season in 2028, preparing for what they hope will be continued growth. What makes this story quietly remarkable is not just the numbers, but the peculiar charm of the birds themselves. Weighing up to four kilograms and living sixty to eighty years, kākāpō have captivated global audiences—over 100,000 people watched a livestream of a mother tending her nest. Males court females by producing deep, booming calls from chest air sacs while sitting in ground bowls, a sound that travels up to five kilometers and resembles a plucked cello string. It's a reminder that conservation can succeed with patience, and that some of nature's most endearing oddities are worth the decades-long effort to save.

What it's really like to live with obsessive-compulsive disorder

Matthew Wright appeared to be thriving—a devoted father, husband, and senior firefighter in Sydney. But beneath the surface, obsessive-compulsive disorder was quietly unraveling his daily life, trapping him in exhausting rituals that consumed more than five hours each day. His journey offers a candid look at how OCD can intensify after trauma and why reaching out for help became a turning point. Matthew's symptoms first emerged in 2020 after a near-miss while driving, leaving him haunted by intrusive thoughts that every pothole might be a body he'd missed. The compulsions escalated: he couldn't drive without a dashcam, then felt compelled to review every minute of footage. In 2023, the stillbirth of his daughter Mia deepened his grief, and when his beloved dog Archie fell ill months later, Matthew's fears crystallized around protecting those he loved. His OCD eventually focused on his newborn daughter Willow, driving him to wash his hands for hours and shower multiple times before noon, all in an effort to shield her from invisible dangers. What makes Matthew's story quietly powerful is his willingness to name the shame and isolation that often accompany OCD—and his decision to seek specialized help at St Vincent's Hospital. His therapist worked at his pace, crafting goals that gave him a vision for reclaiming his life. For anyone who has felt "broken" by invisible struggles, Matthew's account is a reminder that reaching out, even when it feels impossible, can open a path toward healing and community.

Growing Indigenous food trees in East Africa fights malnutrition and biodiversity loss

In East Africa, a beloved fruit is disappearing from local markets. The masuku plum, a native tree whose sweet fruits sell out almost instantly in Malawi, has become increasingly scarce due to widespread deforestation. This scarcity reflects a broader problem: while tree-planting programs proliferate across the region, they typically favor fast-growing, non-native timber species over food-bearing Indigenous trees. The result is a double loss—communities face both worsening malnutrition and declining biodiversity. Researcher Emilie Vansant discovered this gap during her doctoral work, when she found that Malawian women who supplemented their diets with food from native trees had significantly higher levels of essential nutrients like folate, zinc, and vitamin A. These same trees also support richer ecosystems for native plants and animals. Yet when Vansant spoke with farmers about planting more Indigenous food trees, they reported lacking basic information—the most recent planting guide for the region dated back to 1993. In response, she collaborated with Malawian foresters, botanists, and agroecologists to create an illustrated guide covering species like the African custard-apple and the marula, many of which provide multiple edible or medicinal products. The team then hosted workshops with forty landscape restoration workers to identify barriers to integrating these trees into reforestation efforts. This story matters because it demonstrates how ecological restoration and human nutrition can support each other. By looking to what grew in these landscapes before—and what local communities already love to eat—conservation work can nourish both people and ecosystems simultaneously.
