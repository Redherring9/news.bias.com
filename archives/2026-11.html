Honest obits: 'Rolling joints, reading dirty magazines, fishing off a pier'

Mary McGreevy has found an unexpected way to celebrate humanity's beautiful messiness. Two years ago, she launched a TikTok account called Tips from Dead People, where she shares obituaries that reject the polished, resume-style tributes in favor of something more honest and deeply human. These aren't the sanitized summaries of achievements and awards—they're vivid portraits of people in all their contradictory glory. The obituaries McGreevy highlights capture life's delightful paradoxes. There's Renee, who couldn't cook or clean but excelled at "rolling joints, reading dirty magazines, fishing off a pier." There's Trish, remembered through a loving catalog of specifics: her adoration of Vanity Fair and cold potatoes, her hatred of hard butter and gray roots. And Kenny, a self-proclaimed "redneck SOB" who "took the idea of being hard to love as a personal challenge" yet remained deeply loved by his family despite—or perhaps because of—his flaws. McGreevy even wrote one for her own mother, noting her love of collecting cookbooks and hatred of cooking. What makes these obituaries powerful is their refusal to airbrush reality. They understand that grief doesn't call for a LinkedIn profile at 3 a.m.—it calls for the truth of who someone really was. These portraits, oozing with affection despite their unflinching honesty, suggest that being remembered authentically might matter more than being remembered perfectly. In celebrating the Renees, Kennys, and Trishes of the world, McGreevy offers a quietly radical idea: that our contradictions and complications aren't flaws to hide, but the very things that make us unforgettable.

The unexpected neighbourhood pest terrorising this pensioner

In the suburb of Bluewater on Townsville's outskirts, pensioner Mal Richards faces an unusual daily challenge: Eric, a friendly but persistent female emu who has claimed his property as her territory. For nine months, the beady-eyed bird has been waiting at his driveway each morning, following him around his garden, honking throughout the day, and leaving unwelcome deposits on his property. Despite Mr Richards securing his fence, Eric always finds a way through, and the retiree worries about the bird's powerful kick. Eric appears to be a former pet emu, which explains her unusually sociable behavior with humans. While Mr Richards has contacted multiple government departments seeking help, authorities have deemed Eric's behavior non-threatening and advised residents simply to give her space and avoid feeding her. Local laws permit keeping emus on larger properties, provided they're properly enclosed—though Eric's owner remains somewhat mysterious in the reporting. The wider community has embraced Eric as a beloved local mascot, with the neighborhood Facebook page filled with sighting posts and warnings for drivers. Wildlife educator Jackie Hasling calls Eric "a little legend," though she acknowledges the daily frustrations of unwanted emu companionship. This story captures the sometimes-awkward reality of living alongside wildlife in regional Australia, where the line between wild, domestic, and suburban can blur in unexpected ways. It's a gentle reminder that not all neighborhood disputes involve noisy parties or boundary fences—sometimes they involve a determined flightless bird with a talent for fence-hopping and an apparent fondness for one particular pensioner's morning routine.

Losing our baby shaped us in ways we never expected

Robert Yeomans and Heidy Perez Perez met through a language-learning app in Adelaide, where Robert was studying Spanish and Heidy was an international student. After an initial meeting that didn't quite click, they reconnected a year later and built a life together, marrying in 2021. Just months after their wedding, they discovered they were expecting their first child, Thiago, conceived on Valentine's Day—a baby born of their love. At 28 weeks, doctors discovered Thiago wasn't growing properly due to placental issues. On their wedding anniversary, Heidy woke in pain, and at the hospital they received devastating news: Thiago's heartbeat could no longer be detected. Heidy gave birth to him later that day via C-section. The loss tested their relationship profoundly, as they each grieved differently while trying to support one another through unimaginable pain. They learned that loving someone through grief doesn't always mean having the right words—sometimes it simply means being present, listening, and holding space for each other's sorrow. This is a story about how love adapts and deepens through tragedy. Robert and Heidy describe not moving past their grief, but growing around it—becoming more compassionate, understanding, and aware of how precious their time together is. Thiago remains part of their family story, carried forward in how they love and see the world, even as they've welcomed their daughter Sienna. It's a quietly powerful reminder that resilience in relationships isn't about strength without pain, but about choosing to hold onto each other through it.
