Rare ant nest stolen from last place in England where species survives

A colony of narrow-headed ants—possibly containing up to 5,000 individuals and several breeding queens—has been stolen from a nature reserve in Devon, the only place in England where this rare species still survives. Conservation researchers discovered the theft in mid-October when they found a suspicious hole where a known nest had been, along with clear evidence of digging. The loss represents a significant setback for a species that has declined dramatically over the past century due to habitat loss from agriculture and development. Narrow-headed ants, named for their distinctively "pinched" heads, were once common across southern heathlands but are now classified as nationally rare and near threatened. Each colony develops slowly, and the queens can live for up to 25 years, meaning a mature nest represents decades of growth that cannot easily be replaced. Conservationists believe the thief likely had specialized knowledge, as ant nests are not obvious to casual observers. The motive remains unclear, though possibilities include a misguided rewilding attempt or collection by an insect enthusiast. Devon Wildlife Trust emphasized that properly relocating ants requires considerable expertise and careful preparation—work they had been planning themselves as part of long-term recovery efforts. This story quietly underscores how even the smallest creatures face pressures from human activity, and how fragile the last threads of biodiversity can be. It's a reminder that conservation work often unfolds over years and generations, and that setbacks—whether from development or theft—can undo careful stewardship in moments. The loss of a single nest might seem minor, but for England's last population of narrow-headed ants, it's a meaningful blow to survival.

Marchant takes listeners on an intellectual journey across disciplines, weaving together insights from philosophy, physics, psychology, and anthropology to reveal why the present moment is so difficult to define. Rather than settling on a single answer, Marchant embraces the complexity, showing how these varied perspectives enrich rather than resolve the mystery. The talk concludes with a practical turn, connecting these abstract ideas to everyday life. Marchant suggests that understanding the elusive nature of 'now' can actually help us experience it more fully—by stepping away from our habitual relationship with clock time and paying closer attention to our immediate experience. It's a story worth your time because it takes something we all think we understand—this present moment—and reveals layers of wonder hiding in plain sight, ultimately offering both intellectual stimulation and a gentle invitation to be more present in our lives.

Starwatch: Catch the striking trifecta of Venus, the moon and Spica at break of dawn

Early risers around the world are in for a celestial treat this week, as three luminous objects converge in the pre-dawn sky. On the morning of November 7th, Venus, the waning crescent moon, and the star Spica will form a striking alignment visible to the naked eye, creating what astronomers call a trifecta in the eastern sky. Venus will be the star of the show, shining with its characteristic brilliance and serving as an easy landmark for spotting the grouping. Nearby, Spica—the brightest star in the constellation Virgo—will add its own light from 250 light-years away. The slender crescent moon, just two days from its new phase and only four percent illuminated, completes the trio. Patient observers may also glimpse earthshine, a subtle phenomenon where the moon's dark portion glows faintly from sunlight reflected off our own planet. The alignment will appear low on the horizon around 6:00 AM GMT, requiring a clear view to the east-south-east, and will be visible from both the northern and southern hemispheres, though viewers in the south will see it higher in the sky. This story offers a gentle reminder that some of nature's most striking moments require nothing more than waking up early and looking up. In a world of elaborate plans and complex technology, the simple choreography of planets, moons, and stars continues to offer wonder—free, accessible, and quietly spectacular for anyone willing to greet the dawn.

‘They have schedules to follow’: how fast ferries can threaten endangered whales in the Mediterranean

In the protected waters of the Pelagos sanctuary in the north-western Mediterranean, marine biologist Alice Barbarioli spots a telling mark on a sperm whale's tail—a clean cut likely left by a vessel collision. These strikes have long threatened the region's endangered whales, and new data reveals the scale of the problem. An investigation using maritime tracking information found that large passenger and cargo vessels from four major ferry companies routinely travel through this protected habitat at speeds of 15 knots or higher—velocities that almost always prove fatal to whales in the event of a collision. Slowing to below 10 knots dramatically reduces lethality, yet the voluntary speed recommendations introduced in 2023 appear largely ignored. The numbers tell a stark story. Ships spent more than 280,000 hours traveling at dangerous speeds through the particularly sensitive sea area in 2025, with some ferries exceeding 25 knots. Just seven vessels—four from Baleària and three from Grimaldi—accounted for 70% of the fastest travel times. Six ship captains, speaking candidly, explained that without binding regulations, commercial pressures make compliance nearly impossible. "They have schedules to follow," one Grimaldi captain noted, pointing to the tourism industry's tight timetables. The companies named in the investigation did not respond to requests for comment. This story matters because it illuminates a quiet collision between conservation ideals and commercial realities. The Mediterranean hosts two-thirds of the region's fin whales and more than a fifth of its sperm whales in these waters, yet voluntary protections seem insufficient against the momentum of global trade and tourism. It's a reminder that saving endangered species often requires more than good intentions—it demands accountability.

Tens of thousands build 108 Buddhist stupas for Bhutan ‘mindfulness’ city

In a remarkable display of collective effort, more than 40,000 volunteers in Bhutan worked through the night to construct 108 Buddhist stupas along a 12-kilometer stretch of riverbank near the Indian border. The 15-meter-tall sacred monuments, built as "a physical form of prayer made permanent in stone and earth," mark the site of Gelephu Mindfulness City, an ambitious project conceived by King Jigme Khesar Namgyel Wangchuck. Nearly 200,000 people—roughly a quarter of Bhutan's entire population—attended Sunday's ceremony, underscoring the national significance of the undertaking. The mindfulness city represents Bhutan's response to pressing economic challenges, including high unemployment, declining birth rates, and the emigration of skilled workers. Designed by Danish architects, the project aims to transform a small town of 10,000 into a vibrant hub for sustainable business, green technology, education, and tourism. The initiative reflects Bhutan's distinctive approach to development, balancing its famous "Gross National Happiness" philosophy with economic necessity. The project has attracted significant investment, including from Indian billionaire Gautam Adani, and will connect to India's rail network, with an international airport planned for 2029. Bhutan also intends to leverage its cryptocurrency holdings, earned through Bitcoin mining using surplus hydropower, to support the city's development. This story captures a nation at a crossroads, seeking to preserve its cultural identity while adapting to modern economic realities. The image of tens of thousands building sacred structures together through the night speaks to both Bhutan's spiritual traditions and its hopes for the future—a quiet yet profound moment of collective intention in a rapidly changing world.

What does it take to build a sexy basket man?

When a new film required turning actor Alexander Skarsgård into a romantic lead made entirely of wicker, the filmmakers behind *Wicker* knew exactly where to turn: New Zealand's renowned Wētā Workshop. The movie, a 17th-century tale starring Olivia Colman as a fisherwoman who solves her husband problem by crafting one from basketry, presented an unusual challenge—creating a believable, expressive, and yes, attractive man woven from natural materials. For Wētā's team, famous for creating aliens and monsters for science fiction epics, designing a romantic hero from wicker required entirely new methods. Over 18 months, prosthetic makeup designers, weavers, textile artists, and sculptors collaborated to build a costume that allowed Skarsgård's performance to shine through. The final design included nine individual facial prosthetic pieces—separate lips, cheekbones, a nose, and even tiny eyelashes made from harakeke (New Zealand flax). His body consisted of 50 woven plates stitched onto a delicate spandex mesh. The team refined their process until they could complete the actor's transformation in just 90 minutes each morning on the Budapest set, with almost no CGI involved. What makes this story quietly remarkable is how it showcases the intersection of traditional craft and cutting-edge film artistry. The project arrived just as Wētā's team of weavers and textile artists finished work on the *Avatar* films, allowing their specialized skills to flourish in an entirely unexpected context. The result is a film that's become a surprise hit, proving that sometimes the most memorable movie magic comes not from digital effects, but from the patient, creative work of human hands.

Phar Lap's little-known pre-cup trip to the country

In October 1930, just weeks before his legendary Melbourne Cup victory, champion racehorse Phar Lap spent several unhurried days in the border town of Albury, New South Wales—a little-known chapter in Australian racing history. The stopover wasn't planned as a training retreat but arose from a quirk of infrastructure: trains between Sydney and Melbourne had to change at Albury due to differing rail gauges between the states, a requirement that persisted until 1962. Phar Lap and his devoted strapper Tommy Woodcock stayed at Sodens Hotel, a racing-world hub that had hosted at least eight other Melbourne Cup winners. Woodcock slept in the loft above the stables, as was custom, keeping close watch over "Big Red." Remarkably, despite Phar Lap's fame, no one in Albury recognized the horse during their daily walks through town. Woodcock later recalled that the horse wore out his boots and two of his shirts from Phar Lap's habit of nudging his right shoulder. A telegram from the horse's connections extended their stay, ensuring Phar Lap missed the Caulfield Cup and allowing another horse, Amounis, to win at better odds—a strategic move that disappointed bettors hoping for a double. This story offers a tender glimpse into the bond between horse and handler, and the quieter rhythms of a bygone racing era. Woodcock later shared these memories with family friend Wayne Hinchliffe, whose family cared for him in his final years. It's a reminder that behind every legendary victory are humble moments and enduring friendships—worth remembering for their warmth and humanity.

Health Expeditioners return to Iauaretê after 22 years and begin project serving Indigenous people in Amazonas

A medical organization called the Health Expeditioners has returned to Iauaretê, a remote Indigenous community in Brazil's Amazon region, 22 years after their very first mission there. This time, they're launching an ambitious four-year project called "Cuidar para Preservar" (Care to Preserve), bringing specialized healthcare to communities along the Upper Rio Negro. What began in 2004 as a modest effort with four doctors and 100 kilograms of supplies has grown into a sophisticated operation mobilizing 60 professionals and 20 tons of medical equipment. The current expedition, which runs through early November, offers surgeries and care in ophthalmology, general surgery, women's health, and pediatrics. The team brings a complete mobile surgical center capable of performing cataract removals, hernia repairs, and cervical cancer screenings—procedures often unavailable in these isolated areas. Access alone presents significant challenges, requiring transport by river and overland routes to reach communities where healthcare infrastructure remains limited. The project, supported by Brazil's Amazon Fund, also includes telemedicine consultations, training for local health workers, and prosthetics for amputees through a program called Forest in Motion. Over two decades, the Health Expeditioners have completed 58 expeditions, performing more than 11,000 surgeries and 80,000 consultations across the Amazon. This story quietly illustrates how dedicated teams are bridging vast geographic and infrastructure gaps to deliver essential care. It's a reminder that healthcare equity often requires creativity, partnership with Indigenous organizations, and a willingness to bring the hospital to the patient—not the other way around.

Rare beetle that oozes substance from its knees when attacked rediscovered in Wales

After nearly six decades of absence, the short-necked oil beetle has been rediscovered thriving in Wales, offering a hopeful chapter in the story of a creature once thought nearly lost to Britain. This peculiar insect earns its name from its compact build and a remarkable defense mechanism: when threatened, it secretes an oily yellow-orange substance from its knee joints, a strategy as unusual as the beetle's entire life cycle. The species depends on an intricate ecological partnership to survive. Its larvae, equipped with hook-like feet, cling to solitary bees and hitch rides back to their nests, where they feast on pollen stores before emerging as adults with roughly a year to live. Only about one in 10,000 larvae reach adulthood, making any viable population precious. Habitat loss throughout the twentieth century pushed the beetle to the brink, leaving just a handful of suitable sites across the UK. While adults were spotted at Castlemartin range in Pembrokeshire in 2020 and 2022, only dead specimens were found, raising fears the population might not be sustainable. But the 2025 survey brought encouraging news: larvae distributed in patterns suggesting successful breeding from a larger group of young beetles. This rediscovery matters because it reflects the delicate interdependence of ecosystems—the beetle's survival hinges not only on thick vegetation and flower cover but on the health of solitary bee populations. Conservation efforts at the site now support multiple endangered species sharing similar habitat needs, from the shrill carder bee to rare wildflowers. It's a quiet reminder that patience and persistence in conservation can yield surprising returns, and that even the smallest, strangest creatures play irreplaceable roles in the living world around us.
