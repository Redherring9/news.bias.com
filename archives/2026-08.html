Should you take the plunge? How to tell how clean the sea is

As concerns about water quality make headlines across the UK, beachgoers are increasingly seeking reliable ways to assess whether it's safe to swim. The question of clean water has become more pressing, with sewage spills, agricultural runoff, and other pollutants potentially affecting coastal waters—though the picture is more nuanced than it might first appear. Several systems now exist to help swimmers make informed decisions. The Environment Agency rates designated bathing waters from "excellent" to "poor" based on bacteria samples collected over four years, testing for E. coli and intestinal enterococci that can cause illness. However, this official monitoring only runs from mid-May through September and doesn't reflect day-to-day conditions. Water companies provide their own alert systems, like South West Water's WaterFit Live, which notifies users of potential sewage impacts for specific periods after spills. Meanwhile, the campaign group Surfers Against Sewage has developed an app that aggregates data from multiple sources and maintains pollution alerts for 48 hours as a precautionary measure. The Blue Flag scheme offers another benchmark, assessing beaches against strict international criteria. This story matters because it highlights both progress and limitations in public information about water safety. While more tools exist than ever before, gaps remain—official testing is seasonal, alerts vary between systems, and a sewage spill doesn't automatically mean unsafe conditions. For anyone planning a coastal swim, understanding these different resources and their constraints offers a clearer path to making thoughtful choices about when and where to take the plunge.

Dry July for Western Australia as stunning pictures tell story

Western Australia experienced one of its driest Julys on record in 2026, despite the month typically being one of the wettest of the year. Perth received just 56.8 millimeters of rain—less than half its long-term average of 147 millimeters—while the broader South West region saw its lowest July rainfall since records began, averaging only 39.7 millimeters. The unusual dry spell was caused by stubborn high-pressure systems that settled over southern Australia, blocking the cold fronts that normally sweep in from the Southern Ocean to deliver winter rain. Each time a storm approached, another high-pressure system would arrive, steering the weather south and leaving much of Western Australia in calm, settled conditions. The clear skies brought frosty mornings to inland areas, with some locations dropping below zero multiple times. Mid-month brought an unseasonably warm day on July 15, when Jurien Bay hit 26.9°C—its highest July temperature on record—and Perth reached 25.7°C, its warmest July day in 32 years. ABC readers captured the unusual weather through their lenses: double rainbows between brief showers, eerie fog blanketing Perth, and crisp night skies perfect for astrophotography. This story offers a vivid portrait of climate variability, told through the eyes of residents who documented their changing skies. It's a reminder that weather patterns can surprise us, and that communities notice when the rhythms of the seasons shift in unexpected ways.

French hitchhikers put faith in fellow travellers in Top End journey

Two 21-year-old friends from France have traveled more than 5,000 kilometers across outback Australia using only their thumbs, cardboard signs, and considerable patience. William Pertek and Manek Miquel hitchhiked from Queensland to Broome in Western Australia, sometimes waiting up to ten hours for a ride, motivated by a desire to challenge themselves, improve their English, and embrace adventure without spending money on conventional transport. Their journey took them through memorable stops including Larrimah, a Northern Territory town famous for true-crime stories, and tiny Camooweal near the Queensland border. The young travelers found hitchhiking more difficult in Australia than in other countries, requiring creative strategies like writing destination names on cardboard rather than simply holding up a thumb. Yet they were repeatedly moved by Australian generosity: drivers offered food and drinks, one bought them a new tent for the desert crossing, and two women gave them money for sleeping bags. While acknowledging safety concerns, the pair said talking extensively with potential drivers before accepting rides was essential to feeling secure. They also relied on modern technology, with one sharing his phone location with his mother in France to ease her worries. This story offers a quiet reminder that old-fashioned travel methods are finding new life among younger adventurers, aided by social media tips and tracking apps that earlier generations lacked. It's a tale about trust, resilience, and the human connections that emerge when you're willing to wait by the roadside with nothing but a cardboard sign and faith in strangers—something worth considering in an age when we often choose efficiency over serendipity.

Nina Kennedy is often superhuman, but in Glasgow she was a super human

Nina Kennedy, Australia's pole vault star who has dominated major competitions since her breakthrough 2022 Commonwealth Games victory, experienced a rare stumble in Glasgow, claiming silver rather than her expected gold. The 29-year-old, who had won gold at the last three major championships she competed in, found herself battling difficult crosswinds at Scotstoun Stadium that made the competition tricky for all vaulters. Kennedy cleared just three of seven attempts, with her top height of 4.65 metres falling 30 centimetres short of her personal best. New Zealand's Eliza McCartney took gold on countback after both athletes cleared the same height. The challenging conditions kept the final from becoming the showcase many anticipated. Kennedy had recently broken an eight-year-old Oceania record just weeks before the Games, proving her fitness and form despite recovering from hamstring surgery. Yet on this night, the pieces simply didn't align. As she prepared for her third attempt at 4.70 metres, the usually steely competitor's demeanor had shifted, and she couldn't pull off the clutch performance that has become her trademark. What makes this story quietly remarkable is Kennedy's grace in defeat. Her reflection—"I'm superhuman most of the time, but I'm also human a lot of the time"—offers a grounding reminder that even elite athletes who seem invincible face off-nights. For viewers accustomed to watching Kennedy exclusively at the world's biggest stages where she'd built an unbroken gold streak, this silver medal reveals the fine margins in a sport requiring such extraordinary precision and the humanity behind athletic excellence.

Scientist's lifelong obsession with mystery of elusive 'pink zebra' seaweed

Off the coast of Cornwall, beneath waters most associate with the Great Barrier Reef's fame, lies a quieter marvel: maerl beds, ancient carpets of calcified seaweed that host some of the ocean's richest life. Forty years ago, marine scientist Christine Maggs discovered a tiny fragment of pink-striped seaweed living on maerl during her PhD research. It was unlike anything in the scientific literature, and she suspected it might be new to science. But proving that required a larger sample—something that would elude her for decades. Throughout her career, Maggs caught only fleeting glimpses of the elusive "pink zebra" seaweed, never enough to confirm its identity. Then, two years ago, specialist divers surveying the health of Cornish maerl beds brought up samples that independent marine biologist Francis Bunker thought might be what Maggs had been seeking. He sent the specimens to her in London, where she nurtured them in a wine fridge—the perfect cool, dark environment—until they grew large enough for DNA sequencing. The molecular analysis, conducted with colleague Beatriz de Barros Barreto, confirmed what Maggs had hoped: the seaweed was indeed a species new to science. A sample now resides in the Natural History Museum's official collection, and the findings will soon be published in the journal Phycologia. This story reminds us that discovery doesn't always require distant expeditions or high-tech laboratories. Sometimes it asks for patience, a magnifying glass, and four decades of quiet determination. In an age of rapid information, Maggs' lifelong pursuit of one small seaweed offers a different rhythm—one where curiosity, persistence, and wonder reveal that even well-studied waters still hold secrets waiting to be named.
