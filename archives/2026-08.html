Deadly heat pushes Japan’s farmers to go nocturnal

As Japan's summers grow dangerously hot, farmers across the country are fundamentally reshaping their work schedules, shifting to nocturnal and early-morning hours to survive increasingly extreme temperatures. Motoaki Iijima, a 36-year-old flower farmer north of Tokyo, now harvests gerberas under halogen lights long after sunset, a change he made three years ago after a worker suffered heatstroke in his greenhouse. When outside temperatures exceed 35 degrees Celsius, greenhouse interiors can reach over 40 degrees—turning midday work into a potentially fatal activity. The human cost is mounting. In 2024, a record 59 agricultural workers in Japan died from extreme heat, more than double the 2021 toll. Nearly 60% of younger farmers surveyed reported either experiencing heatstroke themselves or knowing someone who had. The challenge is particularly acute for Japan's aging agricultural workforce—roughly 70% of farmers are over 65, making them especially vulnerable to heat stress amplified by the country's high humidity. Heat exposure cost the agricultural sector 926 million lost labor hours in 2024 alone, while economy-wide losses reached approximately $46 billion. Poultry farmer Tetsuya Usuba, still haunted by losing over 500 hens to heat six years ago, now wakes his 4,400 birds at 2:30 a.m. so they can eat before temperatures climb. This story quietly documents a profound transformation in one of humanity's oldest rhythms—the agricultural day. It shows farmers not simply adjusting to climate change but rewriting centuries of practice to protect lives and livelihoods, a shift happening from Vietnam to Spain that speaks to both human adaptability and the escalating toll of a warming planet.

Tapirs, bats and nests: How plastic infiltrates Amazonian wildlife

Deep in the Amazon rainforest, far from cities and human settlements, scientists are discovering an unwelcome visitor: microplastics. Recent studies have found these tiny plastic particles in fish swimming in the protected waters of Anavilhanas National Park, eight hours by boat from the nearest city. About eight out of every ten fish tested contained microplastics in their digestive systems, despite living in what appears to be pristine wilderness. The contamination includes nylon filaments from synthetic clothing and fishing nets, alongside fragments from bottles and packaging. The plastic's journey through the Amazon follows the region's natural water systems. It begins in small forest streams called igarapés, which receive untreated sewage and garbage from cities like Manaus and Belém. From there, microplastic concentrations in these streams measure hundreds of times higher than in the main Amazon River. Seasonal floods then spread the contamination, connecting riverbanks, forests, and lakes in a vast network that carries plastic particles even to the most remote locations. The pollution affects creatures at every level, from the aquatic insects that break down leaves at the base of the food chain to larger animals like tapirs, crabs, and bats. Birds at the river's mouth have even begun building nests from blue plastic debris. This story matters because it reveals how thoroughly human-made pollution has penetrated one of Earth's most biodiverse and seemingly untouched ecosystems. The Amazon's intricate web of waterways, which sustains countless species, has become a distribution network for contamination, showing that no place remains truly isolated from our plastic footprint.
