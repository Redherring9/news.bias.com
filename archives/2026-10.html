Going for gold: World panning championship takes over Cromwell

More than 150 gold panners from over 20 countries have gathered in Cromwell, New Zealand, for the 49th annual World Gold Panning Championship. The event transforms a centuries-old goldfields skill into a high-speed competitive sport, with participants racing to extract tiny gold flakes from buckets of gravel as quickly and accurately as possible. Organizers spent months preparing for the tournament, including sourcing 20 tonnes of gravel and constructing water troughs that meet strict World Gold Panning Association standards. The championship has attracted legendary figures in the sport, including Ken Carlson, who designed the speed pan and has won 39 championship medals throughout his career. New Zealand's competitors are holding their own against this elite international field, qualifying for multiple semi-finals and finals. Beyond the main competition, the week-long event has featured an opening parade, movie nights, markets, and novelty contests like panning with a pāua shell, with festivities set to conclude with a hoedown and medal ceremony. What makes this gathering special isn't just the competitive panning—it's the warmth of the global community it brings together. Competitors are camping at the racecourse, swapping tips, and reconnecting with friends from previous championships. As one Canadian five-time attendee put it, the event offers a chance to see the world while building friendships across borders. It's a reminder that even the most niche pursuits can create meaningful human connections, turning an old prospecting technique into something that glitters with camaraderie as much as gold.

How Assefa ran third fastest marathon ever despite 'destroyed' Achilles

Ethiopian marathon star Tigst Assefa was on pace to reclaim the world record at the Berlin Marathon when disaster struck with just two kilometers remaining. Flying through the course well ahead of record pace, the 29-year-old Olympic silver medalist suddenly began to grimace and limp near the Brandenburg Gate. What happened next defies conventional sports medicine: Assefa continued running on what would later be diagnosed as a complete rupture of her right Achilles tendon. The Achilles connects calf muscles to the heel and acts as a spring during running, making a rupture typically catastrophic. Physiotherapists note that such injuries usually prevent basic functions like walking. Yet Assefa pushed through, completing the race in 2:11:04—the third-fastest women's marathon time in history and a new course record. Her team revealed she'd felt pain from the 31-kilometer mark but kept going, eventually running the final 500 meters with what her representative described as a "completely destroyed" tendon. The carbon-plate super shoes she wore may have provided some mechanical assistance, acting as a partial substitute for the damaged tendon's spring function. This story captures something quietly extraordinary about human determination and the mysteries of the body under extreme duress. Assefa collapsed after crossing the finish line and was immediately flown to Munich for complex surgery. Her performance raises fascinating questions about pain, biomechanics, and what becomes possible when elite athletes operate at the absolute edge of physical capability—even when the body itself begins to fail.

Multiple organ donation performed at Macaé Public Hospital transforms grief into hope

In the coastal city of Macaé, Brazil, a tragic traffic accident became the catalyst for an extraordinary act of generosity. Lucas Nunes Hardie, a 40-year-old public health worker, became a multiple organ and tissue donor following the crash, providing a heart, liver, kidneys, pancreas, corneas, and bones to patients waiting for transplants. His sister Lidiane honored wishes Lucas had expressed during his lifetime, aided by the compassionate support of the hospital's specialized donation team. What makes this story particularly remarkable is the context in which it unfolded. Macaé has achieved a family consent rate for organ donation of 55.5%—significantly higher than the state average of 33%. Health officials attribute this success to the humanized care provided by the Hospital Donation Team, which supports families through grief while respecting donors' wishes. The heart, one of the most complex organs to match, was transported by helicopter directly from the municipal hospital on the same day, underscoring the coordinated effort behind such procedures. This story matters because it illuminates how communities can transform cultural taboos around death and donation through education and compassionate care. Macaé is now hosting a free public congress on organ donation awareness, encouraging residents to have these vital conversations with their families. In a moment of profound loss, Lucas's decision—and his sister's courage to honor it—created ripples of hope for multiple families, turning personal tragedy into a quiet testament to human connection and the possibility of life continuing through generosity.

SpaceX flight led by first Black female commander sets US speed record

Four astronauts arrived at the International Space Station on Thursday after completing the fastest journey from Earth yet achieved by any U.S. spacecraft—a remarkable eight-hour express trip made possible by the station's fortuitous orbital position at launch time. The SpaceX mission, which lifted off from Cape Canaveral after weather concerns and a three-week delay to repair a fuel leak, marks another milestone in commercial spaceflight and sets a new American speed record. The crew is led by Jessica Watkins, a geologist who previously worked on NASA's Mars Curiosity rover and is returning to the ISS for her second mission. She becomes the first Black woman to command a crew to orbit. Joining her are three spaceflight newcomers: NASA's Luke Delaney, a retired Marine; Canada's Joshua Kutryk, a Royal Canadian Air Force colonel; and Russia's Sergey Teteryatnikov, a former submariner. In a poignant human touch, Kutryk's wife is expecting their third child in late November, making him the fourth man to become a father while in orbit—a reminder that six-month missions unfold alongside the earthbound rhythms of family life. This story offers a quiet celebration of progress on multiple fronts: technological achievement in rapid space transit, the diversification of space exploration leadership, and the enduring international cooperation that keeps the ISS functioning. The crew's embrace of the number 13—carried in their mission patch, vehicle license plates, and even a crocheted lucky dragon—adds a touch of human superstition to an otherwise precision-engineered endeavor, reminding us that even at the edge of possibility, people bring their hopes and charms along for the ride.

Mangroves keep waves at bay. A new study helps scientists predict how

Mangrove forests have long been valued for their ability to protect coastal communities from storm surges and rising seas, but scientists have struggled to predict exactly how much wave energy these tangled-root ecosystems can absorb during severe weather. Measuring mangrove performance during typhoons and hurricanes is dangerous and complex, leaving coastal planners without reliable data for the moments that matter most. Now, researchers from China's M5 lab have developed a breakthrough method that uses calm-weather measurements to predict how mangroves will perform during extreme storms. The team tested their approach during Typhoon Yagi, which struck Southeast Asia and southern China in September 2024, generating record-breaking waves nearly three feet high at the edge of a restored mangrove forest along China's Zhanjiang coast. The mangroves reduced those waves to just over one foot after traveling the length of a football field through the forest. The researchers' new equation, dubbed the HU method, successfully predicted this attenuation using only data gathered during peaceful conditions. They've also released a free online tool allowing coastal engineers anywhere in the world to estimate how local mangrove forests might protect their communities. With more than 87 million people affected by climate-related flooding and storms in 2025 alone, this research offers vulnerable coastal communities a practical way to plan nature-based defenses before disaster strikes. The work bridges ecological science and coastal engineering, turning mangrove restoration from a general climate strategy into something communities can measure, predict, and rely on when the next big storm approaches.

Hitting the sweet spot: bear treats itself to baked goods at Colorado farmers’ market

This story offers a window into the intersection of wildlife and human spaces, especially as environmental pressures push animals closer to populated areas. Dozens of men failed to stop an alleged gang rape at Cornell – here’s the misperception that keeps bystanders from intervening and how to short-circuit it

When news emerged of an alleged gang rape by fraternity members at Cornell University, one detail stood out: over 50 men reportedly learned of the assault through a group chat as it unfolded, yet none apparently intervened or alerted authorities. A social psychologist examining this troubling silence points to a pervasive misperception that helps explain bystander inaction in cases of sexual violence. Research reveals a surprising disconnect: while only about 6.5% of male college students in the U.S. and Canada have committed rape, many men who personally oppose sexual violence mistakenly believe their peers are more accepting of it than they actually are. Studies show college men consistently overestimate how much their friends and classmates endorse rape myths and aggressive behavior. This flawed assumption becomes a powerful deterrent to intervention—men's beliefs about their peers' attitudes predict their willingness to act even more strongly than their own stated views. The fear of being ridiculed or appearing weak to other men emerges as the most common reason for failing to step in. Meanwhile, the small percentage of men who do commit assaults tend to overestimate support for their actions and openly describe their behavior, inadvertently reinforcing the false impression that such conduct is widespread and socially accepted. This story matters because it reveals that the problem isn't necessarily changing social norms around sexual violence—it's helping people see that the norm is already better than they think. Correcting these misperceptions through targeted education programs shows real promise in reducing assault and empowering bystanders to act, suggesting that silence often stems not from approval, but from a fundamental misreading of the room.

Araguaia River Dolphins Rescued After Becoming Trapped in Water Pools of Dried River in Tocantins

In western Brazil's Tocantins state, six Araguaia river dolphins found themselves in a precarious situation when dropping water levels left them stranded in isolated pools along the Urubu River. The predicament unfolded near a dam in Lagoa da Confusão, where seasonal drought conditions between May and October had dramatically reduced the river's flow due to solar radiation and heat. Rescue teams from the World Wildlife Fund Brazil and partner organizations worked between August 21 and 24 to assess and relocate the most vulnerable dolphins. Two were found in critical condition—one showing signs of starvation and dehydration from sun exposure—and required careful medical treatment, examination, and transfer to safer waters. Four others were discovered trapped in a separate pool 500 meters away, while a sixth dolphin was spotted alone using drones and kayaks to survey the area. The coordinated effort brought together environmental agencies, local government, civil defense teams, and even a nearby ranch that helped acquire fish to feed the stranded animals. What makes this story quietly remarkable is both the fragility it reveals and the response it inspired. Fortunately, rainfall in the following days raised water levels enough that the four remaining dolphins didn't need relocation, though monitoring continues. It's a reminder of how seasonal patterns increasingly test wildlife resilience, and how community collaboration—from government workers to farmers—can make the difference between survival and loss for species already navigating a changing landscape.

Dolphins keep their social and hunting lives in separate waters, study finds

Dolphins, much like people who prefer to keep work and social life distinct, have been found to separate their hunting grounds from their socializing spots in the ocean. Researchers studying bottlenose dolphins along Florida's Atlantic coast discovered this intriguing behavioral pattern by listening to the sounds the animals make in different locations. Using a solar-powered acoustic wave glider equipped with underwater microphones and environmental sensors, scientists recorded over 62 hours of dolphin vocalizations over two months. They identified two distinct sound patterns: whistles, which dolphins use to communicate with each other, and rapid clicks, which function as biological sonar to locate prey. The whistling sounds were concentrated in nearshore waters where dolphins gather socially, influenced by factors like water temperature and location. The clicking sounds, meanwhile, dominated in areas rich with fish. An unexpected finding emerged from the data as well—fish appear to fall silent when they detect dolphins nearby, suggesting prey species may be eavesdropping on their predators' acoustic signals. This research offers more than a glimpse into dolphin social structure; it provides a practical tool for conservation. By mapping where dolphins feed versus where they socialize, marine managers can better target protections, such as reducing noise pollution in critical habitats. As coastal waters face mounting pressures from heat waves and human development, understanding how dolphins adapt their behavior could serve as an early warning system for broader environmental changes. The study reminds us that animals aren't just inhabitants of their ecosystems—they're sentinels, revealing shifts we might otherwise miss.

From ‘community-based’ to ‘community-led conservation’ in Kenya: Interview with Dickson Kaelo

Dickson Kaelo's path to becoming a leading voice in Kenyan conservation began with lived experience. Growing up Maasai, herding livestock alongside wildlife, he witnessed the high cost of coexistence—lions killing cattle, elephants destroying crops, hyenas raiding animals families depended on for survival. Nearly three decades ago, fresh from college, he sought a middle ground between human-wildlife conflict and exclusionary conservation models that pushed communities out of protected areas. His first attempt in 1998, organizing a community trust near the Maasai Mara, showed promise. It built infrastructure, funded scholarships, and experimented with tourism while keeping livestock on the land. Then it collapsed when the land was subdivided. Kaelo nearly walked away entirely, but returned with a new approach: privately owned land managed collectively. Between 2004 and 2012, working mostly unpaid, he helped establish eight community conserved areas covering 870 square kilometers. That grassroots work eventually led him to head the Kenya Wildlife Conservancies Association in 2013. Today, Kenya has roughly 240 conservancies covering about 15% of the country. Yet Kaelo speaks openly about what hasn't worked. Elite capture, poor governance, unfavorable deals with private investors, unresolved land disputes, and donor dependency remain persistent challenges. Some conservancies have stalled; others have vanished. This story matters because it resists the tidy narrative of conservation success, offering instead an honest account from someone who understands both the promise and the friction of trying to make people, livestock, and wildlife share the same ground.

Google removes over 40 channels with fake AI doctors after BBC News Brasil reports

YouTube has removed over 40 channels featuring fake doctors created with artificial intelligence, following investigative reports by BBC News Brasil and a formal notification from Brazil's government. The channels used AI-generated personas—and in some cases, cloned images of real physicians—to spread unverified health advice, targeting primarily elderly viewers with promises of natural cures and unproven treatments. The scale of the operation was striking: BBC's investigation in July found 29 Portuguese-language channels had accumulated more than 70 million views, posting roughly ten new videos daily. Analysis of thousands of comments revealed elderly users who believed they were watching genuine medical professionals and had begun following the advice—some replacing prescribed medications with home remedies. One 82-year-old woman told reporters she had no idea she was watching artificial intelligence, finding the figure's advice "plausible." At least five channels cloned the face and identity of a real otolaryngologist without permission, prompting him to file a police report for identity theft and defamation. This story offers a sobering look at how AI tools can be weaponized at industrial scale to exploit vulnerable populations. It also shows that accountability is possible: government intervention, investigative journalism, and platform enforcement can work together to curb digital deception. The incident raises important questions about how societies will protect trust and safety as synthetic media becomes harder to distinguish from reality—particularly for those least equipped to spot the difference.

Patterns on butterfly wings can deceive predators with motion illusions

Butterflies may have evolved a remarkable defense mechanism that plays tricks on the eyes of hunting birds. When a predator swoops toward a butterfly in flight, contrasting patterns on the insect's wings can create optical illusions that make it appear to be moving in a different direction than it actually is—buying the butterfly a crucial split-second to escape. This elegant hypothesis has intrigued scientists for years, but evidence from studies of striped animals like zebras and snakes has been mixed, often because researchers didn't account for the movement of wings or limbs. A team led by Jolyon Troscianko at the University of Exeter used high-speed footage of real butterflies, computer modeling, and touch-screen experiments with 100 human volunteers to investigate. They found that the flexible, flapping motion of butterfly wings—which close, open, and twist in a figure-eight pattern—works in concert with bold stripes to create what Troscianko calls a "motion dazzle" effect. During the downstroke, a stripe appears to move slowly downward, but during the upstroke it quickly folds and twists upward, making downward motion far more visible even when the butterfly is climbing. The result is a kind of barber-pole illusion that confuses motion-detection circuits in a predator's brain. Not all butterflies rely on this strategy; brown or red species may depend more on camouflage or warning colors that signal toxicity. This study offers a quietly elegant insight into the coevolution of predator and prey. It reminds us that survival in nature often hinges not on strength or speed, but on deception rendered in the flutter of a wing—a living optical illusion refined over millions of years.

Endangered dolphins survive in a Bangladesh river so polluted it’s ‘not livable’ for fish

In one of the world's most polluted urban rivers, a small population of endangered Ganges river dolphins has found a way to survive against remarkable odds. A year-long study documented at least 20 of these rare mammals living in Bangladesh's Buriganga River, a waterway so contaminated that researchers describe it as unlivable for most fish species. The discovery surprised scientists who conducted surveys along a 27-kilometer stretch between September 2023 and August 2024, observing dolphins in ten of the twelve months and even spotting calves among the groups. The Buriganga River, which flows along the southern edge of Dhaka, absorbs a toxic mixture of untreated industrial waste from tanneries, textile plants, and battery factories, along with municipal sewage. Heavy metals like lead, chromium, and cadmium routinely exceed safety limits, and dissolved oxygen levels plummet near zero during dry seasons. Native fish populations have largely vanished from these hostile waters. Yet the dolphins appear to have adapted by feeding on pollution-tolerant invasive suckermouth catfish from South America, which have thrived where other species cannot. Nearly 16 percent of dolphin sightings included calves, suggesting the population is actively reproducing despite the degraded environment. This story offers a glimpse into nature's surprising resilience while underscoring an urgent conservation challenge. Researchers emphasize that the dolphins' survival in the Buriganga shouldn't be mistaken for stability—their presence is precarious, dependent on an invasive species in a dying ecosystem. The findings remind us that wildlife can adapt in unexpected ways, but true conservation requires addressing root causes, not just celebrating survival against the odds.

PTSD death highlights 'appreciable gap' in support for police

A coronial inquest into the 2022 death of South Australian police officer Benjamin Warren has revealed significant gaps in mental health support for officers exposed to trauma. The 35-year-old senior constable and father of two was found at the SA Police Academy after his ex-wife raised safety concerns. Though he left a note denying any diagnosed mental health conditions, State Coroner Naomi Kereru found Warren was highly likely suffering from PTSD linked to years of exposure to critical incidents during his service, particularly a 2014 double-fatal crash in Whyalla involving a deliberate collision. The inquest heard that Warren had concealed his struggles, a pattern the coroner said should serve as a lesson for how police approach officers involved in traumatic incidents. Just before his death, Warren had posted on Facebook about the 2014 crash, suggesting it continued to haunt him years later. While the coroner concluded his death could not have been prevented, she emphasized this doesn't mean nothing should change going forward. The findings included four key recommendations to the police minister: increasing education about confidential counseling services, developing an electronic system to track officers' cumulative exposure to traumatic events, and implementing mandatory psychological reviews for those reaching certain trauma thresholds. The coroner noted that while Warren never raised concerns about individual incidents, a comprehensive view of his exposure might have prompted earlier intervention. This story matters because it illuminates the hidden toll of service work and points toward systemic changes that could better protect those who spend their careers responding to community crises.

Newly regulated physician associates able to register from today

New Zealand has begun formally registering physician associates, a mid-level medical role designed to support doctors and expand healthcare capacity, particularly in underserved rural areas. Starting today, the Medical Council can register these professionals under new regulations introduced earlier this year, marking a significant shift in how the country approaches healthcare delivery. Physician associates work under doctor supervision to help with patient assessment, diagnosis, treatment, and ongoing care—similar to nurse practitioners in the medical hierarchy. While common in countries like the United States, United Kingdom, and Canada, the role is new to New Zealand's regulated healthcare system. About 60 PAs are already working across the country, all trained internationally due to the previous absence of local programs. Regulation now opens the door for New Zealand-based training and establishes professional standards and public accountability. Supporters, including the New Zealand Physician Associate Society, believe formalizing the role will improve patient access to timely care. The change hasn't come without concern. Some health workers questioned whether introducing PAs represents good value, while the New Zealand Resident Doctors' Association has raised patient safety issues based on international experience. They point to reports from the UK citing confusion over the role leading to patient harm and preventable deaths, with recommendations that PAs not diagnose patients who haven't first seen a doctor. The association emphasizes that patients have the right to know who is treating them and to request a doctor. This story matters because it reflects a global conversation about how healthcare systems balance workforce shortages with maintaining safety and clarity in patient care.

Floating scarecrows: how Cornish fishers are stopping seabirds drowning in their nets

Off the coast of Cornwall, a creative collaboration between fishers, scientists, and conservationists is showing promising results in protecting endangered seabirds from accidental drowning. Each year, an estimated 95,000 diving birds—including razorbills and guillemots—become fatally entangled in fishing nets across European waters. Now, simple devices that mimic predatory threats are proving remarkably effective at steering these birds away from danger. The two-year trial tested a "looming-eyes buoy"—a floating scarecrow with a spinning head and large, intimidating eyes—alongside a commercially available bird-shaped kite. Ten Cornish fishers equipped their boats with cameras to document how seabirds responded to these deterrents near gillnets, the vertical walls of netting that hang beneath the water's surface. The results, published in Conservation Letters by University of Exeter researchers, revealed that the kites reduced seabird deaths by 71%, while the buoys achieved a 59% reduction. The study also identified winter as the season of highest risk in Cornish waters, offering valuable guidance for when these protections matter most. This research represents more than a decade of effort to find solutions that protect wildlife without burdening fishers. The devices are practical, affordable, and don't interfere with fishing operations—a rare win-win in conservation. For seabird populations already under pressure, these unassuming tools could make a meaningful difference, transforming an accidental tragedy into an opportunity for coexistence between human livelihood and the natural world.
