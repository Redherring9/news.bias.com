Hunter-gatherer societies are often put on a pedestal as paragons of equality – but the reality is more complicated

Hunter-gatherer societies like Tanzania's Hadza people are often celebrated as models of equality, where resources are shared generously and hierarchies seem absent. When a Hadza hunter brings down an animal, the entire camp participates in dividing the meat, with families from miles around receiving portions. This vivid image has fueled a popular narrative that early human societies were naturally egalitarian, free from the inequalities that plague modern life. But anthropologists who work directly with these communities are finding a more nuanced reality. A comprehensive review of over a century of ethnographic research reveals that no society, not even those living traditional hunter-gatherer lifestyles, completely lacks inequality. Among the Ju/'Hoansi of the Kalahari, certain families controlled valuable hunting territories around waterholes. The Bayaka of central Africa traded and hoarded ritual knowledge despite having little material wealth. Gender inequalities appear nearly universal, with women performing more childcare and men dominating community decision-making. Even the Hadza reserve certain cuts of meat exclusively for older men and accord greater respect to successful hunters. This research doesn't diminish the genuine equalities these societies do maintain—modest possessions, constrained leadership, consensus decision-making—but it challenges romanticized portrayals that paint them as utopian paradises. The truth, researchers suggest, lies somewhere between the "Garden of Eden narrative" and claims that hierarchy is inevitable. Understanding these complexities matters as modern societies grapple with growing inequality, offering realistic rather than mythologized insights into human nature and social organization.

From jellyfish to 'gossamer worms': expedition finds 31 new species in the South Atlantic

Scientists aboard the research vessel Falkor (too) have discovered 31 species new to science during a 15-day expedition in international waters off the coast of Brazil. The mission, called Designing the Future 3, took place in April 2026 and was led by zoologist Karen Osborn from the Smithsonian's National Museum of Natural History. The expedition focused on the midwater zone—the vast, largely unexplored region of ocean between the sunlit surface and the seafloor. Among the discoveries are nine jellyfish, seven siphonophores, seven ctenophores, four larvaceans, two giant rhizarians, one amphipod, and a striking transparent worm from the genus Tomopteris, known as a gossamer worm. These creatures represent some of the ocean's most unusual life forms: siphonophores are colonial organisms where each apparent individual is actually a colony of specialized units working together; ctenophores propel themselves with rows of cilia that create shimmering light effects; and giant rhizarians, despite being visible to the naked eye, are single-celled organisms. The gossamer worm proved particularly fascinating, moving through the water with unexpected speed that challenges scientists' understanding of how body shape relates to swimming ability. This expedition reminds us that Earth's largest habitat remains largely mysterious. The midwater occupies roughly one billion cubic kilometers and plays a crucial role in processes like the global carbon cycle, yet only a tiny fraction has been explored. These discoveries offer a glimpse into an alien world that exists not in distant space, but in the depths of our own oceans—a reminder that wonder and scientific frontier can be found much closer to home than we might imagine.

Inside the 'secret society' keeping the art of bellringing alive

In the bell tower of All Saints Cathedral in Bathurst, New South Wales, a small group gathers at dusk to practice an art form that has remained largely unchanged since 17th-century England. They're bellringers, participants in what one practitioner calls "such a secret society, but such a lovely community." Their craft, known as "change ringing," involves pulling numbered ropes attached to brass bells mounted on wooden wheels, following complex musical compositions that resemble lines of code more than traditional sheet music. The pursuit demands extraordinary dedication. A "peal"—the pinnacle achievement in bellringing—requires hours of continuous ringing without error, testing both mental focus and physical endurance. Nearby in Orange, accomplished ringer Jim Woolford trains the next generation using simulators, concerned that towers worldwide are falling silent for lack of teachers. This story offers a window into a living tradition that thrives quietly in church towers across the world. One bolt, ¥400,000: Why Japan’s Oshima ‘tsumugi’ silk costs a pretty penny

In the world of Japanese textiles, Oshima tsumugi stands apart as a marvel of patience and precision. A single bolt of this silk fabric can cost ¥400,000 (roughly $2,700), and once you understand the process behind it, the price begins to make sense. What might appear to be printed patterns are actually created through an intricate technique where threads are dyed before weaving, then carefully aligned on the loom so that designs emerge from the fabric's very structure. As one textile specialist observes, the process is almost mathematical in its exactitude—weavers can't simply work continuously but must constantly adjust to ensure perfect alignment. Originating from Amami Oshima, a remote island between Okinawa and southern Kyushu, this textile tradition represents one of Japan's most elaborate and rare crafts. Creating a standard five-square-meter bolt involves approximately 60 distinct processes, each performed by highly skilled specialists. The timeline reflects this complexity: a single tanmono, the standard bolt used for kimono fabric, can require up to a year to complete. The tradition embodies a kind of slow craft that stands in stark contrast to modern production methods, with knowledge passed between generations of artisans who have dedicated their lives to mastering specific steps in the lengthy process. This story offers a window into a textile tradition where artistry, mathematics, and extraordinary patience converge. In an age of fast fashion and instant gratification, Oshima tsumugi reminds us that some beautiful things simply cannot be rushed—and that true craftsmanship sometimes demands we measure time not in hours or days, but in seasons and years.

Probiotics prevented coral bleaching during a Red Sea heat wave: Study

Scientists have achieved a promising breakthrough in coral conservation by successfully protecting reef corals from bleaching during a marine heat wave using probiotics. In a field study conducted off the coast of Saudi Arabia in 2022, researchers treated corals in their underwater "Coral Probiotic Village" with beneficial bacteria isolated from healthy corals. When temperatures rose in the Red Sea, these treated corals resisted bleaching while others succumbed to the stress. The research, led by microbial ecologist Raquel Peixoto at King Abdullah University of Science and Technology, marks a significant step forward because it demonstrates effectiveness in the wild rather than just in laboratory tanks. The team tested two probiotic treatments, and notably found that even heat-killed versions of the bacteria—called postbiotics—offered protection. This discovery could make the approach more practical and affordable for wider use, as postbiotics are cheaper and easier to store than live bacteria. The probiotics work by altering the coral's microbiome, boosting beneficial bacteria while reducing harmful pathogens, without affecting the surrounding seawater ecosystem. With coral reefs facing an existential crisis—scientists predict 70-90% could face severe degradation by 2100 if temperatures rise 1.5°C above pre-industrial levels—this research offers a glimmer of hope. While questions remain about long-term survival rates and implementation challenges, the study represents an important tool in a broader conservation strategy. As one independent researcher noted, demonstrating that probiotics can mitigate heat stress on actual reefs, not just in controlled settings, is truly remarkable. It's a quiet reminder that sometimes the smallest organisms hold answers to our biggest environmental challenges.

Patterns on butterfly wings can deceive predators with motion illusions

Butterflies may have evolved a remarkable defense mechanism that plays tricks on the eyes of hunting birds. When a predator swoops toward a butterfly in flight, contrasting patterns on the insect's wings can create optical illusions that make it appear to be moving in a different direction than it actually is—buying the butterfly a crucial split-second to escape. This elegant hypothesis has intrigued scientists for years, but evidence from studies of striped animals like zebras and snakes has been mixed, often because researchers didn't account for the movement of wings or limbs. A team led by Jolyon Troscianko at the University of Exeter used high-speed footage of real butterflies, computer modeling, and touch-screen experiments with 100 human volunteers to investigate. They found that the flexible, flapping motion of butterfly wings—which close, open, and twist in a figure-eight pattern—works in concert with bold stripes to create what Troscianko calls a "motion dazzle" effect. During the downstroke, a stripe appears to move slowly downward, but during the upstroke it quickly folds and twists upward, making downward motion far more visible even when the butterfly is climbing. The result is a kind of barber-pole illusion that confuses motion-detection circuits in a predator's brain. Not all butterflies rely on this strategy; brown or red species may depend more on camouflage or warning colors that signal toxicity. This study offers a quietly elegant insight into the coevolution of predator and prey. It reminds us that survival in nature often hinges not on strength or speed, but on deception rendered in the flutter of a wing—a living optical illusion refined over millions of years.
