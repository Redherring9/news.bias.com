Auckland Council using discounts to separate trash and treasure at transfer station

Hiroo Kanamori, the Japanese scientist who created the scale that replaced Richter's (and why it's better for measuring large earthquakes)

When Hiroo Kanamori was a child in wartime Japan, he saw snow-covered mountains in Nagano for the first time and wondered how such natural grandeur came to be. Growing up during World War II without school supplies or formal classes, he learned to entertain himself by building small motors from salvaged materials—a practice that taught him to think independently. That curiosity about the natural world eventually led him to geophysics and to one of seismology's most important innovations: the moment magnitude scale, which now measures earthquakes worldwide. Kanamori developed his scale in 1977, later expanding it in collaboration with American seismologist Thomas Hanks. It replaced the famous Richter scale, particularly for larger earthquakes, though Richter's method remains in use for smaller tremors. The challenge scientists faced for decades was distilling the complexity of an earthquake into a single, quickly calculable number. Before Richter's 1935 breakthrough, researchers relied on "intensity" measurements—how strongly a quake was felt in different locations—which generated many numbers for a single event. Richter focused on the maximum amplitude of seismic waves recorded by identical instruments in Southern California, creating the first unified magnitude system. Now 90 and professor emeritus at Caltech, Kanamori has spent over half a century advancing our understanding of earthquakes. His story reminds us that scientific breakthroughs often begin with childhood wonder and the habit of asking questions. The tools he developed help communities around the world understand and prepare for seismic events, turning a boy's curiosity about mountains into knowledge that saves lives.

This technique has remained unchanged since the 1600s, when these shimmering shoes were made exclusively for Nawabs, the Muslim rulers of Awadh, symbolizing wealth and status. Over the past century, silver nagras evolved from royal footwear into a beloved wedding tradition across Lucknow's social classes. Brides, in particular, adopted them as an essential part of wedding attire. But over the past year, orders have disappeared. Silver prices, already climbing due to tight supplies and investor demand, surged further following the U.S.-Iran conflict in February as global uncertainty drove investors toward precious metals. Prices that fluctuated around $830 per kilogram in 2023 have now soared beyond reach for many Lucknow shoppers. Painted wind turbines can dramatically reduce bird deaths, study finds

A surprisingly simple solution may help address one of renewable energy's unintended consequences. Researchers in Spain have found that painting a single blade of wind turbines black can reduce bird fatalities by nearly 74%, offering a low-cost intervention for a problem that affects up to a million birds annually in the United States alone. The issue stems from what scientists call "motion smear"—when turbine blades spin, their white surfaces blur into near-invisibility for birds in flight. By painting just one of the three blades a contrasting color, the turbines become more discernible, giving birds a better chance to navigate safely around them. The Spanish study, conducted at El Ruedo wind farm between 2012 and 2024, reinforces findings from Norway and South Africa, where similar painting interventions reduced bird deaths by 70% and 83% respectively. A Dutch study, however, found no significant effect, suggesting the approach may work differently depending on local conditions or bird populations. The solution appears especially important for vulnerable species like raptors and migrating songbirds, which have low reproductive rates and long lifespans, making additional mortality particularly damaging to their populations. While wind turbine collisions represent a fraction of bird deaths compared to cats or building strikes, the findings arrive at a critical moment as the world expands renewable energy infrastructure. The challenge now is scaling up: painting blades after installation is expensive, and manufacturers have hesitated to paint them during production, citing concerns about heat absorption and weight balance. Still, this story offers a quiet reminder that sometimes the most effective solutions are also the simplest, and that addressing climate change needn't come at the expense of the natural world it aims to protect.

When a predator swoops toward a butterfly in flight, contrasting patterns on the insect's wings can create optical illusions that make it appear to be moving in a different direction than it actually is—buying the butterfly a crucial split-second to escape. This elegant hypothesis has intrigued scientists for years, but evidence from studies of striped animals like zebras and snakes has been mixed, often because researchers didn't account for the movement of wings or limbs. A team led by Jolyon Troscianko at the University of Exeter used high-speed footage of real butterflies, computer modeling, and touch-screen experiments with 100 human volunteers to investigate. They found that the flexible, flapping motion of butterfly wings—which close, open, and twist in a figure-eight pattern—works in concert with bold stripes to create what Troscianko calls a "motion dazzle" effect. During the downstroke, a stripe appears to move slowly downward, but during the upstroke it quickly folds and twists upward, making downward motion far more visible even when the butterfly is climbing. The result is a kind of barber-pole illusion that confuses motion-detection circuits in a predator's brain. Not all butterflies rely on this strategy; brown or red species may depend more on camouflage or warning colors that signal toxicity. This study offers a quietly elegant insight into the coevolution of predator and prey. It reminds us that survival in nature often hinges not on strength or speed, but on deception rendered in the flutter of a wing—a living optical illusion refined over millions of years.

Endangered dolphins survive in a Bangladesh river so polluted it’s ‘not livable’ for fish

In one of the world's most polluted urban rivers, a small population of endangered Ganges river dolphins has found a way to survive against remarkable odds. A year-long study documented at least 20 of these rare mammals living in Bangladesh's Buriganga River, a waterway so contaminated that researchers describe it as unlivable for most fish species. The discovery surprised scientists who conducted surveys along a 27-kilometer stretch between September 2023 and August 2024, observing dolphins in ten of the twelve months and even spotting calves among the groups. The Buriganga River, which flows along the southern edge of Dhaka, absorbs a toxic mixture of untreated industrial waste from tanneries, textile plants, and battery factories, along with municipal sewage. Heavy metals like lead, chromium, and cadmium routinely exceed safety limits, and dissolved oxygen levels plummet near zero during dry seasons. Native fish populations have largely vanished from these hostile waters. Yet the dolphins appear to have adapted by feeding on pollution-tolerant invasive suckermouth catfish from South America, which have thrived where other species cannot. Nearly 16 percent of dolphin sightings included calves, suggesting the population is actively reproducing despite the degraded environment. This story offers a glimpse into nature's surprising resilience while underscoring an urgent conservation challenge. Researchers emphasize that the dolphins' survival in the Buriganga shouldn't be mistaken for stability—their presence is precarious, dependent on an invasive species in a dying ecosystem. The findings remind us that wildlife can adapt in unexpected ways, but true conservation requires addressing root causes, not just celebrating survival against the odds.

The 35-year-old senior constable and father of two was found at the SA Police Academy after his ex-wife raised safety concerns. Though he left a note denying any diagnosed mental health conditions, State Coroner Naomi Kereru found Warren was highly likely suffering from PTSD linked to years of exposure to critical incidents during his service, particularly a 2014 double-fatal crash in Whyalla involving a deliberate collision. The inquest heard that Warren had concealed his struggles, a pattern the coroner said should serve as a lesson for how police approach officers involved in traumatic incidents. Just before his death, Warren had posted on Facebook about the 2014 crash, suggesting it continued to haunt him years later. While the coroner concluded his death could not have been prevented, she emphasized this doesn't mean nothing should change going forward. The findings included four key recommendations to the police minister: increasing education about confidential counseling services, developing an electronic system to track officers' cumulative exposure to traumatic events, and implementing mandatory psychological reviews for those reaching certain trauma thresholds. The coroner noted that while Warren never raised concerns about individual incidents, a comprehensive view of his exposure might have prompted earlier intervention. This story matters because it illuminates the hidden toll of service work and points toward systemic changes that could better protect those who spend their careers responding to community crises.

Newly regulated physician associates able to register from today

New Zealand has begun formally registering physician associates, a mid-level medical role designed to support doctors and expand healthcare capacity, particularly in underserved rural areas. Starting today, the Medical Council can register these professionals under new regulations introduced earlier this year, marking a significant shift in how the country approaches healthcare delivery. Physician associates work under doctor supervision to help with patient assessment, diagnosis, treatment, and ongoing care—similar to nurse practitioners in the medical hierarchy. While common in countries like the United States, United Kingdom, and Canada, the role is new to New Zealand's regulated healthcare system. About 60 PAs are already working across the country, all trained internationally due to the previous absence of local programs. Regulation now opens the door for New Zealand-based training and establishes professional standards and public accountability. Supporters, including the New Zealand Physician Associate Society, believe formalizing the role will improve patient access to timely care. The change hasn't come without concern. Some health workers questioned whether introducing PAs represents good value, while the New Zealand Resident Doctors' Association has raised patient safety issues based on international experience. They point to reports from the UK citing confusion over the role leading to patient harm and preventable deaths, with recommendations that PAs not diagnose patients who haven't first seen a doctor. The association emphasizes that patients have the right to know who is treating them and to request a doctor. This story matters because it reflects a global conversation about how healthcare systems balance workforce shortages with maintaining safety and clarity in patient care.

Floating scarecrows: how Cornish fishers are stopping seabirds drowning in their nets

Off the coast of Cornwall, a creative collaboration between fishers, scientists, and conservationists is showing promising results in protecting endangered seabirds from accidental drowning. Each year, an estimated 95,000 diving birds—including razorbills and guillemots—become fatally entangled in fishing nets across European waters. Now, simple devices that mimic predatory threats are proving remarkably effective at steering these birds away from danger. The two-year trial tested a "looming-eyes buoy"—a floating scarecrow with a spinning head and large, intimidating eyes—alongside a commercially available bird-shaped kite. Ten Cornish fishers equipped their boats with cameras to document how seabirds responded to these deterrents near gillnets, the vertical walls of netting that hang beneath the water's surface. The results, published in Conservation Letters by University of Exeter researchers, revealed that the kites reduced seabird deaths by 71%, while the buoys achieved a 59% reduction. The study also identified winter as the season of highest risk in Cornish waters, offering valuable guidance for when these protections matter most. This research represents more than a decade of effort to find solutions that protect wildlife without burdening fishers. The devices are practical, affordable, and don't interfere with fishing operations—a rare win-win in conservation. For seabird populations already under pressure, these unassuming tools could make a meaningful difference, transforming an accidental tragedy into an opportunity for coexistence between human livelihood and the natural world.
